# Saved by Faith and Hospitality: Salvation as Divine Welcome (based on Joshua Jipp's framework)

## Overview

Most gospel presentations frame salvation primarily in legal or
transactional terms — guilt, verdict, payment. Joshua Jipp's work
offers a complementary lens: salvation described throughout Luke-Acts
and Paul's letters as being welcomed as a stranger into God's house.
This is not a replacement for penal or covenantal categories, but an
additional angle that resonates strongly in honor-shame and collectivist
cultures, where the deepest fear is often exclusion rather than guilt.

## Food, Stigma, and Identity in Luke-Acts

Luke's Gospel is unusually preoccupied with meals, and unusually
attentive to who is stigmatized at them. Jesus is criticized precisely
for eating with "sinners" — a category that, in the social world of the
text, meant something like the permanently disreputable: people whose
occupation, ethnicity, or moral history had placed them outside
acceptable society. By persistently accepting invitations from, and
extending invitations to, these stigmatized people, Jesus enacts a
central Lukan theme: "the year of the Lord's favor" (Luke 4:18-19) is a
year of welcome for people society has shut out. The resurrected Jesus
continues this pattern in Luke 24 and Acts, appearing to disciples in
the context of shared meals (the road to Emmaus) and instructing the
early church to continue table fellowship as a defining practice.

## Jesus as Divine Host, and as Guest

A distinctive move in this framework is treating Jesus as functioning
in both directions simultaneously: he acts as the divine host who
welcomes unworthy guests to his table (the parable of the great banquet
in Luke 14, where the invited guests refuse and the outcasts are
brought in instead), and as the vulnerable guest who depends on the
hospitality of others (relying on the households of Martha and Mary,
Zacchaeus, and the disciples at Emmaus). This double movement matters
for missiology: it means the missionary's posture should mirror both —
sometimes extending hospitality as Christ's representative, and
sometimes humbly receiving hospitality from those being reached, rather
than assuming the "helper" role always belongs to the outsider bringing
the gospel in.

## The Church's Embrace of Stigma

Because the early church continued to eat across social lines that the
wider culture considered scandalous, one of its most visible marks of
identity became its willingness to absorb the stigma attached to its
members — eating with the diseased, the ethnically despised, the
formerly enslaved. This is framed as more than tolerance: it is the
church's participation in the same hospitality God has already shown
it. The logic runs: because we ourselves were once strangers welcomed
by grace, our own hospitality toward others who carry stigma is not
optional generosity but an outworking of what we've received.

## Ecclesial Hospitality Across Difference (Paul)

The Corinthian correspondence extends this theme into the challenge of
maintaining table fellowship across real social and theological
difference — wealthy and poor believers, Jew and Gentile, those with
different consciences about food. Paul's instructions repeatedly push
toward maintaining the shared table even amid disagreement, rather than
splitting into homogeneous groups, treating unity-across-difference at
the table as a visible sign of the gospel's power to reconcile.

## Application to cross-cultural ministry

Framing salvation as "being welcomed as a stranger" tends to land
differently — and often more immediately — in cultures oriented around
belonging, family, and face than a purely legal framing does. For
missionaries working among people who fear exclusion more acutely than
they fear condemnation, this vocabulary offers a biblically faithful
and culturally resonant way to present the gospel without diluting its
content.

## Discussion prompts for training

- How might presenting salvation as "welcome" rather than only
  "verdict" change how you approach someone from a shame-based culture?
- In what ways does your ministry currently ask new believers to
  absorb social stigma the way the early church did — and are you
  modeling that yourself, or only asking it of others?
- Where do you currently need to practice being a humble guest,
  receiving hospitality, rather than only being the one who extends it?
