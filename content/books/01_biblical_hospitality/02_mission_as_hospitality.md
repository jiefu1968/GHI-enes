# Mission as Hospitality: Biblical Foundations (based on Edward Smither's framework)

## Overview

A common weakness in mission training is treating hospitality as a
cultural nicety rather than a theological category. Edward Smither's
work traces hospitality across the whole biblical storyline, arguing
that God himself is fundamentally a hospitable God, and that mission —
properly understood — is an extension of that hospitality outward
toward strangers, not just a program of persuasion.

## Hospitality in the Old Testament

The Old Testament repeatedly links hospitality to covenant. Abraham's
welcome of the three visitors at Mamre (Genesis 18) is not a random act
of kindness — it is the setting in which God announces covenant
promise. Meals in the ancient Near East sealed agreements between
parties; to share bread was to declare peace and obligation toward one
another. Israel's own hospitality laws (gleaning rights for the poor
and the foreigner, cities of refuge, protection of the sojourner) flow
directly from Israel's self-understanding as a people who were once
strangers in Egypt and were shown hospitality by God himself. The book
of Ruth is a sustained case study: a Moabite widow is drawn into
Israel's covenant community precisely through the hospitality of Boaz,
prefiguring how outsiders are incorporated into God's people. The
prophetic image of a coming "messianic banquet" (echoed in Isaiah)
casts Israel's hospitality practices as anticipations of a larger,
future welcome God intends for all nations.

## Hospitality in the New Testament

Greco-Roman culture had its own hospitality codes (xenia) — guests and
hosts owed each other specific obligations, and hosting a stranger well
was a mark of honor. The New Testament both draws on and radically
reworks this background. Jesus is repeatedly both host and guest,
often in socially inappropriate combinations — accepting hospitality
from tax collectors, offering hospitality to those who could never
repay him. The early church's rapid, cross-cultural growth in Acts
depended heavily on households opening their doors: Lydia's home,
Cornelius's home, the "breaking of bread" from house to house in
Acts 2. Paul's letters repeatedly instruct churches to "pursue
hospitality" (Romans 12:13) and list it as a qualification for elders
(1 Timothy 3, Titus 1) — not an optional virtue for especially generous
people, but a structural expectation for how the church functions
cross-culturally.

## Hospitality as Missional Method

Because hospitality was already a recognized moral category in both
Jewish and Greco-Roman worlds, the early church's practice of radical,
boundary-crossing hospitality (welcoming people across class, ethnicity,
and status lines at the same table) functioned as an unusually
persuasive apologetic. Outsiders could see, physically, that this new
community treated people differently than the surrounding culture did.
For contemporary cross-cultural workers, the implication is that
hospitality is not merely a bridge-building tactic prior to "real"
ministry — it is itself a enactment of the gospel's claim that God
welcomes strangers, and it needs to be practiced with the same
theological seriousness as preaching or teaching.

## Application to cross-cultural and diaspora ministry

Many honor-shame societies (much of the Chinese-speaking world included)
already carry rich hospitality expectations around receiving guests,
reciprocal gift-giving, and face. Rather than importing a Western
model of hospitality, missionaries are better served learning to
recognize and redeem the hospitality instincts already present in a
host culture — using them as a vehicle for gospel presence rather than
treating them as an obstacle to be worked around.

## Discussion prompts for training

- How does Abraham's hospitality at Mamre reshape how we think about
  "ordinary" welcome as a place where God shows up?
- What existing hospitality customs in your target culture could
  become natural entry points for gospel relationship, rather than
  needing to be replaced by imported methods?
- Why might qualifying for church leadership (1 Timothy 3) include
  hospitality specifically, rather than leaving it as a personal
  preference?
