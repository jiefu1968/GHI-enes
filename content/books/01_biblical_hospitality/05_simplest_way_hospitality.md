# The Simplest Way to Change the World: Hospitality for Ordinary People (based on Willis & Clements' framework)

## Overview

This framework is aimed at overcoming the most common obstacle to
hospitality: the sense that one's home, cooking, schedule, or social
skills aren't good enough to bother inviting anyone in. Its central
argument is that small, unremarkable acts of welcome, repeated
consistently, carry more evangelistic and discipleship potential than
occasional impressive events — a "weapon for the gospel" available to
completely ordinary people, not just naturally outgoing hosts or those
with beautiful homes.

## Ordinary Does Not Equal Insignificant

A key reframe for training purposes: much mission strategy
unintentionally trains workers to think in terms of scale — the bigger
event, the more polished program, the more people reached at once.
This framework pushes the opposite direction, arguing that small,
repeatable, low-cost acts of welcome (a regular meal, an open door, a
standing invitation) compound over time into deeper transformation than
sporadic large efforts, because they build the kind of sustained trust
relationships require.

## The Cultural Currents Working Against Hospitality

Two forces are named as the main obstacles to hospitality in modern
life: isolation (increasing physical and social separation from
neighbors, enabled by technology and mobility) and relaxation (treating
home as a private retreat from other people rather than a space to be
shared). Naming these as "currents" — ongoing cultural pressures rather
than personal failures — is a deliberately non-shaming way to help
missionaries recognize what they're working against, so they can make
deliberate, counter-cultural choices rather than assuming hospitality
will happen automatically.

## What Biblical Hospitality Actually Requires

The practical bar for hospitality is set intentionally low: it does not
require a large home, elaborate cooking, or a naturally extroverted
personality. What it requires is willingness to be interrupted,
willingness to let people see an imperfect life, and consistency over
time. This lowers the barrier to entry significantly for missionary
trainees who may otherwise assume hospitality is a specialized gift
some have and others lack.

## Application to cross-cultural and diaspora ministry

For missionaries working in urban, transient, or diaspora contexts
(international students, migrant workers, urban professionals) where
isolation is often especially acute, low-barrier, repeatable hospitality
practices are particularly well suited: a standing weekly meal, an
open-door policy on a specific evening, or a simple habit of walking
the neighborhood are more sustainable and more replicable by new
believers than elaborate one-time events — which matters directly for
multiplication and indigenous leadership, since new believers can
imitate a simple practice far more easily than an elaborate one.

## Discussion prompts for training

- Which of the two named "currents" — isolation or relaxation — do you
  personally feel pulled toward, and what would counteracting it look
  like this week?
- What is the simplest, most repeatable act of hospitality you could
  realistically sustain for a year, rather than a single impressive
  event you could do once?
- How would you teach a brand-new believer in your context to practice
  hospitality in a way they could immediately replicate with their own
  network?
