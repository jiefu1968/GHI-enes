# A Biblical Approach to Chinese Traditions: Evaluating Festivals, Ancestor Veneration, and Custom (based on Daniel Tong's framework)

## Overview

This framework addresses a genuine pastoral dilemma many Chinese
Christians face: how to maintain healthy appreciation for a rich
cultural heritage while discerning which specific traditions,
practices, and beliefs are compatible with Christian faith and which
are not. Rather than offering blanket rejection of Chinese culture (a
posture the author explicitly resists as unnecessarily impoverishing)
or uncritical acceptance (which risks real syncretism), the book works
through specific traditions one at a time, first describing the
practice and its underlying religious or philosophical basis, then
evaluating it in light of Scripture.

## A Centuries-Old Debate

The tension between honoring Chinese heritage and maintaining biblical
fidelity is not new — it echoes the historic "Chinese Rites
Controversy" among early Catholic missionaries and later Protestant
missionary debates (including figures like James Hudson Taylor) over
whether Confucian ancestral rites and ceremonial practices were
compatible with Christian conversion. Historical attempted solutions
ranged from the Roman Catholic concept of "accommodation" (permitting
significant adaptation of local ritual forms) to a more rigid
Protestant tendency toward wholesale rejection of anything associated
with traditional practice — both approaches carrying real theological
and pastoral risks, whether syncretism on one side or unnecessary,
culturally alienating rigidity on the other.

## Principles for Evaluation

Rather than issuing a simple verdict for each tradition, the framework
proposes several evaluative lenses to apply to any specific custom:
examining its origins (whether a practice traces to documented
historical fact or unverifiable myth), its context and connotation in
present practice (the same outward action can carry different meaning
depending on setting and intent), whether its symbolism has drifted
into functional superstition (treating an object or gesture as having
inherent magical power rather than merely symbolic or memorial
significance), and whether the practice's emphasis falls on
proclamation (implicitly affirming a non-Christian religious claim)
versus mere social practice (participating in a widely shared cultural
form without affirming its underlying religious content). Applying
these lenses consistently allows Chinese Christians to reach
principled, case-by-case conclusions rather than either a blanket
prohibition or blanket permission applied uniformly across very
different practices.

## The Three Teachings and Popular Religion

The framework surveys the philosophical and doctrinal basis of
Confucianism (including the five cardinal relationships and debates
over whether Confucius functions as a deity and Confucianism as a
religion), Buddhism (including the Theravada/Mahayana distinction), and
Daoism, alongside "Shenism" — the broader, less formally organized
popular Chinese religious practice (deity worship, folk ritual,
divination) that operates alongside and often blends with the three
formal traditions in everyday practice. Understanding each
tradition's actual doctrinal content, rather than treating "Chinese
religion" as one undifferentiated mass, is treated as a prerequisite
for evaluating any specific practice derived from it.

## Festivals, Ancestor Veneration, and Practical Application

Major Chinese festivals (including Chinese New Year practices such as
Lo Hei) are examined individually for their historical origins,
religious content, and modern social function, distinguishing
elements that are purely cultural/social from elements carrying
explicit religious affirmation incompatible with Christian conviction.
Ancestor veneration receives extended treatment, addressing the
practical realities of funerals and the ancestral altar specifically —
recognizing that Chinese Christians must navigate genuine family
expectation and grief around these practices with both conviction and
sensitivity, distinguishing filial honor and memory (which Christian
faith can affirm) from religious veneration treating ancestors as
ongoing objects of worship or as spiritually efficacious mediators
(which raises genuine theological concern). Traditional Chinese
medicine and practices like ghost marriages receive similarly careful,
practice-by-practice evaluation, distinguishing what may reflect
legitimate natural remedies or general revelation from what carries
specific occultic or superstitious assumptions incompatible with
Christian faith.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

This framework's practice of evaluating specific traditions
individually, rather than issuing a single blanket ruling on "Chinese
culture" as a whole, offers missionaries and Chinese believers a
transferable method applicable well beyond the specific examples
covered: examine a practice's origin, its present context and
connotation, whether its symbolism has drifted into functional
superstition, and whether its emphasis falls on religious proclamation
or mere social practice. This method avoids both the pastoral harm of
unnecessary cultural rejection and the theological harm of uncritical
syncretism.

## Discussion prompts for training

- Apply the four evaluative lenses (origins, context/connotation,
  symbolism vs. superstition, proclamation vs. practice) to a specific
  festival or custom common in your own context — what conclusion do
  you reach?
- How would you pastorally help a new Chinese believer navigate family
  expectations around funeral rites and the ancestral altar without
  either dishonoring their family or compromising their faith?
- Where do you see the historical tension between Catholic
  "accommodation" and Protestant rejection playing out in
  contemporary debates about contextualization in your own ministry
  context?
