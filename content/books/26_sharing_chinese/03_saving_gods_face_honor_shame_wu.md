# Saving God's Face: A Chinese Honor-Shame Soteriology (based on Jackson Wu's framework)

## Overview

This academically rigorous framework argues that Chinese honor-shame
categories, taken seriously as an interpretive lens rather than merely
a communication strategy, actually illuminate biblical soteriology
(the doctrine of salvation) more comprehensively than the traditionally
dominant Western legal/guilt framework alone. The central
methodological claim is significant: this is not merely translating an
already-settled Western theology into Chinese-friendly illustrations,
but doing biblical exegesis itself through a Chinese honor-shame lens,
arguing that doing so surfaces genuine dimensions of the biblical text
that a purely Western legal framework tends to overlook or minimize.

## Theological Contextualization in Practice

A foundational methodological critique targets a common but flawed
pattern: theologizing that unconsciously assumes a Western framework
already represents "true" or "supracultural" theology, then merely
translates or illustrates that already-fixed theology using local
cultural terms — a logical fallacy the framework calls "assuming the
gospel." Because all theology, including Western theology, is
inevitably shaped by its own cultural context, genuine contextualization
requires using a target culture's own worldview categories as a lens
for interpreting Scripture directly, allowing that lens to potentially
surface aspects of the biblical text a different cultural lens might
have overlooked, rather than simply repackaging conclusions already
reached through a different cultural lens.

## Honor and Shame in Chinese Culture and in Scripture

"Face" (面子), central to Chinese social life, functions as a
sophisticated, comprehensive social currency governing reputation,
relationship, and social standing, operating by its own internal logic
distinct from (though sometimes overlapping) Western notions of
individual pride or reputation. Careful examination of Scripture
reveals honor and shame are not merely occasional biblical themes but
pervasive categories running through the entire biblical narrative,
from Genesis (Adam and Eve's shame after the Fall) through the
Prophets and directly into Paul's letters, suggesting honor-shame is
not primarily a "cultural translation tool" for reaching Chinese
audiences but a genuine, major biblical category that Western
theology, shaped by its own guilt-innocence cultural assumptions, has
significantly underemphasized.

## A Soteriology of Honor and Shame

The framework's central constructive proposal reinterprets justification
— traditionally understood in Western Protestant theology primarily as
a legal declaration of "not guilty" — as fundamentally concerned with
honor and shame: God's righteousness is his own honor and covenant
faithfulness, and justification is God's act of restoring honor to
those who trust in Christ, removing their shame before God and
granting them a right, honorable standing in his household rather than
merely canceling a legal debt. This is argued carefully through close
exegesis of key Pauline texts (particularly Romans), showing that
"righteousness" language in its original context carries strong
honor-shame connotations that a purely forensic/legal reading can
underemphasize, without denying that legal, forensic categories are
also genuinely present in Scripture. The proposal explicitly resists
simply replacing a "Western" guilt framework with a "Chinese" shame
framework as though they were mutually exclusive alternatives;
instead, both are argued to be genuinely, simultaneously present within
Scripture's own presentation of salvation, with the honor-shame
dimension simply having received less attention in Western academic
theology.

## Group Identity, Faith, and Works

Because Chinese culture (like the biblical world) operates with strong
collective, group-oriented identity rather than Western individualism,
faith and works are examined not merely as individual acts but as
markers of group identity and belonging — being "in Christ" reframed
partly as gaining honorable membership in a new family/community
whose identity marker is faith in Christ, complementing rather than
replacing the traditional understanding of individual faith and
personal justification.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

This framework offers missionaries working in Chinese contexts a
theologically rigorous foundation for presenting the gospel through
honor-shame categories with full confidence that this is not a
watered-down cultural accommodation but a legitimate, exegetically
grounded reading of what Scripture itself teaches about salvation.
Presenting salvation as God restoring honor and removing shame,
alongside forgiving legal guilt, offers Chinese listeners — for whom
face, honor, and shame are often more immediately felt categories than
abstract legal guilt — a gospel presentation that connects with their
deepest cultural intuitions without diluting biblical content.

## Discussion prompts for training

- How does reframing justification as "honor restored" alongside "guilt
  forgiven" change how you would present the gospel to someone deeply
  shaped by face and honor-shame dynamics?
- Why does the author insist this project is contextualized exegesis,
  not merely cultural translation of an already-fixed Western theology
  — and why does that methodological distinction matter?
- Where in Scripture (beyond Romans) do you see honor-shame themes that
  a purely guilt-innocence reading might overlook or underemphasize?
