# The Chinese Way: Contextualizing the Gospel for Chinese Culture (based on Hann-Tzuu Joey Tan's framework)

## Overview

This framework addresses the historic challenge of evangelizing
Chinese people, tracing why the gospel has often struggled to gain
sustained traction despite significant missionary effort across
centuries, and proposes specific contextualization strategies built
around two conceptual pillars identified as central to Chinese
culture: harmony and filial piety.

## The Challenge of Evangelizing the Chinese

The historical record of Protestant and Catholic missions to China
shows significant effort and periods of real growth, alongside
recurring cultural barriers: Christianity has often been perceived, at
least historically, as a "foreign" religion associated with Western
imperial powers rather than an authentically Chinese option, a
perception with a long, complicated history extending from early
missionary encounters through the colonial and Communist eras.
Overcoming this perception of foreignness, rather than any single
doctrinal obstacle, is treated as the central strategic challenge for
sustained Chinese evangelization.

## The Relationship Between Gospel and Culture

Following well-established missiological principle, the gospel's core
content is distinguished from any particular cultural form it has
historically been packaged in — meaning effective Chinese
evangelization requires distinguishing which aspects of historic
Western Christian practice are essential biblical content and which
are merely inherited Western cultural packaging that can, and often
should, be exchanged for authentically Chinese cultural forms without
compromising the gospel's substance. Contextualization, properly
practiced, is not a compromise of the gospel but a necessary
condition for the gospel to be heard as good news rather than as a
demand for cultural conversion to a foreign way of life.

## Three Teachings Shaping Traditional Chinese Culture

Confucianism, Daoism, and Buddhism have historically operated not as
mutually exclusive, competing systems (as separate world religions
often do) but as three complementary influences most Chinese people
have drawn on simultaneously throughout history — described as "the
unity of the three teachings." Confucianism supplies much of Chinese
culture's ethical and social framework, Daoism contributes cosmological
and aesthetic sensibility, and Buddhism contributes soteriological and
devotional practice, together forming an integrated cultural
inheritance rather than three separate religious choices — a point
directly relevant to how a Chinese person's default religious
disposition tends toward integrative "both/and" thinking rather than
the exclusive "either/or" religious commitment Christian conversion
typically requires.

## Two Essential Conceptual Elements: Harmony and Filial Piety

Two concepts are identified as especially central across all three
teachings and Chinese culture generally. Harmony describes the deeply
held value of maintaining balanced, peaceful relationships — within
the family, community, and cosmos — often prioritized above individual
assertion of personal rights or open confrontation, even at real
personal cost. Filial piety describes the deep obligation of respect,
care, and deference owed to parents and ancestors, extending beyond a
person's lifetime into ongoing ancestor veneration, and functioning as
arguably the single most foundational value shaping Chinese family
structure and social obligation. Both concepts run deep enough that
they function less like discrete beliefs a person holds and more like
the operating assumptions through which a Chinese person interprets
nearly every social and religious question, including how they will
initially evaluate the gospel's claims and demands.

## Suggestions for Contextualizing the Gospel

Building directly on these two concepts, harmony is proposed as a
natural cultural contact point for the gospel: the gospel's message of
reconciliation — between God and humanity, and among human beings —
can be presented as the fulfillment of humanity's deepest longing for
genuine harmony, rather than as a foreign disruption of existing
social harmony. Filial piety, similarly, offers rich potential for
gospel bridge-building: honoring God as the ultimate Father to whom
all filial devotion is ultimately owed can be presented as fulfilling
rather than replacing filial devotion to human parents, addressing
directly the common and painful perception that becoming Christian
requires abandoning one's family obligations. Rather than treating
these deep cultural values as obstacles to be overcome, this framework
treats them as pre-existing categories the gospel can be shown to
fulfill and complete.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

Missionaries working among Chinese people, whether in mainland China,
Taiwan, or the global Chinese diaspora, benefit directly from
presenting the gospel through harmony and filial piety as bridge
concepts rather than treating Chinese cultural values as competitors to
Christian conviction requiring wholesale rejection. This reframes
common evangelistic objections (that becoming Christian means
abandoning one's family, or disrupting social harmony) as
misunderstandings to be corrected through better contextualized
presentation, rather than as accurate objections requiring the convert
to genuinely choose between their family and their faith.

## Discussion prompts for training

- How would you present the gospel's message of reconciliation as
  fulfilling, rather than disrupting, a Chinese listener's existing
  value of social harmony?
- How would you help a Chinese inquirer see honoring God as the
  ultimate Father as fulfilling, rather than replacing, their filial
  obligations to their own parents?
- Where do you see the "unity of the three teachings" (integrative,
  both/and religious disposition) creating a specific challenge for
  presenting the gospel's exclusive claims, and how would you navigate
  that directly?
