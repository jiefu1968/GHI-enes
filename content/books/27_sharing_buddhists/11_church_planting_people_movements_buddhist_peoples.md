# Church Planting, People Movements, and Cultural Fit Among Buddhist Peoples

## Overview

This file completes the synthesis of *Sharing Jesus Effectively in the
Buddhist World*, covering the "meekness" approach to Thai witness,
the question of whether Christianity and Buddhism share a comparable
"temple ethos," structural lessons from a missionary's own
disappointing church-planting experience, the history and statistics
of the Karen people movement in Burma/Thailand, and incarnational
house-church strategy in Japan.

## Meekness as a Culturally Appropriate Approach to the Thai

Nantachai Mejudhon opens with a sobering historical statistic:
despite Catholic missionaries reaching Thailand as early as 1511 and
planting only six churches across three centuries of work, and
despite continuous Protestant mission since 1828 (the first Thai
convert appearing only in 1859, nineteen years after the American
Presbyterian mission arrived), professing Christians numbered a mere
0.6 percent of the Thai population by 1980 — after 165 years of
sustained evangelistic effort. Mejudhon's study investigates a
specific Thai cultural-religious behavior pattern, "meekness"
(associated with kindness and gentleness), and argues that missionary
approaches ignoring or undervaluing this pattern have been
substantially responsible for the strikingly low historical
conversion rate — despite Thai society's notable general
receptiveness to missionaries themselves, who have never faced
persecution and have often received government support for their
institutional work.

## Do Christianity and Buddhism Share a "Temple Ethos"?

Steve Spaulding frames a question posed to him directly by a
prominent Indochinese church leader: "Where are your temples?" — that
is, where is the physical edifice reflecting the church's spiritual
rootedness, social standing, membership, professional class,
territory, financial strength, and public worship, comparable to the
role Buddhist temples visibly play in the same society? Spaulding, the
son of missionaries raised in the shadow of actual Buddhist temples
and village ceremonies (including elaborate death and cremation
rituals), and simultaneously the grandson of the founder of a major
North American Bible school, brings a doubled personal perspective to
this genuinely open missiological question: whether the church's
own emphasis on gathered public worship in a defined sacred space can
or should be developed to meet the same social and religious
functions a temple provides in Buddhist societies, without simply
imitating Buddhist temple architecture or ritual for its own sake.

## Structural Lessons from a Disappointing Church-Planting Effort

One contributor's first-person account illustrates a structural
critique increasingly common in the missiological literature on
Buddhist contexts: two and a half years of intensive, well-resourced
effort by two missionary couples — tract distribution, prayer walks,
the Jesus film, Christmas events, open-air crusades and revival
meetings, English classes, home visits, small groups, Bible studies,
and a booth at the annual local fair — produced a group of only about
thirty-five people, still far short of self-sufficiently supporting
its own pastor. Reflecting after eleven years in vocational ministry,
the author records a growing conviction that "there has to be a
better way to do this" than continuing to intensify the same
activity-heavy, program-driven model, and that attributing meager
results simply to cultural "resistance" — a common comfort among
missionaries facing similar results — was not, on reflection, a
sufficient explanation. This account functions in the anthology as a
motivating case for the more structural, indigenous-movement-oriented
approaches (house churches, people movements) developed in later
chapters.

## The Karen People Movement: A Century of Data

Alex G. Smith's chapter-length case study traces the Karen people
movement in Burma and Thailand using Donald McGavran's "people
movement" framework — the historical observation, following Kenneth
Scott Latourette's monumental history of Christian expansion, that
sustained church growth across history has typically come not through
individual conversions in isolation but through movements of whole
peoples, with entire kinship and social networks turning toward the
faith together. Smith presents net Protestant Karen membership growth
across more than a century: from 70 members in 1881 to 240 the
following year, reaching several hundred by the early 1900s, roughly
800-1,400 by the mid-twentieth century, and climbing to over 7,000 by
1980, though the trajectory was neither smooth nor guaranteed.

Smith identifies a substantial list of specific factors that
prevented this promising movement from growing considerably larger,
especially in its first half-century: inconsistent long-term
commitment from both missionaries and Karen workers themselves;
inadequate nurture, grounding, and follow-up teaching of new
believers; insufficient preparation of converts to resist the pull of
resilient traditional spirit practices; neglected pastoral care of
both new and older established churches; failure to adequately train
indigenous Karen leadership to sustain the churches themselves; the
inherent mobility of Karen communities practicing swidden (slash-and-
burn) cultivation; external political control and oversight
originating from Burma rather than autonomous local direction;
broader cultural and political pressures from the dominant national
society; a generally weak strategy that failed to focus efficiently
on existing social networks and family-based evangelism; and, perhaps
most tellingly, the outright "orphaning" of struggling people
movements by the sending missions themselves once initial momentum
slowed. This granular list of specific historical failure points
functions as a practical checklist for contemporary workers seeking
to sustain, rather than merely initiate, movements among Buddhist
peoples.

## Incarnational House-Church Strategy in Japan

Mitsuo Fukuda describes founding what became the RAC Network (first
the Research Association for Contextualization, renamed in 2002 the
Rethinking Authentic Christianity Network) to catalyze house-church
multiplication in Japan, motivated by discouragement at how little
fruit years of publishing, lecturing, and conference work had
produced relative to the vision of a genuinely contextualized
Japanese Christianity. Fukuda's central strategic conviction is that
churches structured around direct, personal access to God, with flat
rather than hierarchical relationship structures, better reflect how
Japanese people actually live relationally than do more institutional
or clergy-centered models — a conviction that shifted his network's
work from research and publication alone toward direct field coaching
of house-church planters. He notes with some irony that when he was
baptized roughly twenty-five years before writing, longtime believers
told him Christians made up less than one percent of the Japanese
population — a figure that, by his own account, has not meaningfully
improved since, underscoring the scale of the contextualization
challenge Japan's own strongly syncretistic Buddhist-Shinto religious
culture (documented in the companion file on the spirit world of East
Asia, drawing on Yuzo Imamura's testimony) continues to pose.

## Missiological Reflection

Read together, these case studies suggest that the single largest
predictor of durable Buddhist-context church growth is not the
intensity or creativity of initial evangelistic activity, but
sustained attention to indigenous leadership development, culturally
appropriate communication style (meekness rather than confrontation,
in the Thai case), realistic structural expectations for gathered
worship and community life (the temple-ethos question), and
above all the refusal to abandon promising movements once they
require patient, unglamorous pastoral investment rather than
continued programmatic activity — the single factor Smith's Karen
data identifies as most consistently fatal to otherwise promising
movements.
