# Tibetan Buddhism, Meditation, and Christianity's Historical Encounter with Buddhism

## Overview

This file continues the synthesis of David Burnett's *The Spirit of
Buddhism*, covering the tantric development of Tibetan Buddhism, the
centrality of meditation practice across Buddhist traditions, the
four historical phases of Christian missionary contact with Buddhism,
the West's nineteenth-century "discovery" of Buddhism as an object of
study, and Buddhism's twentieth-century global resurgence.

## The Tibetan Tradition and Tantra

Between roughly the third and seventh centuries AD, a distinctively
tantric tradition emerged within Indian Buddhism, often described as
the "Third Turning of the Wheel of Dharma" — following the first
turning (the Buddha's original sermon on the Four Noble Truths) and
the second (the Mahayana revelation of emptiness/*sunyata*).
Tantra's traditional origin story holds it to be the Buddha's own
esoteric teaching; linguistically, *tantra* designates both a
distinct class of scripture (alongside the Vinaya and Abhidharma
texts) and, more broadly, a practice aimed at carrying the mind from
the surface of ordinary existence to the "hidden groundwork" of
reality beneath it. Practically, this is pursued through ritual and
especially tantric yoga, on the theory that passion itself can be
exhausted through passion — cravings for food, drink, or sex
overcome not by suppression but by a disciplined rising above them.
Tibetan tradition sometimes claims to include all three
"vehicles" — Hinayana (Theravada), Mahayana, and Mantrayana (tantra)
— with the mantra vehicle understood as a direct route capable of
reaching buddhahood within a single lifetime rather than across many
reincarnations, through highly specific and guarded practices.

Two Tibetan Buddhist ideals with far-reaching cultural consequences
deserve particular missiological attention. The **ideal of
detachment**, inherited from earlier Vedic and Brahmanic ideals of the
holy man who abandons home and family to seek enlightenment,
encourages withdrawal from worldly relationships; this has produced
societies with comparatively weak family obligation structures —
children are relatively free to leave home for religious purposes,
fathers are often emotionally distant figures commanding respect but
not necessarily love, and marital breakup is common (with divorce
rates reported as high as 30% among some Tibetan Buddhist peoples).
The **ideal of nonviolence**, extended chiefly toward animals, has
shaped both individual behavior (workers carefully relocating
earthworms during construction rather than risk killing them) and
statecraft (a sixteenth-century Dalai Lama insisting the Mongol
Khan's empire end animal sacrifice; the thirteenth Dalai Lama citing
concern for animals as one reason to demand a British military
withdrawal from Tibet). Because farming itself is considered to
carry the sin of inadvertently killing small creatures in the soil,
and deliberately killing animals for food is considered gravely
sinful, Tibetans have traditionally preferred that butchering be done
by someone of another religion, usually a Muslim — meaning the simple
act of eating carries a burden of guilt scarcely imaginable to
outsiders.

## Meditation as the Living Center of Buddhist Practice

Meditation is not unique to Buddhism as a spiritual discipline, but
its centrality to the very possibility of liberation is distinctively
Buddhist — practiced not only by monks and nuns but, wherever
Buddhism has been vigorous, by committed lay people as well. Each of
the Four Noble Truths implies a corresponding activity, and the
fourth — the path leading to the cessation of suffering — is
specifically *bhavana*, "bringing into being," commonly translated
"meditation": the disciplined cultivation of the wisdom (Sanskrit:
*prajna*) that directly perceives reality "as it really is." Learning
meditation, in this tradition, resembles learning to play a musical
instrument more than absorbing a body of information — it demands
sustained regular practice and patience rather than mere intellectual
assent, a point with direct bearing on why purely propositional or
argumentative approaches to Buddhist audiences often fall flat: the
Buddhist framework for religious knowing is fundamentally experiential
and practice-based rather than doctrinal.

## Four Phases of Christian Contact with Buddhism

Burnett identifies four broad historical phases of Christian
missionary contact with the Buddhist world.

**The Nestorians.** Emerging in the Tigris-Euphrates valley in the
mid-second century, the Nestorian church — so named after the
Christian scholar Nestorius, condemned by the Council of Ephesus in
431 for teaching a duality in the person of Christ, whose followers
migrated eastward into Persia — remained a religious minority even
under pressure from the Sassanid state religion of Zoroastrianism
after AD 300. Despite (or perhaps because of) this minority status,
the church developed an outstanding missionary vision, penetrating
Afghanistan, India, Turkestan, Siberia, and eventually China, where
Nestorian traders arrived by 578 and missionaries by 635.

**The Jesuits.** By the seventeenth century the Catholic church was
sending missionaries deep into Central Asia and, eventually, Tibet
itself. The Jesuit Antonio de Andrade, disguised as a Hindu pilgrim,
departed India in March 1624, crossed a 17,900-foot Himalayan pass,
and reached Tsaparang, capital of the western Tibetan kingdom of
Gu-ge, where he impressed the king and queen sufficiently to be
granted permission to establish a mission and preach the gospel — a
promising opening that, like most such Jesuit initiatives in Tibet,
would prove difficult to sustain over subsequent generations.

**Western Protestants.** Nineteenth-century Protestant missions
extended across the Tibetan Buddhist cultural sphere, which by then
stretched, under Lhasa's religious influence, from Siberia to the
Himalayas and from Manchuria to the Volga. This "grand opportunity,"
as Burnett terms it, was substantially curtailed by twentieth-century
political upheaval: the 1917 Russian Revolution and subsequent civil
war spilled into Mongolia, where a Soviet-backed government declared
the world's second Communist state in 1924 and launched a campaign of
terror against lamas and monasteries; by the outbreak of World War II,
Tibetan Buddhism as an institution had virtually ceased to exist in
Mongolia, alongside the parallel destruction of Christian missionary
work in the same territory. (Mongolian Buddhism has undergone a
revival since the country's 1990 democratic reforms.)

**The contemporary international mission** continues this history
into the present, operating in a religio-political landscape shaped
by the preceding three phases and by the twentieth century's
alternating waves of suppression and revival across the Tibetan
Buddhist world.

## Theocracy and National Identity

For most Tibetan Buddhists, *chö* (dharma/religion) is inseparable
from the whole of life — there is no clean division between religion
and politics. Mahayana Buddhism entered Tibet under royal sponsorship
and flourished partly because of its political ties to the Chinese
imperial court; from the time of Song Tsen Gampo, Tibetan kings took
the title "chö gyal" ("religious king"). This fusion of religious and
political authority reached its fullest expression in 1642, when the
fifth Dalai Lama, backed by a Mongol army, seized control of the
country for his Gelugpa order — a theocratic arrangement that
persisted until 1950. The same pattern of lama-kings or reincarnating
monk-rulers (*khutukhtus* in Mongolia) was reproduced in Sikkim,
Bhutan, and Mongolia; only the Himalayan hill tribes under Hindu or
Muslim rule, and the Mongol tribes under Russian rule, escaped this
theocratic pattern. Communism dismantled these theocracies across
the region in the twentieth century, but the underlying cultural
assumption — that religious and political legitimacy are
inseparable — continues to shape Tibetan Buddhist identity and its
diaspora politics around the present Dalai Lama.

## The West's Discovery of "Buddhism"

It is striking how recently Buddhism became known to the West as a
distinct religious tradition: as late as 1802, the *English
Encyclopaedia* could describe "Budun" only as a Ceylonese god fabled
to have risen through successive transmigrations from an insect.
European travelers and Jesuit missionaries to Tibet, China, and Japan
had recorded only garbled impressions of what they took to be an
obscure cult. It was colonial-era information gathering — texts
collected and shipped to London and Paris for translation — that
gradually produced, by the mid-1830s, a coherent Western understanding
of what would come to be called "Buddhism," distinguished for the
first time from Hinduism and recognized as one of Asia's great
religious traditions. The term "oriental renaissance" (coined by
Friedrich Schlegel in 1803) captures the excitement with which
educated nineteenth-century Europeans greeted this discovery — an
excitement later crystallized in popular literary form in works like
Edwin Arnold's poem *The Light of Asia*.

## Buddhist Resurgence

Despite the intensity of various Christian missionary efforts across
Asia, converts to Christianity have historically remained the
exception rather than the rule. Toward the end of the nineteenth
century, the earlier assimilation of Western culture across Buddhist
Asia was arrested, and a new sense of cultural and religious identity
began to emerge — a Buddhist revival closely associated with movements
toward national independence and driven substantially by the educated
laity rather than the monastic establishment. A key figure was Don
David Hevavitarana (1864-1933) of Colombo, later known as Anagarika
Dharmapala: though educated in Roman Catholic and Anglican schools
(there being no Buddhist schools in Colombo at the time), an
experience that left him with an impression of Christianity as
fanatical and intolerant of other faiths, he became one of the most
influential lay champions of a modernized, socially engaged "Protestant
Buddhism" in Sri Lanka — a template for lay-led Buddhist revival that
would echo across twentieth-century Buddhist Asia and, eventually, the
West.

## Missiological Reflection

The historical record surveyed here cuts two ways for Christian
mission. On one hand, it documents a real history of costly, often
short-lived missionary engagement with the Buddhist world across
sixteen centuries, from the Nestorians through the Jesuits to
Protestant missions — engagement repeatedly disrupted less by
Buddhist resistance alone than by political upheaval (Sassanid
pressure, Chinese and Mongolian political change, Soviet
persecution). On the other hand, it documents the resilience and
adaptability of Buddhism itself, which has repeatedly renewed itself
through lay-led revival movements closely tied to national and
cultural identity — a dynamic Christian workers need to understand
rather than underestimate.
