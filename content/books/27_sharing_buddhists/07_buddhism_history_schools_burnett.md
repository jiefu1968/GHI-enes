# Buddhism: History, Schools, and Development

## Overview

This file synthesizes the historical core of David Burnett's *The
Spirit of Buddhism: A Christian Perspective on Buddhist Thought* —
tracing the Buddha's life and teaching, the councils and doctrinal
splits that produced Buddhism's major schools, the rise of Mahayana
philosophy, and Buddhism's expansion across South, Southeast, and East
Asia. A companion file covers Tibetan Buddhism, meditation practice,
Buddhism's encounter with Christian missions, and its modern global
resurgence.

## The Life of Siddartha Gautama

Gautama's life is veiled by time and shrouded in the myths of later
followers; early Pali literature nowhere provides a complete
historical record, though enough fragments survive to confirm he was
a historical figure. The first full biography, Ashvagosha's
*Buddhacarita* ("The Acts of the Buddha"), dates only to the first
century AD, centuries after Gautama's death, and each Buddhist school
has since told his story with its own doctrinal emphasis. Western
scholars have long debated the exact dates of his life, working
backward from King Asoka's accession (c. 268 BC); depending on which
Buddhist source is followed, Gautama would have lived either 566-486
BC or 448-368 BC. Multiple titles attach to him: "the Buddha" (the
Enlightened or Awakened One, a title earned after his enlightenment),
Siddartha or Siddatha (his personal name, meaning "goal achieved"),
and Gautama or Gotama (his clan name).

Gautama was born to a warrior nobleman, Suddhadana, and his wife Maya,
among the Sakya people near the modern India-Nepal border. Tradition
holds that the astrologer Asita examined the infant and foresaw two
possible destinies: universal monarch or supreme Buddha. His father,
hoping for the former, raised him in royal luxury, sheltered from
suffering — setting the stage for the traditional account of his later
encounter with old age, sickness, and death outside the palace walls,
which propelled his renunciation and quest for enlightenment,
eventually articulated as the Four Noble Truths and the Middle Path
between asceticism and indulgence.

## Councils, Controversies, and the Origins of Sectarian Buddhism

Before his death (parinirvana), the Buddha deliberately declined to
appoint a successor or central authority, instructing his disciple
Ananda that the Dharma (doctrine) and Vinaya (discipline) he had
taught would themselves serve as teacher after his death. This
individualist orientation — emphasis on personal endeavor toward
enlightenment rather than institutional hierarchy — concentrated later
disputes on determining the correct content of Dharma and Vinaya.
Because the Buddha deliberately refused to speculate on certain
questions (such as his own nature after nirvana), viewing such
speculation as a distraction from the practical goal of liberation,
his silence on these unanswered questions became fertile ground for
later doctrinal divergence, though such divergence rarely produced the
kind of acrimonious splits familiar from Christian church history —
adherents of different schools often continued living in the same
monasteries.

A major flashpoint came roughly forty years after the Buddha's death,
at a non-canonical council at Pataliputra, associated with a monk
named Mahadeva, who publicly questioned the perfection of the Arhats
(those believed to have achieved enlightenment) — proposing five
points, including that Arhats remain subject to sexual temptation,
retain residual ignorance, can experience doubt, depend on others for
information, and can cry out in despair. This controversy raised two
enduring issues: the insularity of the Arhat's solitary pursuit of
enlightenment versus concern for the laity, and the growing gap
between the exalted status later attributed to the Buddha himself and
that of ordinary enlightened Arhats — a gap that would widen
dramatically with the rise of Mahayana.

## Asoka and the Spread Beyond India

Buddhism's transformation from a regional Indian movement to a
transnational religion owes much to the patronage of King Asoka (r.
c. 269-232 BC), whose missionary initiative sent Buddhist teaching
beyond his empire's borders, notably to Ceylon (modern Sri Lanka),
where it took firm root and later became an important center for
preserving Theravada tradition after Buddhism declined in India
itself. The Mauryan Empire declined rapidly after Asoka's death,
eventually giving way to periods of invasion and political
fragmentation, but the popular memory of the Buddha as one of India's
great religious teachers endured and continued to spread through Sri
Lanka and Southeast Asia.

## Theravada in South-East Asia

From the very beginning, the Buddha and his early Sangha conceived of
themselves as a missionary organization; within weeks of his own
enlightenment he sent out his first disciples with the charge to
"wander for the gain of the many, for the happiness of the many, out
of compassion for the world" and to preach the Dharma, "lovely in the
beginning, lovely in the middle, lovely at the end." In contrast to
the exclusivism of the Brahmanism from which it emerged, the Buddha's
message was addressed to all people regardless of caste. Ceylon's own
tradition, recorded in the *Dipavamsa* ("Island Chronicle") and
*Mahavamsa* ("Great Chronicle"), along with the writings of the
scholar Buddhaghosa, credits the island's conversion to Asoka's
missionary initiative, after which Ceylon became a crucial center for
transmitting and preserving the Theravada tradition to the rest of
Southeast Asia.

## The Rise of Mahayana Buddhism

Political upheaval following Asoka's death — invasions from the
northwest, rival dynasties on the Deccan Plateau, and the eventual rise
of the Gupta Empire under Candra Gupta (from AD 320) — provided the
backdrop for a major philosophical development within Buddhism.
Because the Buddha had encouraged each follower to pursue
enlightenment and teach according to their own understanding, provided
it remained within the basic framework of the Dharma, this openness to
innovation eventually produced, between roughly 100 BC and AD 100, a
substantial body of new Buddhist writings that would come to be known
as Mahayana ("Great Vehicle") — in implicit contrast to what its
proponents termed the more limited "Hinayana" ("Lesser Vehicle") of
earlier Theravada-oriented schools.

## The Silence of the Buddha and the Philosophy of Emptiness

The Buddha's characteristic silence on certain metaphysical questions
— such as whether the self (atman) exists — was not merely an
evasion but reflected a considered philosophical position: to affirm
atman would align him with a rival doctrine he rejected, while to
deny it outright risked being heard as nihilism; facing the limits of
language itself, he chose silence. It took roughly six centuries,
however, for this silence to crystallize into a fully articulated
philosophy, achieved by the philosopher Nagarjuna (born c. AD 150),
founder of the Madhyamika ("Middle Way" or "Emptiness Teaching")
school, who identified the highest wisdom of Buddhism with the
concept of *sunyata* ("emptiness"). Madhyamika, along with the later
Yogacara school, came to dominate Mahayana thought in India; Yogacara
in particular would exert significant influence on the later
development of Tibetan Buddhism.

## Chinese Expressions of Buddhism

By the time Buddhism became firmly established in China — reaching
its fullest classical development during the Tang dynasty of the
eighth and ninth centuries — Indian Buddhism had already flourished
for roughly a thousand years, and the great works of both Theravada
and Mahayana traditions were already in existence. Chinese scholars,
who held the written word in especially high regard, accepted the
Chinese translations of Buddhist scriptures as the literal words of
the Buddha; given the sheer volume of this literature, individual
scholars typically specialized in and elevated particular texts as
paramount, treating others as merely introductory. Out of this
intensive study, and in creative dialogue with pre-existing Confucian
and Taoist thought, Chinese scholars formulated substantially new
doctrinal syntheses, giving rise to the distinctively Chinese schools
that were mostly founded during the unified Sui and Tang dynasties
(late sixth through eighth centuries) — schools that would later
travel onward to shape Buddhism in Korea and Japan.

## Missiological Reflection

This historical survey underscores two points of enduring relevance
for Christian witness. First, Buddhism has been a genuinely missionary
religion since its inception — a fact too often underestimated by
Christians who perceive it as a passive philosophy rather than an
actively propagating faith. Second, the doctrinal diversity that
emerged from councils, controversies, and regional adaptation across
a millennium means that no single argument or approach addresses
"Buddhism" as a whole; understanding which stream of the tradition —
Theravada, early Mahayana philosophy, or the regionally adapted forms
that later developed in China, Japan, and Tibet — a particular
Buddhist neighbor draws upon is essential to any effective
conversation about Christ.
