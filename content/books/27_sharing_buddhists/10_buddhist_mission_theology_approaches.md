# Buddhist Mission Theology: Demographics, Approaches, and Ministry Models

## Overview

This file synthesizes several chapters from *Sharing Jesus Effectively
in the Buddhist World* (ed. David Lim, Steve Spaulding, and Paul De
Neui), an anthology of essays by evangelical mission "reflective
practitioners" committed to developing more biblical and effective
approaches to reaching Buddhist peoples. It covers the demographic
scale of the task, five common missiological approaches to Buddhism
and their respective strengths and weaknesses, the specific challenge
of presenting Christ's deity to a Theravada Buddhist mind, and two
contrasting ministry philosophy models — "strongman" apologetics
versus the gentler, more indirect "upaya" (skillful means) approach.

## The Demographic Scale of Global Buddhism

Todd M. Johnson and Bobby Jangsun Ryu open the anthology by noting
that images of the Buddha — ranging from his gaunt South Asian
depictions to his portly East Asian form — are ubiquitous globally,
and that hundreds of millions of people turn to these images as
objects of devotion. They highlight a linguistic distinction worth
retaining for mission strategy: the Pali/Sanskrit term *sāsana* can
refer both to the localized religion or tradition of a particular
nation (Thai Buddhism, Japanese Buddhism, and so on, each shaped by
its own ethnic, linguistic, and cultural community) and to the wider,
transcendent global community of Buddhists to which each local
community understands itself to belong. Effective strategic thinking
about Buddhist mission needs to hold both scales in view
simultaneously — the specific local Buddhism a missionary actually
encounters, and the broader global Buddhist self-understanding that
shapes how that local community interprets outside religious claims.

## Five Approaches to Christian Mission Among Buddhists

Terry Muck identifies five common historical approaches Christian
mission has taken toward Buddhism (and, more broadly, toward people of
other faiths), each with real strengths and real limitations:

**Concept comparisons** — the most common approach, following the
church's own doctrinal tradition (dating back at least to its early
contact with Greek philosophy): analyzing a religion into its
constituent doctrines, comparing and contrasting them with Christian
doctrine on content and function, and arguing for Christianity's
superior coherence and effectiveness. This method's popularity stems
from how naturally it follows the way Christian doctrine itself is
conceptualized, taught, and used evangelistically — but its
weaknesses include a tendency to reduce a lived, practice-centered
religion like Buddhism to a set of abstract propositions that may not
correspond to how ordinary Buddhists actually experience their faith.

**Cultural forms**, **scientific witness**, **ritual practices**, and
**folk religion functions** constitute the remaining four approaches
Muck surveys, each addressing a different dimension of how Buddhism
is actually lived — its material and artistic culture, its claims to
compatibility with (or superiority to) modern science, its ritual
life, and the everyday functional needs (protection, luck, guidance)
that folk Buddhist practice serves for ordinary adherents. A mature
missiological strategy typically draws on several of these approaches
in combination, matched to the specific Buddhist population and
individual in view, rather than relying on concept comparison alone.

## The Difficulty of Presenting Christ's Deity to Theravada Buddhists

M. S. Vasanthakumar addresses a problem central to Christian witness
in Theravada contexts, especially Sri Lanka: in societies whose
worldview has a place for theistic conceptions, evangelists can
readily present Jesus Christ as God or as one of several gods within
an existing theistic framework. But Theravada Buddhist societies
present a starkly different challenge, because their worldview simply
has no structural place for a theistic conception of ultimate
reality. Buddhist audiences, absent careful groundwork, tend to
assimilate the Buddhist perception of Jesus Christ to categories they
already possess — comparing him, for instance, to the Buddha prior to
his own enlightenment, or to a Hindu god — rather than grasping the
distinctively Christian claim of full and unique deity. Every
Christian defense of Christ's deity risks appearing simply futile or
category-confused to a Theravada audience unless the underlying
metaphysical gap is addressed directly and patiently, rather than
assumed away.

## Two Ministry Philosophy Models: Strongman versus Gentle Strength (Upaya)

Russell H. Bowers, Jr. observes that just as the substantive messages
of Christianity and Buddhism display superficial similarities
resting atop deep philosophical differences, so too do their
respective philosophies of ministry and propagation — and reflecting
on *how* each faith spreads itself can illuminate Christian
self-understanding as much as understanding of Buddhism itself.

The **"minister as strongman"** model presents the biblical Christian
as unintimidated and unyielding, ceding no ground whatsoever to
those outside the faith — exemplified by pastors in contexts like the
rapidly growing Cambodian church (legalized in 1990) who fear being
criticized by fellow pastors as compromisers if they show any
apparent accommodation to surrounding Buddhist-animist practice among
new converts working out how to relate to unconverted family and
neighbors.

By contrast, Bowers develops a model of **"gentle strength" drawing
on the Buddhist concept of *upaya* ("skillful means")** — the idea,
native to Mahayana Buddhist teaching itself, that a teacher
strategically adapts the form, pace, and directness of instruction to
the actual capacity and readiness of the hearer, rather than
delivering the fullest or most direct statement of truth
indiscriminately in every circumstance. Applied to Christian
ministry among Buddhists, this suggests that genuine doctrinal
firmness and evangelistic conviction need not be paired with a
confrontational or maximally direct communication style; strength and
gentleness are not, on this model, in tension with one another.

## Missiological Reflection

Taken together, these chapters caution against two opposite errors:
treating Buddhist mission as a matter of superior argumentation alone
(the limitation of concept-comparison approaches and of "strongman"
apologetics), and treating philosophical or cultural adaptation as a
substitute for the substantive theological work needed to actually
communicate distinctive Christian claims — such as Christ's full
deity — into a worldview genuinely structured without a theistic
category. Effective practitioners, the anthology suggests, need both
patient contextual sensitivity (drawing where appropriate on concepts
like *upaya*) and clarity about which theological claims cannot be
softened without being lost altogether.
