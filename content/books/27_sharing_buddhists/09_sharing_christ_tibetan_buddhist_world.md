# Sharing Christ in the Tibetan Buddhist World: The Ambassador Model

## Overview

This file synthesizes key material from *Sharing Christ in the
Tibetan Buddhist World* (2nd ed.), a practical missiological manual
focused specifically on Tibetan Buddhist peoples across Central Asia.
The book organizes its guidance around Paul's self-description as an
"ambassador for Christ" (2 Corinthians 5:19-20), developing this into
a framework — the "Seven Pillars of Wisdom" — for preparation,
spiritual conflict, historical understanding, and practical
engagement.

## The Ambassador Model

An ambassador is a representative sent by one nation to another,
tasked with explaining his home government's policies to a host
country's people — which requires him first to understand that host
country's people, their problems, and how they see life. A skillful
ambassador can present his own nation's policies in terms the host
population can understand and appreciate, even without securing their
agreement. In the same way, Christians represent the kingdom of God
to the people of a "host country" — in this case, the Tibetan
Buddhist world — and their words, actions, and use of time shape what
that host population comes to think about God's kingdom. No one
simply appoints himself ambassador without prior training in lesser
posts; likewise, Christians seeking to represent Christ effectively
across this particular cultural and religious divide need
intentional preparation, not just goodwill.

## Preparation: The Ambassador in Training

Effective preparation begins with being settled in the basics of
one's own faith and having a clear personal commitment to Christ —
because Tibetan Buddhism, especially in its Western-facing forms,
propagates itself partly through the appeal of occult mysticism and
sophisticated philosophical argument (for many Western adherents, it
functions more as philosophy than as religion in any traditional
sense). Christians unsettled in their own faith, or recently
converted, are advised to seek ministry elsewhere until more firmly
grounded, as are those with any personal background in occult
practice, given the particular risks discussed below. Formal training
in Bible, systematic theology, and apologetics is especially valuable
for engaging philosophically minded Tibetan Buddhist interlocutors,
for whom the problem of suffering functions as a central
intellectual and existential concern requiring a substantive, not
superficial, Christian response.

## The Ambassador in Spiritual Conflict

The New Testament's accounts of Jesus exercising authority over evil
spirits (e.g., Mark 5:1-20) and confronting Satan directly (Matthew
4:1-11) are sometimes read by Western Christians as merely
allegorical, or as a first-century cultural accommodation with no
ongoing relevance. Christian workers among Tibetan Buddhists in
Central Asia, however, consistently report that demons and spirits
are experienced as powerful, active forces — feared or worshiped by
the great majority of the local population, and discussed in an
entirely matter-of-fact way as part of ordinary daily life. Tantra,
one of Tibetan Buddhism's most significant doctrines, explicitly
encourages a kind of possession by spiritual forces as a means toward
final enlightenment; even educated Western Tibetan Buddhists who
regard such spirits merely as projections of the subconscious mind,
denying them independent reality, still participate in a system whose
occult practices — astrology, the use of oracles, and others — offer
real opportunity for spiritual manipulation and harm, whether or not
the practitioner grants them ontological reality. Christian workers in
this field will regularly meet people entangled in a wide variety of
occult practices, some of them the product of real spiritual forces
rather than mere cultural residue, even though Western Tibetan
Buddhists rarely discuss these practices openly outside their own
community.

## The Ambassador at Work: A Pauline Precedent

The early church faced an analogous challenge as Christianity spread
beyond Jewish synagogues into the Greek and Roman world, where people
worshiped a pantheon of unpredictable gods and goddesses through
magic, offerings, and occult ritual, believed the stars controlled
human destiny, made offerings to nature spirits, and were beginning to
worship the emperor as a god — a worldview with real structural
parallels to the Tibetan Buddhist context. Paul's speech to the
Areopagus in Athens (Acts 17), delivered to the city's most learned
philosophers after he had carefully observed their objects of worship
(including an altar "TO AN UNKNOWN GOD"), models the ambassador's
task: neither dismissing the host culture's religious seriousness nor
capitulating to its content, but finding a genuine point of contact —
in Paul's case, the altar to an unknown god — from which to proclaim
the God who actually made the world.

## Historical Background: Buddhism's Path to Tibet

Buddhism reached Tibet by a circuitous historical route through the
Silk Road oasis cities (Kashgar, Khotan, Turfan, Dunhuang), which
became centers of Buddhist learning and culture during the first
millennium AD and where Tibetans had their earliest tentative contact
with both Mahayana Buddhism and, separately, Nestorian Christianity —
though Mahayana had a substantial head start, reaching China some six
hundred years before its Christian counterpart. The Mahayana emphasis
on liberation achievable within a single lifetime, with the specific
method left to the individual practitioner, left the tradition
structurally open to absorbing elements from other Indian religious
currents, particularly those promising a shorter path to
enlightenment — a dynamic that helps explain Tibetan Buddhism's later
absorption of tantric practice.

By the seventeenth century, Tibetan Buddhism's religious dominance
extended across a vast swath of Central Asia — from Siberia to the
Himalayas, from Manchuria to the Volga — with Lhasa functioning as
the recognized spiritual center for the entire region. This scale of
influence is directly relevant to strategic thinking about mission
among the various peoples (Tibetans, Mongolians, and others) who look
to Lhasa's religious authority even where they are not ethnically
Tibetan themselves.

## Twentieth-Century Political Rupture

The political convulsions of the twentieth century radically altered
this landscape. Soviet-backed Communist forces invaded Mongolia by
1920, and Outer Mongolia declared itself the world's second Communist
state in 1924; the new Soviet-aligned government waged a campaign of
terror specifically against lamas and their monasteries, jailing or
executing leading Buddhists and destroying their institutions, such
that by the outbreak of World War II, Tibetan Buddhism as an
organized institution had virtually ceased to exist within Mongolia.
This suppression continued under hardline Stalinism until Mongolia's
1990 democratic reforms, after which Tibetan Buddhism began a
significant revival, with previously closed or destroyed monasteries
reopened and Mongolian monks expressing renewed interest in spreading
basic Buddhist teaching among the population — a reminder that
periods of severe religious suppression in this region have
historically been followed by vigorous religious revival once
political conditions shift, a pattern with implications for how
Christian workers understand the durability of their own gains and
losses over time in the same territory.

## Missiological Reflection

The ambassador framework offers a disciplined alternative to two
common but inadequate postures: an aggressive "strongman" apologetics
that treats every point of Tibetan Buddhist belief as an enemy
position to be defeated, and an uncritical accommodation that
either denies the reality of the spiritual forces Tibetan Buddhists
themselves take seriously, or absorbs Buddhist concepts into Christian
practice without discernment. Genuine ambassadorial work requires the
patient historical, cultural, and spiritual preparation outlined
above — understanding both the philosophical sophistication that
attracts many Western adherents and the occult and spiritual realities
that shape everyday life for Tibetan Buddhists in Asia — before
attempting to communicate the gospel in terms the host culture can
actually understand and weigh.
