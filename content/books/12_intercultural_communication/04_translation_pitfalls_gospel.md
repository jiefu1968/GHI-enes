# Translation and Interpretation Pitfalls in Gospel Communication

Communicating the gospel across a language boundary is not simply substituting words — every translation choice carries theological weight, and every interpreter or translator is also, unavoidably, a cultural mediator.

## False cognates and false friends
Words that sound similar across languages can carry very different meanings. A term borrowed to translate "sin" or "grace" may already carry a strong prior meaning in the receptor language's traditional religious vocabulary (folk religion, Buddhist, or Confucian usage) that colors or distorts the biblical concept being communicated — sometimes helpfully (a bridge concept), sometimes in ways that quietly smuggle in a different worldview than the one intended.

## Untranslatable idioms
Biblical idioms ("gird up your loins," "stiff-necked people," a Semitic idiom for anger) do not carry meaning when translated word-for-word into a language where the same body-part or image has no equivalent association. A literal rendering can produce confusion or unintended comedy rather than the intended emotional or rhetorical effect.

## Formal-equivalence vs. dynamic-equivalence translation
Two major philosophies of Bible translation make different tradeoffs:

- **Formal equivalence** (word-for-word, e.g., ESV, NASB) prioritizes preserving the original language's grammatical structure and word choices as closely as possible, even where the result reads less naturally in the receptor language. Strength: preserves ambiguity, wordplay, and structure that later close study may depend on. Weakness: can obscure meaning for readers without background training.
- **Dynamic/functional equivalence** (thought-for-thought, e.g., NIV, NLT) prioritizes reproducing the same effect and meaning in the receptor language, even where that requires restructuring sentences or choosing a different idiom. Strength: more immediately intelligible, especially for oral or first-generation-literate audiences. Weakness: the translator's own interpretive judgment plays a larger role in determining "the meaning" a reader ultimately receives.

Neither philosophy is theologically illegitimate on its own; missionaries working with Bible translation or interpretation should understand which choice a given translation has made and why, especially when a term of real theological weight (justification, propitiation, covenant) is at stake.

## Practical application for interpreted ministry
When working through a live interpreter (preaching, teaching, counseling), short, simple sentence units interpret far more reliably than long, subordinate-clause-heavy sentences — not because the audience is unsophisticated, but because interpretation compounds ambiguity with every added clause. Checking in with the interpreter beforehand about how they render key theological terms (grace, repentance, sin, holy) is worth more than almost any other single preparation step, since these terms are exactly where false cognates and worldview-freighted vocabulary do the most damage.
