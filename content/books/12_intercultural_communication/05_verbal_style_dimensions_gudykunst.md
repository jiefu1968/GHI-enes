# Verbal Communication Style Dimensions (Gudykunst)

Alongside Hall's high-context/low-context spectrum (file 01), William Gudykunst and collaborators identified additional axes specifically about *verbal* style — how much is said, how directly, and whose voice the words center. These are useful because two cultures can be similarly "high-context" and still differ sharply on these dimensions, or vice versa.

## 1. Direct vs. Indirect
How explicitly a want, need, opinion, or refusal is stated in words. A direct style states the point plainly ("I disagree," "No, I can't help with that"). An indirect style hints, qualifies, or embeds the point in a story, question, or third-party reference, leaving the listener to infer the actual message.

## 2. Elaborate vs. Exacting vs. Succinct
How much talk is considered normal and appropriate:
- **Elaborate** — rich, expressive, sometimes repetitive language is valued (common in many Arab and Latin cultures); a short answer to a warm question can feel curt or even rude.
- **Exacting** — the precise amount of information needed is given, no more, no less (common in much of Northern Europe).
- **Succinct** — understatement, silence, and terse answers are valued; a person who talks "too much" is seen as unwise or untrustworthy (found in various East Asian and Native American communication norms).

## 3. Personal vs. Contextual
Whether an utterance centers the speaker's individual voice and feelings (personal style — common in individualist cultures) or is shaped primarily by the speaker's *role and relationship* to the listener (contextual style — common in collectivist, hierarchical cultures, where what is said and how depends heavily on relative status, age, and role).

## 4. Instrumental vs. Affective
Whether speech is primarily goal- and task-oriented, focused on achieving a clear outcome for the sender (instrumental — common in low-context, individualist cultures), or primarily relationship-oriented, focused on the receiver's face and the relationship itself, even at some cost to efficiency (affective — common in high-context, collectivist cultures).

## Why layering these onto Hall's model matters
A missionary who only thinks in terms of "high-context vs. low-context" can still misjudge a specific interaction. For example: two high-context cultures might differ sharply on elaborate vs. succinct (an elaborate high-context culture and a succinct high-context culture both rely heavily on context, but sound completely different out loud). Reading a person's communication only through one dimension misses real, practically important variation — these four additional axes give a fuller diagnostic picture of *why* a specific conversation felt off, beyond just "they're high-context and I'm low-context."
