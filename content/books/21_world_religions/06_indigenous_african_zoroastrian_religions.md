# Indigenous American Religions, African Traditional Religion, and Zoroastrianism

## Overview

This piece surveys three religious traditions less commonly covered in
standard missiological training but directly relevant to missionaries
serving indigenous American communities, African peoples and their
global diaspora, or historically Zoroastrian-influenced regions
(particularly Iran and parts of South Asia). Alongside their
significant historical influence — Zoroastrianism, for instance, likely
shaped later Jewish, Christian, and Islamic ideas about angels, a
cosmic conflict between good and evil, and final judgment — these
traditions remain living faiths and cultural substrates practiced or
culturally influential today.

## Indigenous Religions of the Americas

Indigenous North, Central, and South American religious traditions
vary enormously by nation and region, yet share recurring patterns
worth understanding as a family of related worldviews rather than a
single uniform "Native American religion." Common threads include: a
strong sense of the sacred pervading the natural world (animals,
plants, geographic features, and weather often understood as
inhabited by or expressive of spiritual power); belief in a soul or
vital force distinguishable from the physical body, often with
specific beliefs about its journey after death; ritual practices tied
closely to subsistence activities (hunting rites among peoples like
the Naskapi, agricultural ceremonies among Pueblo peoples like the
Hopi); and origin stories and sacred narratives carrying deep moral and
cosmological significance, transmitted orally across generations.
Mesoamerican and Andean civilizations (the Aztec and Inca empires among
them) developed highly organized state religions with elaborate
calendars, temple architecture, and priesthoods, in contrast to the
smaller-scale, more decentralized religious life of many North
American peoples. Colonization brought catastrophic disruption to
these traditions — through forced conversion, suppression, and
demographic collapse — but does not represent a straightforward
storyline of consistent religious substitution; many indigenous
communities blended, resisted, or preserved traditional practice
alongside or underneath imposed Christian forms, a pattern missionaries
should understand rather than assume indigenous conversion was simply
complete and uncomplicated.

## African Traditional Religion

Traditional African religious systems, again enormously diverse across
the continent's thousands of ethnic groups, nonetheless share several
recurring structural features useful for missiological understanding.
Most traditional African religious systems affirm a supreme creator
God, often understood as somewhat distant from everyday affairs,
alongside a populated spiritual world of lesser deities, ancestral
spirits, and other spiritual forces that are more immediately and
practically engaged in daily religious life than the supreme God
himself. Ancestors hold particularly significant standing — deceased
family and community members are frequently understood to remain
active participants in the community's life, requiring ongoing
remembrance, respect, and ritual attention, with failure to honor them
properly believed capable of bringing misfortune. Divination,
healing, and protective ritual (addressing illness, misfortune, and
social conflict) form a significant, practically oriented dimension of
traditional religious life, engaging spiritual causation behind
everyday crises in ways structurally similar to what missiologists
elsewhere call the "excluded middle" — the zone of everyday spiritual
concern that a purely Western, two-tiered (natural/supernatural)
worldview tends to leave unaddressed. Traditional African religion has
historically shown a strong pattern of coexisting or blending with both
Christianity and Islam across the continent, producing a wide range of
syncretistic and contextualized expressions that missionaries need to
engage with theological discernment rather than either wholesale
rejection or uncritical acceptance.

## Zoroastrianism

Zoroastrianism, founded by the Iranian prophet Zarathustra (dates
debated among scholars, commonly placed sometime between roughly 1500
and 600 BCE), is among the world's oldest continuously practiced
monotheistic religions and exerted significant historical influence on
Judaism, Christianity, and Islam during the periods of Persian
imperial contact with the Jewish people (notably during and after the
Babylonian exile, when the Persian king Cyrus permitted the Jews'
return, an event Isaiah's prophecy addresses by name). Zoroastrian
theology centers on Ahura Mazda, the wise, good creator God, engaged in
cosmic conflict against a spirit of falsehood and destruction, with
human beings called to align themselves with truth and goodness
through their own free moral choice; sacred fire serves as a central
ritual symbol of purity and divine presence, tended in dedicated fire
temples. Zoroastrian eschatology anticipates a final judgment, bodily
resurrection, and the ultimate triumph of good over evil — concepts
strikingly parallel to later Jewish and Christian apocalyptic
expectation, leading many scholars to trace at least some influence
from Persian Zoroastrian thought on post-exilic Jewish theology, though
the direction and extent of that influence remains debated. Today,
Zoroastrianism survives as a much smaller minority religion,
particularly among the Parsi community of India and smaller
communities in Iran and the global diaspora.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

Missionaries serving indigenous American or African communities
benefit from recognizing that colonization-era conversion was often
incomplete, contested, or blended rather than a clean historical
replacement of traditional religion with Christianity, meaning
traditional beliefs about ancestors, spiritual causation of everyday
crises, and sacred connection to land and nature frequently remain
active beneath a Christian profession and require the same kind of
direct, respectful theological engagement missiologists recommend for
addressing the "excluded middle" elsewhere in the world. Zoroastrianism's
likely historical influence on Jewish and Christian apocalyptic
categories (final judgment, resurrection, cosmic conflict between good
and evil) offers an interesting point of shared vocabulary for
engaging Zoroastrian or Parsi friends, while still requiring clear
presentation of Christianity's distinctive claims about Christ's unique
incarnation, atonement, and resurrection.

## Discussion prompts for training

- Where do you see traditional ancestor veneration or spiritual
  causation beliefs remaining active beneath a Christian profession in
  a community you serve or know, and how would you engage that
  directly rather than assuming it has simply disappeared?
- How does the "excluded middle" concept help explain why traditional
  divination and healing practices persist even among professing
  Christians in many African contexts?
- What common ground might Zoroastrianism's expectation of final
  judgment and cosmic victory of good over evil offer for a gospel
  conversation with someone from that background, and where would you
  need to clearly present Christianity's distinctive claims?
