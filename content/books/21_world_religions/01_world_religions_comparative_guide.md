# Major World Religions: A Comparative Guide for Gospel Witness

## Overview

This framework surveys the major world religions and belief systems a
missionary is likely to encounter, organized consistently around the
questions that matter most for gospel conversation: who is God, who is
Jesus, what happens after death, how is sin and salvation understood,
and what practical approach helps a believer engage someone from that
background with the gospel. The underlying conviction is that generic
knowledge of comparative religion is not the goal — the purpose is
equipping ordinary believers to actually converse with and persuasively
reach people from each background, not merely to win debates.

## Judaism

Contemporary Judaism includes Orthodox, Conservative, and Reform
branches, ranging from strict traditional observance to significantly
liberalized practice, and in many circles Jewish identity functions
more like an ethnic and cultural inheritance than a strict set of
theological convictions — it is possible to be ethnically and
culturally Jewish while being secular, agnostic, or even atheist.
Traditional Judaism affirms one God, rejects the Trinity and Jesus'
messianic claims, looks to the Hebrew Scriptures (without the New
Testament) as authoritative, and tends to emphasize righteous living
and good deeds over a settled doctrine of assured salvation. Engaging
Jewish friends well means taking seriously their reverence for the
Hebrew Bible as common ground, while lovingly and patiently presenting
Jesus as Israel's promised Messiah using messianic prophecy they
already hold as Scripture.

## Mormonism (The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints)

Mormonism presents itself as a restoration of authentic, lost
Christianity through founder Joseph Smith's claimed revelations,
adding the Book of Mormon, Doctrine and Covenants, and Pearl of Great
Price alongside the Bible as scripture. Mormon theology teaches that
God the Father has a physical body and was once a man who progressed
to godhood, that faithful Mormons may themselves progress toward
godhood in eternity, and that Jesus is a created spirit-being (the
literal spirit-brother of Lucifer in traditional Mormon teaching)
rather than the eternal, uncreated second person of the Trinity.
Salvation in Mormon theology depends significantly on grace combined
with ongoing obedience and temple ordinances rather than being secured
by faith in Christ's finished work alone. Effective engagement
typically focuses on the character of God (one eternal, uncreated
Being versus an exalted, once-human Father), the person of Christ (his
eternal deity versus a created status), and the sufficiency of grace
versus a system requiring ongoing works for full salvation.

## Jehovah's Witnesses

Jehovah's Witnesses, organized under the Watch Tower Bible and Tract
Society, deny the Trinity, teach that Jesus was created by Jehovah as
the archangel Michael before his earthly life (not eternally
pre-existent God), and use their own translation of Scripture (the New
World Translation) with renderings that support these distinctive
doctrines, notably at John 1:1. Only a limited number (144,000) are
taught to reign with Christ in heaven, while the great majority of
faithful Witnesses hope for eternal life on a restored, paradise earth
rather than heaven itself. Effective engagement typically centers on
the full deity and eternality of Christ (John 1:1 and related texts in
their original context and grammar) and the sufficiency of Christ's
atoning work rather than an organization's ongoing mediating authority.

## Islam

Islam affirms strict monotheism (tawhid) built around the Five
Pillars (declaration of faith, prayer, almsgiving, fasting during
Ramadan, and pilgrimage to Mecca), holds the Quran as God's final,
verbally dictated revelation through the prophet Muhammad, and honors
Jesus (Isa) as a significant prophet and the virgin-born Messiah while
firmly denying his deity, sonship, crucifixion, and any need for
atonement, since Islam teaches that God forgives sin directly through
repentance and good works rather than substitutionary sacrifice.
Heaven and hell are understood in vivid, literal terms, with final
destiny significantly dependent on the balance of a person's good and
bad deeds. Effective engagement often finds common ground in shared
reverence for Jesus as a significant, virgin-born prophet, then
carefully unpacks what the Quran and Hadith actually say about him,
while addressing the honor-shame and assurance-of-salvation concerns
that are often more immediately felt than abstract doctrinal debate.

## Roman Catholicism

Roman Catholicism shares the core Christian affirmations of the
Trinity and Christ's deity with Protestant Christianity, while
differing significantly on the mechanism and certainty of salvation —
historically teaching that justification involves an ongoing process
combining grace, faith, the sacraments (especially baptism, penance,
and the Eucharist), and personal merit rather than being secured
instantaneously and fully by faith in Christ's finished work alone.
Catholic tradition and church teaching (magisterium) function
alongside Scripture as authoritative, purgatory is taught as a place of
purification for those who die imperfectly sanctified, and Mary and
the saints hold a significant place in devotional life and
intercession. Engaging Catholic friends well means affirming shared
core Christian convictions honestly while gently exploring the
certainty (or uncertainty) of present assurance of salvation, and
pointing to the sufficiency of Christ's finished work as the sole
ground of acceptance before God.

## Hinduism

Hinduism is not a single unified system but a broad family of
traditions sharing common concepts: karma (the moral law of cause and
effect across lifetimes), samsara (the cycle of reincarnation), and
moksha (liberation from that cycle), typically pursued through
disciplined religious practice, devotion to one or more of many
deities, or philosophical realization of unity with the divine
(Brahman). Sin is generally understood less as rebellion against a
personal, holy God and more as accumulated karmic debt worked off
through the soul's own effort across many lifetimes, and the concept
of Jesus, where acknowledged, is typically absorbed as one of many
legitimate divine incarnations or enlightened teachers rather than the
unique, exclusive Savior of Christian conviction. Effective engagement
often addresses the exhausting uncertainty karma offers (there is
never assurance that enough has been done) contrasted with the
finished, gracious assurance of the gospel, and directly but
respectfully addresses Christ's unique, exclusive claims rather than
allowing him to be absorbed as merely one incarnation among many.

## Buddhism

Buddhism, arising from Siddhartha Gautama's teaching, centers on the
Four Noble Truths (life inherently involves suffering; suffering
arises from craving and attachment; suffering can cease; the Eightfold
Path leads to that cessation) and the Eightfold Path of right
understanding, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort,
mindfulness, and concentration. Classical Buddhism is often
non-theistic or agnostic about a creator God, treats the self and
soul as ultimately illusory or impermanent, and aims at nirvana — the
extinguishing of craving and the cycle of rebirth — through disciplined
personal effort rather than reliance on a savior or external grace.
Effective engagement often centers on the reality and seriousness of
suffering (common ground with the Buddhist diagnosis), the question of
whether an ultimately impersonal universe can ground real moral meaning
and lasting hope, and the good news that release from suffering and
guilt is offered as a gift through Christ rather than achieved solely
through disciplined self-effort.

## Atheism

Atheism denies the existence of any god, typically grounding morality,
meaning, and origins in purely naturalistic explanations rather than
divine revelation or purpose. Common atheist objections include the
problem of evil and suffering, perceived conflicts between science and
faith, and skepticism about the reliability of Scripture and religious
experience generally. Effective engagement typically requires patient,
non-defensive conversation addressing the actual philosophical and
evidential objections raised, exploring whether a purely naturalistic
worldview can consistently ground objective morality and meaning, and
bearing honest witness to both the reasonableness of Christian faith
and its personal, transformative reality.

## Nominal or "False Convert" Christianity

A significant population identifies as Christian by upbringing,
culture, or infrequent church attendance without evidence of genuine
regeneration or transformed life — sometimes termed "nominal" or
"false convert" Christianity. This population often assumes they
already possess saving faith based on cultural identity, a past
decision, or church membership, making them, in some respects, more
difficult to reach than someone who openly identifies with another
religion altogether, since they believe the gospel does not apply to
them personally. Effective engagement typically involves examining
what the person actually believes happened at conversion, gently
testing whether repentance and a transformed life have accompanied
their profession, and clearly presenting the biblical gospel rather
than assuming shared understanding based on a shared cultural label.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

Across every tradition surveyed, the most effective engagement
consistently follows a similar pattern: find genuine common ground
without minimizing real differences, identify precisely where that
tradition's view of God, Christ, sin, and salvation diverges from
biblical teaching, and address the underlying felt need (assurance,
liberation from guilt, certainty of acceptance) the gospel answers
uniquely. Missionaries preparing to engage any of these backgrounds
benefit from knowing not just what a tradition officially teaches, but
what its ordinary adherents actually feel is at stake spiritually,
since effective witness addresses both.

## Discussion prompts for training

- Which of these religious backgrounds is most common in your current
  ministry context, and which specific doctrinal difference (nature of
  God, person of Christ, basis of salvation) would you prioritize
  addressing first?
- How would you find genuine common ground with a Muslim, Jewish, or
  Hindu friend without implying that all paths are equally valid?
- Why might a nominal or "false convert" Christian be, in some ways, a
  more difficult person to reach with the gospel than someone from an
  entirely different religion?
