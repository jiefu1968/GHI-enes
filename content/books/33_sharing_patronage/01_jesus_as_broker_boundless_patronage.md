# Jesus as Broker: The Patron-Broker-Client Triad, and the Boundlessness of Divine Patronage

## Beyond the simple patron-client pair: the broker
Patronage in the ancient world was often not a simple two-party relationship. A **broker** (the classical term was *mediator*) was a patron who provided access to *another*, greater patron who actually held the resource being sought — connecting a lower-status client to a higher-status source of favor they could never approach directly. Brokerage was itself considered a highly valuable benefit in its own right: without the right connections, a client would simply never have access to what they needed, regardless of merit or need.

A vivid New Testament example: the centurion in Luke 7 who seeks healing for his servant does not approach Jesus directly — as a Gentile outsider, he instead calls on the Jewish elders he has already benefited (by funding their synagogue) to broker access to Jesus on his behalf, trusting that their shared community ties with Jesus make success more likely. The elders, in turn, are motivated to broker this favor because the centurion has already been *their* benefactor — brokerage runs on the same reciprocity logic as patronage itself.

## Jesus cast as the ultimate Broker of access to God
The New Testament repeatedly casts Jesus not merely as a gift from God but specifically in the *role* of broker or mediator — the one who connects otherwise-inaccessible people to God the Father, the ultimate Patron:
- "No one comes to the Father except through me" (John 14:6) and "no one knows the Father except the Son" (Matt 11:27) place Jesus in the classic broker position: the sole channel of access to a greater patron.
- **"There is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus"** (1 Tim 2:5) uses the technical broker/mediator language explicitly.
- Hebrews develops this most fully: Jesus's priesthood succeeds where "neither angels nor Moses nor generations of Levitical priests" could — opening direct access to God's "throne of favor" (Heb 4:14-16) by removing the obstacle (sin) that stood in the way (Heb 10:1-14). The Latin word for priest, *pontifex*, literally means "bridge-maker" — underscoring the inherently mediating, brokering nature of the priestly role that Jesus fulfills perfectly and permanently.

## Disciples as brokers too — but with a crucial correction
Jesus extends the brokering role to his disciples, sending them out with his own authority to heal and teach (continuing his benefaction after the ascension, e.g., Acts 4:9-10's healing attributed to Jesus's ongoing "beneficence"). But Jesus deliberately corrects the disciples' natural instinct to use this brokering role for status-building — early on, they act as "gatekeepers," regulating and jealously guarding access to Jesus (Mark 9:38-39, 10:13-14), exactly as a status-conscious broker would in the ordinary patronage system. Jesus's correction is pointed: true kingdom brokerage does not exist to build up the broker's own power or prestige through networking with the influential — instead, he elevates "the weak, the little ones" as brokers too (Matt 18:5; Mark 9:37; Luke 9:46-48), inverting the normal logic where only the well-connected become valuable brokers.

**Missiological application**: this is a direct, pointed caution for any missionary who finds themselves in a brokering position (connecting local believers to outside resources, networks, or opportunities) — the temptation to build personal importance or a client network through controlling access mirrors exactly what Jesus corrected in his own disciples, and the biblical corrective is to broker access in a way that empowers "the little ones" rather than accumulating personal status or a network of the indebted.

## The boundlessness of divine patronage — a key theological and apologetic contrast
Human patronage in the ancient world operated under conditions of genuine scarcity — a patron's power came partly from the ability to *refuse*, and the finite nature of any human patron's beneficence made clients compete intensely for limited favor. This reflects what social-scientific critics call the "limited good" mentality: since resources (including honor) were perceived as inherently scarce, one person's gain was implicitly another's loss.

Scripture draws an explicit and striking contrast: unlike a human patron who must ration limited favor, **God is proclaimed as the giver of boundless benefits to whomever asks** — "Ask, and it will be given to you... For everyone who asks receives" (Luke 11:9-13), and "if any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God, who gives generously to all without reproach" (James 1:5). This is a genuine point of good news distinct from the ordinary patronage logic a patronage-culture hearer already understands intuitively: God's favor is not a zero-sum resource requiring anxious competition for a patron's limited attention — it is offered without the scarcity, jockeying, and risk of refusal that characterizes every human patron relationship.
