# Incarnational Communication: The Gospel as a Person, Not Just a Message

## Overview

This file synthesizes *Issues in Contextualization*, a missiological
work centered on two closely related concerns the author names
"communication" and "expression" — how the gospel is actually
understood by a hearer, and how it is authentically lived out
("expressed") within a receiving culture, using Jesus' own incarnation
as the controlling model for both. The book grounds its argument for
Insider Movement methodology directly in this incarnational pattern,
arguing that Jesus himself modeled what an "insider" approach to
communicating God looks like.

## The Halfway Bridge: A Governing Metaphor

The book opens with a memorable image: a real bridge the author once
saw, built only halfway across a river in 1934, without a proper plan
ever being made to complete the connection to the opposite bank — a
bridge good, in the author's dry observation, only for suicide, since
no one could actually use it to reach the other side. This image
becomes a governing metaphor for a pervasive failure in missionary
(and, the author adds, much ordinary pulpit) communication: speakers
routinely communicate only halfway across the "communicational
bridge," using their own language, concepts, and what the author
calls "theologese," and then simply expect their hearers to construct
the remaining half of the bridge themselves in order to understand
what has been said.

The burden this places on hearers is illustrated concretely: seminary-trained
listeners, already fluent in a preacher's specialized
vocabulary, may indeed be able to complete their half of the bridge
and follow along — but ordinary hearers, lacking that same technical
formation, may have no idea what has actually been communicated, even
though every individual word was, in a narrow linguistic sense,
"understood." The problem compounds further on the mission field: a
missionary who preached effectively at home using illustrations drawn
entirely from their own cultural context (the book recounts a
prominent American pastor speaking, through an interpreter, to an
all-Japanese audience using only American illustrations) may lose
nearly the entire audience except for a small minority with enough
personal exposure to the speaker's home culture to bridge the gap
themselves. In cross-cultural communication, the book warns, it is
possible not only for a communicator's words to fail to land, but for
their very manner of life to actively hinder effective presentation of
their message.

## The Gospel Is a Person, Not Primarily a Set of Words

The book's central theological move is a deliberate re-reading of John
1:1. The familiar translation, "In the beginning was the Word,"
easily misleads readers into a static, word-centered concept of how
God communicates — the book approvingly cites J. B. Phillips's more
dynamic rendering, "At the beginning, God expressed Himself," as
better capturing the active, personal character of divine
communication. On this reading, God's supreme communicative act was
not the transmission of a verbal message alone, but the "wrapping" of
that message in an actual person — a real human being who himself
*is* the message from God, not merely its messenger. Jesus, in this
framework, both showed the message and modeled the method
simultaneously: the message is a Person; the method is incarnation
itself, understood specifically as a strategy of *insider*
communication.

The book pushes this insight further than a merely doctrinal point
about the incarnation: Jesus did not simply arrive among humanity as
an external visitor but *became* an insider so thoroughly that many of
his contemporaries did not even register his significant presence
among them, or noticed him but declined to take him seriously, or
actively discounted him for speaking a socially non-prestigious
dialect (Galilean Aramaic, rather than the more prestigious Judean
form or classical Hebrew). Communication theory's observation that
"the communicator is the major part of his or her message" finds, in
this reading, its supreme confirmation in God's own chosen method: not
a message parachuted in from outside a culture, but a message
embodied by someone who became, at real personal cost, a genuine
insider within it.

## Insider Movements as an Extension of the Incarnational Pattern

The book explicitly frames Insider Movement methodology as a direct
extension of this incarnational logic rather than a novel or
theologically risky innovation: an insider movement, on this account,
begins with incarnational communication (a missionary or a
believer becoming a genuine cultural insider, following Jesus' own
pattern) and continues as an insider activity, expressed through
forms the receiving culture itself recognizes as authentic, all done
specifically to honor Jesus rather than to dilute commitment to him.
The book's stated priority, captured in the phrase "a focus on faith,
not religion," is that incarnational, insider-oriented movements
should center on the substance of trusting and following Jesus,
rather than requiring converts to adopt an entirely new external
religious system and its associated social and cultural markers as a
precondition of authentic faith.

## Missiological Reflection

The halfway-bridge metaphor and the "gospel is a person" theological
argument together supply this whole module's clearest, most memorable
statement of contextualization's basic rationale: communication that
stops at the midpoint of the river, technically correct but
practically unreachable by its intended audience, fails the same
incarnational standard Jesus himself met by becoming a genuine
insider rather than remaining a message delivered from a safe cultural
distance. Read alongside this module's other files — Hiebert's caution
about forms too tightly bound to non-Christian meaning, and the C1-C6
spectrum's more granular map of Muslim-context contextualization
choices — this incarnational framework supplies the deeper theological
"why" underneath the more technical "how" question those other
sources address: contextualization is warranted not merely because it
tends to work better pragmatically, but because it follows the pattern
God himself chose for his own definitive act of communication.
