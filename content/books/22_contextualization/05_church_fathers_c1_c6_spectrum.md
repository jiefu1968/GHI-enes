# An Evangelical Approach in Light of the Church Fathers, and the C1-C6 Spectrum

## Overview

This file synthesizes material from a scholarly monograph titled
*Contextualization of the Gospel: Towards an Evangelical Approach in
the Light of Scripture and the Church Fathers*, which engages
extensively with the work of Moreau, Flemming, Kevin Higgins, and Rick
Brown to build an evangelical framework for evaluating
contextualization proposals — including sustained attention to how
the early church fathers themselves navigated analogous questions, and
detailed engagement with John Travis's widely used C1-C6 spectrum for
describing degrees of contextualization among Muslim-background
believers.

## Why Look to the Church Fathers?

The book's distinctive contribution is bringing the historical witness
of the church fathers into direct conversation with contemporary
contextualization debates that are more often argued using only
modern missiological or biblical-theological categories. Very few
existing treatments of biblical precedent for contextualization move
beyond a preliminary level of engagement with this patristic material,
making sustained attention to how early Christian leaders handled
comparable challenges — particularly the ongoing presence of
Judaizing practice among early Gentile and Jewish-Christian
congregations — a genuinely distinctive contribution. One example
explored is John Chrysostom's fourth-century homilies confronting
Antiochene congregation members who continued participating in Jewish
festival practices; whatever one concludes about the specific
rhetorical tone of these sermons (a matter of real scholarly debate,
with some readers judging Chrysostom's language harsher than
comparable New Testament precedents), the underlying pastoral question
he faced — how much continuity with a previous religious community's
practices can coexist with authentic Christian identity — is
structurally similar to contemporary debates about Insider Movements
among Muslim-background believers today.

A separate, related strand of patristic engagement worth noting: the
book identifies Edward Rommen's later work *Come and See* (2013) as a
notable example of sustained engagement between contextualization
theory and the church fathers, written from Rommen's own theological
journey — he had co-authored the (evangelical) 1989 edition of
*Contextualization: Meanings, Methods, and Models* before later moving
toward Eastern Orthodoxy, at which point he revisited contextualization
explicitly through the lens of church tradition, a dimension he
concluded his earlier evangelical work had underdeveloped. Rommen's
later book proposes several principles drawn from Eastern Orthodox
practice: that the gospel is fundamentally a person (not primarily a
set of propositions) to be introduced, that the church itself is the
focal point of evangelistic activity, and that the sacraments function
as the privileged locus of Christ's ongoing presence in the world —
a rather different set of contextualization priorities than most
evangelical treatments emphasize, offered as a corrective rather than
a wholesale alternative.

## The C1-C6 Spectrum

Among the most widely used practical tools in contemporary
contextualization debate — especially concerning Muslim evangelism —
is missiologist John Travis's C1-C6 spectrum, describing a range of
possible identities and worship forms available to Christ-centered
communities within Muslim contexts:

- **C1-C3** represent forms with comparatively little disagreement in
the missiological literature over their acceptability — ranging from
traditional churches using a language distinct from the local Muslim
community's own, through churches using the local language but with
few local cultural forms, to churches incorporating more local
cultural (but not distinctly Islamic) forms.
- **C4** describes contextualized, Christ-centered communities that
adopt insider language and biblically permissible cultural and
Islamic forms — for example, avoiding pork, raising hands in prayer,
or using Islamic dress and terminology. Members of C4 communities
often describe themselves as "followers of Isa the Messiah" and are
typically not regarded as Muslims by the surrounding Muslim
community, though at most they may be seen as a deviant form of
Islam. C4 is broadly, though not universally, considered acceptable
by missiologists — some regard even this level as potentially
over-contextualized.
- **C5** describes communities of believers who follow Jesus while
remaining culturally and officially Muslim, viewed as Muslims by
their own community, meeting regularly with fellow C5 believers,
evangelizing other Muslims, and using extensive Islamic forms in
their gatherings — with some C5 believers continuing to attend mosque
worship. C5, along with C6, is judged by a significant number of
missiologists to be over-contextualized, risking genuine syncretism.
- **C6** describes secret or "underground" believers who make no
public identification with Christ at all, for reasons of severe
persecution risk.

## Ongoing Debate Around the Spectrum

The book documents real, continuing disagreement over C4 and
especially C5: Phil Parshall has argued forcefully that C5
contextualization carries serious theological "danger," while other
practitioners (including missiologist Kevin Higgins, closely
associated with Insider Movement theology) have defended forms of C5
practice as biblically legitimate — arguing, for instance, from the
example of the "devoted" believers described in Acts who continued
some Jewish temple observance even after coming to faith in Jesus.
Notably, the book reports that many practitioners, including Travis
himself, have since begun moving away from treating the C-scale as an
adequate descriptive tool altogether, or have started drawing a firmer
distinction between C5 specifically and the broader phenomenon of
Insider Movements more generally — a sign that even the spectrum's own
originator recognizes its limitations as debate has matured.

## Missiological Reflection

The C1-C6 spectrum's enduring usefulness lies less in providing a
settled verdict on which level of contextualization is always correct
than in giving missiologists and practitioners a shared, precise
vocabulary for locating where a specific ministry approach actually
sits — a genuine service to a debate that easily collapses into vague
mutual accusations of either "compromise" or "cultural imperialism"
without such a shared reference framework. Read alongside the church
fathers material, the book's overall argument is that contemporary
contextualization debates, however novel their specific missiological
vocabulary (the C-scale itself dates only to the 1990s), are grappling
with a genuinely ancient question the church has faced since its
first Jewish-Gentile controversies: how much continuity with a
convert's prior religious community and practice is compatible with
authentic new life in Christ.
