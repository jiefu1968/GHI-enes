# Critical Contextualization and the Form-Meaning Debate

## Overview

This file synthesizes one of the most consequential theoretical
disputes in contextualization studies: the disagreement between
Charles Kraft and Paul Hiebert over how cultural forms relate to
meaning, and Hiebert's own influential alternative proposal, "critical
contextualization." It draws on both Moreau's *Contextualization:
Meanings, Methods, and Models* and the more specialized treatment of
this debate found in scholarship on the Insider Movement controversy,
where the form-meaning question becomes practically decisive.

## Kraft's Position: Forms as Relatively Neutral Vessels

Building on his dynamic-equivalence model (covered in a companion file
in this module), Kraft's position, in its strongest form, treats
cultural forms as comparatively neutral vessels that can be filled
with new, Christian meaning through the contextualization process — a
ritual, a word, a garment, or a social practice does not carry a fixed,
unchangeable meaning attached to it permanently; meaning can be
renegotiated as a form is repurposed for Christian use. This position
gives contextualizers considerable freedom to adopt local cultural and
even religious forms, provided they attach appropriately Christian
meaning to them.

## Hiebert's Critique: Forms Are Not Empty Vessels

Hiebert and other critics have challenged this as too simplistic and
too radical a separation of form from meaning. Haleblian's often-cited
objection is that form and meaning cannot "easily be separated like
oil and water" — forms are not always neutral, empty vessels waiting
to be filled with whatever meaning a contextualizer intends. Hiebert's
own, more carefully worked-out position holds that meaning attached to
a given form is created and controlled by the dominant groups within
a society, and that it is unrealistic to expect a form "with a deep
history within a context" to be fully stripped of its existing
meaning and given a new Christian one — especially where a dominant
group continues actively to enforce the form's traditional meaning
through real social pressure. Some forms, Hiebert further argues, are
not merely culturally specific but intrinsically oppressive or evil,
and cannot be redeemed for Christian use through relabeling alone.

Between these two positions sits a spectrum of other proposed
solutions — from Sherwood Lingenfelter's considerably more restrictive
view that forms borrowed from other faiths are never neutral but
rather inherently sinful, to more nuanced accounts holding that no
single, uniform relationship between form and meaning applies across
every case. Hiebert's own resolution is the most widely accepted
among these mediating positions: he places cultural forms along a
spectrum, from those with only a loose or arbitrary connection to
their traditional meaning (which can usually be contextualized and
given new Christian meaning without great difficulty or risk) to those
with a tight, deeply entrenched connection to a non-Christian meaning
(which resist successful contextualization and are likely, if
attempted, to produce misunderstanding or outright syncretism rather
than genuine communication).

## Critical Contextualization: A Four-Step Process

Hiebert's constructive proposal — critical contextualization — offers
a disciplined process for evaluating exactly this kind of case,
rather than deciding a priori whether cultural forms in general are
safe or dangerous to adopt. The model requires, first, a thorough,
respectful *description* of the local cultural form under
consideration — understanding what a practice actually means and does
within its own cultural logic before evaluating it, echoing the
phenomenological "understand before you judge" principle found
elsewhere in missiological method. This first step is precisely where
Hiebert's spectrum comes into play: determining, empirically and
carefully, how tight or loose the existing link is between this
particular form and its traditional meaning is what allows a
missionary or local church to judge whether a given form is a
promising candidate for contextualization or a poor one.

Subsequent steps in Hiebert's process involve the local Christian
community itself studying relevant Scripture in dialogue with the
cultural form in question, and then reaching a *communal* — not
merely missionary-imposed — decision about whether and how the form
can be incorporated into Christian practice, retained temporarily as a
transitional accommodation, or rejected outright. This communal,
Scripture-saturated deliberation process is itself part of what
distinguishes "critical" contextualization from either naive
wholesale adoption of local forms or equally naive wholesale rejection
of them.

## Why This Debate Matters Practically: The Insider Movement Case

This form-meaning disagreement is not merely academic; it sits at the
center of real, high-stakes missiological decisions — most visibly in
debates over Insider Movements among Muslim-background believers
(covered in depth in a companion file), where practitioners must
decide whether specific Islamic worship forms (posture in prayer,
specific Arabic religious vocabulary, participation in mosque
attendance, fasting during Ramadan) can be filled with new, genuinely
Christian meaning, or whether their historical and social entanglement
with Islamic theology is too tight to permit this. A Kraft-leaning
practitioner is more likely to judge a wide range of such forms
suitable for reuse; a Hiebert-leaning practitioner is more likely to
subject each form to careful scrutiny of how tightly bound it remains
to its original meaning within the specific community in question,
and to expect that some forms — however attractive as bridges to
belief — cannot be safely repurposed.

## Missiological Reflection

Neither Kraft's nor Hiebert's position, taken to an extreme, serves
the church well: an uncritical assumption that any form can be
safely repurposed risks real syncretism, while a blanket suspicion of
all local forms risks the opposite failure — an unnecessarily foreign,
poorly communicated gospel that Kraft's whole project was designed to
avoid. Hiebert's critical contextualization process offers a
disciplined middle path: neither assuming neutrality nor assuming
danger in advance, but requiring careful, communal, Scripture-informed
discernment case by case — a process this module's companion file on
Insider Movements shows being applied, with considerable
disagreement, to some of the most contested contextualization
decisions facing the contemporary missions movement.
