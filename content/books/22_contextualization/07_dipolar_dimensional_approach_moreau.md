# The Dipolar and Dimensional Approach to Contextualizing the Faith

## Overview

This file synthesizes the organizing framework of A. Scott Moreau's
*Contextualizing the Faith: A Holistic Approach*, built around two
complementary ideas: contextualization's essentially "dipolar" nature
(a universal, normative pole held in continual dialogue with a local,
particular pole), and a "dimensional" approach borrowed from
religious-studies scholar Ninian Smart's seven dimensions of religion,
adapted here as a comprehensive checklist for what contextualizing the
Christian faith actually involves.

## The Dipolar Nature of Contextualization

Moreau frames contextualization through an image drawn from Revelation
7: a vast multitude "from every nation, tribe, people and language,"
dressed identically in white robes, holding palm branches, uniting in
a single declaration of praise. This image supplies a governing
metaphor with two poles held in continual dialogue: one pole is the
*commonality* — the identical robes, branches, and shared declaration
— and the other is the *individuality* — the distinct nations, tribes,
peoples, and languages the multitude is drawn from. Contextualization,
in Moreau's account, is best understood as the continual, necessary
conversation between these two poles: one universal and transcendent,
one local and immanent; one eternal, one limited to a particular time
and place. Crucially, the two poles do not carry equal theological
weight — the normative, universal pole takes priority, while the
local pole is the necessary soil in which that universal content must
become embodied and expressed.

**The universal or normative pole** is, for Moreau, the gospel itself
— a content he compresses into the deliberately brief formula "Jesus
the Christ is Lord," then carefully unpacks term by term: *Jesus* as
the actual historical, incarnate person, uniquely and ontologically
God's Son (not merely adopted, as believers are); *the Christ* as his
proper title (Messiah, Savior) rather than, as popular usage often
mistakenly assumes, simply his family name; and *is Lord* as the
declaration of his unrestricted reign over the entire created order,
a reign whose final, universal acknowledgment is history's destined
end point.

**The local pole**, by contrast, is the endlessly varied concrete
setting in which believers actually live out this confession — whether
a secret house fellowship in a country hostile to open Christian
practice or an ultramodern megachurch sanctuary in South Korea.
Christians are, in Moreau's phrase, "inescapably embedded" in some
local setting, whether that setting is their culture of birth or an
adopted diaspora context; this pole is inherently dynamic and
ever-changing, in contrast to the fixed, eternal character of the
universal pole it serves to express — Moreau's own extended metaphor
describes it as the soil into which the universal pole must be
planted to grow into an actual, livable faith.

## The Dimensional Approach: Borrowing from Ninian Smart

Moreau's second organizing move addresses a genuinely difficult
question: if contextualization potentially touches every conceivable
way believers express their faith, how can that sprawling scope be
organized into something usable? His solution borrows religious
studies scholar Ninian Smart's seven-dimensional framework for
describing religions in general — not originally designed with
contextualization specifically in mind, but repurposed here as a
comprehensive checklist ensuring no major dimension of lived faith
gets overlooked:

1. **The doctrinal/philosophical dimension** — a faith community's
formal beliefs and theological reasoning.
2. **The ritual dimension** — its structured worship practices,
sacraments, and ceremonies.
3. **The mythic/narrative dimension** — the stories a community tells
about itself and its origins, including how it retells the biblical
story in its own idiom.
4. **The experiential and emotional dimension** — the felt, lived
texture of faith, including religious emotion and personal
spiritual experience.
5. **The ethical and legal dimension** — the moral norms and codes of
conduct a faith community holds its members to.
6. **The social dimension** — the systems of association, kinship,
economic exchange, and social organization within which a community's
faith is embedded (a dimension large enough that Moreau devotes
several chapters just to its component systems).
7. **The material dimension** — the physical, tangible expressions of
faith: buildings, art, objects, and other material culture.

## Why the Social Dimension Gets Special Attention

Moreau treats the social dimension as unusually large and consequential,
splitting its treatment across several chapters covering distinct
systems: *association and kinship* (how people form groups, including
and excluding others, and how kinship networks — expanded through
marriage and economic life — shape church growth and organization in
ways contextualization must reckon with); and *exchange/economics*
(the multiple forms of capital, physical and otherwise, that every
society produces and circulates, and how Christian community life
intersects with these existing economic systems rather than existing
in some separate, purely spiritual sphere untouched by them).

## Missiological Reflection

Moreau's dipolar-plus-dimensional framework offers Christian workers a
disciplined way to avoid two common, opposite failures: treating
contextualization as if it only concerned surface-level cultural style
(music, dress, greetings — arguably only the material and some ritual
dimensions), while neglecting deeper dimensions like social structure,
economic exchange, or a community's own narrative self-understanding;
or, conversely, treating contextualization as boundlessly expansive
and therefore practically unmanageable. Working systematically through
Smart's seven dimensions, applied always in continual dialogue with
the fixed, universal content of "Jesus the Christ is Lord," gives
missionaries and local church leaders a genuinely comprehensive, yet
usable, framework for asking what a fully contextualized expression of
Christian faith would need to include in their own specific setting —
a question this module's other files, on Insider Movements and on the
church fathers, show being answered very differently across different
historical and contemporary contexts.
