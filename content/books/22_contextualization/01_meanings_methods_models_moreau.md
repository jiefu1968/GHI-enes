# Contextualization: Origins, Meanings, and the Theological Continuum

## Overview

This file synthesizes the historical and definitional foundations of
"contextualization" as a missiological term, drawing primarily on A.
Scott Moreau's *Contextualization: Meanings, Methods, and Models*
(originally co-authored with David J. Hesselgrave and Edward Rommen in
its earlier edition). It covers where the term came from, why
evangelicals were initially suspicious of it, the theological
continuum underlying different definitions, and Charles Kraft's
influential dynamic-equivalence model — one of the most widely cited
frameworks in the whole field.

## Where the Word Came From

"Contextualization" is a comparatively recent addition to missiological
vocabulary. It made its public debut through *Ministry in Context*, a
1972 publication of the Theological Education Fund (TEF), a body
launched by the International Missionary Council in 1957-58 and later
absorbed into the World Council of Churches' Division of World Mission
and Evangelism. The TEF's mandate evolved across three phases: an
initial "advance" mandate (extending funding, textbooks, and library
resources to Majority World theological schools), a "re-think" mandate
aimed at fostering "a real encounter between the student and the
Gospel in terms of his own forms of thought and culture," and finally
a "reform" mandate (1970-77) — the period in which the term
*contextualization* itself was coined to name this whole project of
adapting theological education to local context.

## Why Evangelicals Were Initially Wary

Because contextualization originated within the conciliar,
ecumenical wing of the World Council of Churches rather than
evangelical circles, many evangelicals initially distrusted the term,
fearing it implied excessive concession to culture at the expense of
biblical authority — a fear of syncretism or doctrinal compromise.
Over subsequent decades, evangelical missiologists increasingly
adopted and redefined the term for their own purposes, while an
ongoing internal evangelical debate has continued about which specific
methods and models of contextualization are legitimate — a debate
sharpened considerably by the more recent application of
contextualization principles to Muslim evangelism through Insider
Movements (covered in a companion file in this module).

## The Theological Continuum Behind Different Definitions

Moreau's central diagnostic insight is that definitions of
contextualization differ so widely largely because they rest on
different underlying theological orientations regarding how divine
revelation and human culture relate. Every definition involves both
supracultural/divine and cultural/human elements, but the *proportion*
of weight given to each varies enormously — the closer a theological
orientation sits to classical orthodoxy, the more weight it gives to
biblical revelation as the controlling factor; the closer it sits to
classical theological liberalism, the more weight it gives to human
reason and cultural experience. This single continuum explains why
some approaches to contextualization risk producing what critics call
a "new gospel" (excessive concession to context), while others aim
only to enhance the communication of the "old gospel" (contextualizing
form while preserving content).

**Apostolic contextualization**, associated with definitions from
Bruce J. Nicholls and George W. Peters, exemplifies the more
conservative end of this continuum. Nicholls defines contextualization
as "the translation of the unchanging content of the Gospel of the
Kingdom into verbal form meaningful to the peoples in their separate
cultures." Peters's related but more restrictive definition holds that
contextualization means discovering "the legitimate implications of
the Gospel in a given situation" — going deeper than mere application,
since implications are "demanded by a proper exegesis of the text"
rather than optional. Both scholars affirm that Scripture, though given
through real human cultural and linguistic forms (from Joel to John),
carries a supracultural divine message that contextualization serves
to communicate rather than to alter.

## Charles Kraft's Dynamic-Equivalence Model

Among the most influential contextualization frameworks is Charles H.
Kraft's, developed most fully in his landmark *Christianity in
Culture* (1979) and built directly on Eugene Nida's dynamic-equivalence
theory of Bible translation. Where formal, word-for-word translation
focuses on surface-level linguistic correspondence between source and
target languages, dynamic equivalence aims for the effect and response
a message produces in its receptors — Nida described this as the
"closest natural equivalence" to the source message, weighing
closeness, naturalness, and equivalence together. Kraft extends this
translation paradigm into a general model for *all* contextualization
activity, arguably placing even more emphasis on response and effect
than Nida himself — a shift substantial enough that Moreau suggests
Kraft's approach might more precisely be termed an "impact
translation" model.

Kraft applies this dynamic-equivalence paradigm specifically to the
task of biblical hermeneutics, diagnosing two especially common
interpretive errors. First, interpreters habitually read Scripture
through the lens of their own cultural conditioning, forgetting that
the biblical text itself arrived through Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek
cultural forms whose assumptions are often left unstated in the text —
Kraft's example is the Gospel of John's account of the Samaritan woman
at the well, which never explains the cultural significance of her
being there at midday or of needing to fetch her "husband" before
responding to Jesus, assumptions a modern Western reader easily misses
entirely. Left unaddressed, this error risks either flattening the
text into a purely Western monocultural reading or, taken to its
logical extreme in scholars like Bultmann, motivating a wholesale
"demythologizing" of Scripture that strips out its supernatural
content as merely a cultural husk. Second, interpreters wrongly assume
a text has one single, self-evident "plain meaning," failing to
reckon with how deeply meaning depends on the interpreter's own
cultural vantage point.

## Missiological Reflection

This continuum framework matters practically because it gives
Christian workers a diagnostic tool for locating any given
contextualization proposal — their own or someone else's — along a
spectrum from excessive concession to cultural context (risking a
different gospel) to excessive resistance to it (risking
incomprehensibility). Kraft's dynamic-equivalence model, meanwhile,
reframes contextualization's central task away from merely finding
correct words and toward securing genuine understanding and
appropriate response in the receiving culture — a shift with direct
implications for how missionaries prepare sermons, translate
Scripture, and evaluate whether a message has actually been
communicated, not merely transmitted.
