# Contextualization in Acts and Paul's Letters

## Overview

This file synthesizes Dean Flemming's *Contextualization in the New
Testament: Patterns for Theology and Mission*, focusing on the book's
treatment of Acts (particularly Paul's Areopagus address) and Paul's
letters as sustained, deliberate acts of contextualization — not
incidental features of the New Testament text, but a central pattern
for how the earliest church did theology and mission at all.

## The Areopagus Speech: Contextualization Under Pressure

Paul's address to the Areopagus in Athens (Acts 17:16-34) is among
the most studied biblical case studies in the entire contextualization
literature, and for good reason: it shows a missionary adapting his
approach in real time to an audience unlike any he had faced before.
Luke's narrative deliberately foregrounds this adaptation — he almost
entirely ignores Paul's synagogue ministry in Athens, choosing instead
to concentrate on Paul's encounter with the city's pagan
inhabitants, whom he approaches not through the synagogue but through
the *agora* (marketplace), debating publicly like a Greek philosopher
on the intellectuals' own turf.

Two philosophical schools dominate the scene: the Epicureans and the
Stoics, whose beliefs (especially Stoic ones) Paul directly engages in
his address. Luke describes their initial reaction to Paul's public
preaching as a mixture of contempt, confusion, and faddish curiosity —
a difficult audience by any measure. The accusation that Paul was
introducing "foreign deities" into the Greek pantheon likely rests on
a specific misunderstanding: hearing him speak of "Jesus" and
"Anastasis" (Resurrection, grammatically feminine in Greek), some
listeners apparently assumed he was proclaiming a divine couple — a
telling illustration of how easily a contextualizing communicator's
message can be misheard through the framework of the audience's own
prior categories. Paul is ultimately summoned to explain himself
before the Areopagus itself, the supreme governing council of Athens
responsible for religious questions — meaning his address carries
real civic and religious stakes, not merely academic interest.

Paul's actual speech models several deliberate contextualizing moves:
he opens by finding a genuine point of contact in the Athenians' own
religious landscape (traditionally, an altar "to an unknown god"),
argues from premises Stoic philosophy itself would recognize (that
God, as Creator, "does not live in temples built by human hands" and
"is not far from any one of us"), and even quotes Greek poets
approvingly before pivoting to the specifically Christian and,
crucially, unassimilable claims his message ultimately requires —
God's call to repentance and the certainty of judgment through the
man he raised from the dead. The speech thus illustrates contextualization's
essential double movement: genuine, respectful engagement with the
hearers' own categories and prior beliefs, combined with an equally
genuine refusal to dissolve the gospel's distinctive and, to Greek
ears, offensive claims (bodily resurrection, in particular, meets a
mixed reception at the speech's close) into something more
comfortably assimilable.

## Paul's Letters: Doing Theology in Context, Not Just Applying It

Flemming's broader thesis extends beyond Acts to Paul's letters
themselves, arguing that Paul does not simply take a fixed,
pre-formed theology and apply it uniformly to different situations;
rather, he does theology *in* context, letting the specific pastoral,
cultural, and social pressures facing each congregation shape how he
articulates the gospel for them. This reframes contextualization not
as a secondary, practical add-on to "real" (context-free) theology,
but as constitutive of how New Testament theology itself was actually
produced — a significant methodological claim with direct
implications for how contemporary theologians and missionaries should
understand their own task of articulating the gospel for a specific
audience.

## Case Study: Colossians as a Response to Syncretism

Flemming's treatment of Colossians offers what may be the New
Testament's most direct case study in a church's own struggle with
syncretism — the very risk contextualization, done poorly, always
threatens. The "philosophy" Paul confronts in Colossians 2:8-23 has
resisted confident scholarly identification with any single known
religious movement (early Gnosticism, Greek philosophy, or Jewish
mysticism have all been proposed, none fully persuasively); Flemming
follows the more cautious consensus view that the Colossian problem is
better understood as a kind of syncretistic amalgam drawing on
multiple religious ingredients at once. This is unsurprising given the
Lycus Valley's position as a genuine crossroads of cultures in western
Asia Minor — indigenous Phrygian religion already blended with Greek
and Roman influences, a significant Jewish minority added further
complexity, and the wider Hellenistic religious culture was
characteristically open to experimenting with combining different
beliefs and practices.

Paul's response to this threat, in Flemming's reading, is deliberately
twofold: negatively, he directly confronts the syncretistic teaching
and names the danger of submitting to it; positively, he does not stop
at mere negation but offers the Colossian church a fresh, positive
contextualization of the gospel suited to their specific new life
setting — reasserting what is genuinely central to the gospel message
in terms that directly answer the specific syncretistic pressures
they face, rather than issuing only a generic doctrinal correction
disconnected from their actual situation. Flemming treats this pairing
— naming syncretism clearly while offering a positive, contextually
apt alternative rather than only a negative warning — as a template
for how the contemporary church might respond to its own syncretistic
pressures.

## Missiological Reflection

Read together, the Areopagus speech and the Colossians case study
model the two poles contextualization must hold together
simultaneously: genuine, respectful engagement with a specific
audience's own cultural and religious categories (the Areopagus
speech's method) and vigilant, theologically substantive resistance to
syncretism when a specific context's pressures threaten to reshape
the gospel's content rather than merely its form of expression (the
Colossians case study). Flemming's overarching argument is that these
are not two separate biblical patterns in tension with each other, but
a single, integrated apostolic practice — the New Testament writers
did not see contextualizing communication and guarding against
syncretism as opposing tasks, but as two necessary dimensions of the
same missional-theological task.
