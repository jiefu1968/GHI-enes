# Contextualizing the Gospel Story and Confronting Empire in Revelation

## Overview

This file continues the synthesis of Dean Flemming's *Contextualization
in the New Testament*, covering how the four Gospel writers each
contextualize the story of Jesus for a distinct target audience, and
how the book of Revelation contextualizes the gospel as a direct,
coded confrontation with imperial Rome.

## The Gospels: One Story, Four Contextualized Tellings

Flemming's treatment of the Gospels rests on a simple but
consequential observation: the church did not receive one single,
uniform account of Jesus' life and teaching, but four distinct
Gospels, each written for a particular audience and shaped by that
audience's specific concerns, questions, and cultural vantage point.
This plurality is not a problem for the New Testament's coherence but
itself a paradigm for contextualization: each evangelist selects,
arranges, and frames the same essential story — Jesus' identity,
teaching, death, and resurrection — differently, in order to
communicate it faithfully and effectively to a specific target
audience, rather than producing one generic, context-free biography
intended for everyone equally.

Matthew's Gospel, for example, is widely read as addressed
significantly to a Jewish or Jewish-Christian audience, saturated with
Old Testament fulfillment citations and framing Jesus against the
backdrop of Israel's own story and expectations. Luke's Gospel, by
contrast, shows greater attentiveness to a wider Greco-Roman audience
and to social themes (the poor, women, outsiders) that would resonate
differently within the Roman Empire's own social landscape. John's
Gospel develops a more explicitly theological and philosophical
register (beginning with the Logos concept in its prologue) suited to
engaging a Hellenistic intellectual environment already accustomed to
discourse about a rational principle underlying the universe. In each
case, the contextualizing choice is not a compromise of the story's
truth but a deliberate strategy for making that same truth land with
its intended hearers.

## Revelation: Contextualizing the Gospel Against Empire

Flemming's treatment of Revelation addresses a very different kind of
contextual pressure than the Gospels or Paul's letters: not primarily
a communication gap to be bridged through cultural translation, but a
direct, high-stakes political and religious confrontation with the
Roman imperial cult and the pressures it placed on churches in Asia
Minor to compromise with emperor worship and the wider economic and
social system built around it. Revelation contextualizes the gospel
for this specific situation not by adapting its content to make it
more palatable to imperial power, but by using the coded, symbolic,
apocalyptic literary conventions already familiar to its audience to
expose Rome's imperial claims as a counterfeit, ultimately doomed
rival to Christ's own kingship — communicating a message of
resistance and hope in exactly the literary idiom that would carry
that message safely and effectively to a persecuted, minority
audience under real political pressure.

This case is a valuable corrective to any assumption that
contextualization always looks like accommodation or gentle bridge-building.
Sometimes the situation itself calls for contextualized
*resistance* — communicating the gospel's countercultural claims in
locally intelligible form precisely in order to strengthen a
community's resolve against assimilation to the dominant culture's
religious and political system, rather than to ease the church's
path into greater cultural comfort.

## Missiological Reflection

Together, the Gospels' fourfold contextualized retelling of a single
story and Revelation's contextualized resistance literature
demonstrate the range of what contextualization can mean depending on
a community's actual situation: sometimes the task is building
comprehension across a real cultural gap (as with the Gospels'
differing target audiences), and sometimes it is fortifying a
community's faithfulness against active pressure to assimilate to a
dominant, competing power (as with Revelation's stance toward Rome).
Both remain genuinely contextual, in Flemming's sense, because both
carefully calibrate the form and strategy of gospel communication to
the concrete situation of a specific audience — neither delivers a
one-size-fits-all message indifferent to who is actually listening and
what pressures they actually face.
