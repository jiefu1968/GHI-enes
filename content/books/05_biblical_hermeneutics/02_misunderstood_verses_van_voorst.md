# Commonly Misunderstood Verses: Reading Beloved Texts in Context

## Overview

This file synthesizes Robert E. Van Voorst's *Commonly Misunderstood
Verses of the Bible: What They Really Mean* (Wipf & Stock), which
walks through two dozen of the Bible's most frequently misapplied
verses, showing how careful attention to literary and historical
context corrects popular but mistaken readings. This file focuses on
two of the book's clearest case studies — Jeremiah 29:11 and the "eye
for an eye" law in Exodus 21 — as concrete illustrations of the
interpretive principles this whole module addresses more abstractly
elsewhere.

## Jeremiah 29:11: A Promise to Exiles, Not a Personal Life Verse

Jeremiah 29:11 ("I know the plans I have for you... plans to prosper
you and not to harm you, plans to give you hope and a future") ranks,
by some measures, as the second most frequently accessed single Bible
verse online, trailing only John 3:16 — a beloved verse appearing on
posters, plaques, refrigerator magnets, and in contemporary Christian
songs, and used to open the widely circulated "Four Spiritual Laws"
evangelistic tract. Its popular use treats the verse as a personal,
individual promise: God has a specific, good plan for *my* life, my
career decision, my finances, my present difficulty.

Van Voorst shows this popular application to be a serious
misreading once the verse's actual historical setting is taken into
account. The passage addresses Judean exiles recently deported to
Babylon after Jerusalem's conquest in 586 BCE — people in a genuine
crisis of faith (echoed in Psalm 137:4, "How can we sing the Lord's
song in a strange land?"). Jeremiah 29 specifically confronts false
prophets among the exiles who promised a swift, two-year return home
— a hopeful but false message God explicitly rejects through Jeremiah.
The real message of chapter 29 is considerably harder than the
popular verse alone suggests: the exile will last a full seventy
years (a complete human lifespan, per Psalm 90:10), meaning most of
the actual exiles addressed would not live to see the promised
restoration themselves — the "you" who eventually experiences God's
good plan is primarily their descendants, not the initial hearers.
Jeremiah's accompanying counsel — settle in for the long term, marry,
raise children, "seek the welfare of the city" of Babylon (v. 7) —
underscores that the passage offers hope for a covenant *people* across
a specific, extended historical process, not a guarantee that any
individual reader's immediate personal circumstances will resolve
favorably on their own timeline.

## "An Eye for an Eye": A Limit on Vengeance, Not a License for It

The lex talionis formula in Exodus 21 ("eye for eye, tooth for tooth")
carries a strongly negative popular reputation — invoked as a
byword for primitive vengeance (Gandhi's famous line, "an eye for an
eye makes the whole world blind," reflects this common association),
and dismissed by many contemporary Christians as an Old Testament
relic Jesus explicitly overturned in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew
5:38-42).

Van Voorst argues this popular reading gets the law's original
function backward. Ancient Near Eastern societies, including
Israelite society, were commonly organized around honor and the
practice of the blood feud — a cycle of escalating retaliatory
violence that could persist across generations once triggered. Read in
this light, the lex talionis functions as a *limitation* on violence,
not a license for it: it caps retribution at strict proportionality
(an eye, not a life, for an eye) rather than permitting the
open-ended escalation a feuding culture would otherwise default to. The
fuller passage (Exodus 21:22-27) reinforces this protective, limiting
function even further by extending its logic to protect the most
vulnerable: when a master injures a slave's eye or tooth, the
prescribed remedy is not equivalent physical injury to the master but
the slave's outright emancipation — a remedy considerably more
generous toward the vulnerable party than a literal, mechanical
application of "eye for eye" would produce. Read this way, the text
that modern readers reflexively associate with harsh, mechanical
vengeance turns out, in its original context, to function as an early
legal mechanism for restraining the far harsher default of
uncontrolled retaliatory violence.

## Missiological Reflection

Both case studies model the same basic interpretive discipline:
recovering a verse's original historical and literary setting before
applying it, rather than extracting a free-floating spiritual
principle from an isolated sentence. This has direct missiological
weight, since beloved, decontextualized verses like Jeremiah 29:11
often travel first — through greeting cards, songs, and social media —
ahead of the fuller passages that actually explain them, meaning
Christian workers frequently encounter these verses already deeply
embedded in a convert's or seeker's prior devotional life, requiring
patient re-teaching rather than a first, neutral introduction. Handled
well, as both of Van Voorst's case studies show, restoring a text's
actual context does not diminish its power — Jeremiah 29:11's real
message about God's faithfulness across a multi-generational, costly
waiting period is, if anything, more theologically substantial than
the individualized comfort-verse version, and the lex talionis's
actual function as an early check on unrestrained vengeance offers a
richer picture of divine concern for justice than the "primitive
barbarism" caricature allows.
