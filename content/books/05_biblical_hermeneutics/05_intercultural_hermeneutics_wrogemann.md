# Intercultural Hermeneutics: Reading Across Cultural Difference

## Overview

This file synthesizes Henning Wrogemann's *Intercultural
Hermeneutics* (Intercultural Theology, Volume One), a major work of
German missiology that extends biblical hermeneutics into the wider
task of understanding — and rightly interpreting — human meaning
across genuine cultural difference. Where this module's other files
focus on interpreting the biblical text itself, Wrogemann's book
addresses the equally necessary companion task: interpreting the
lived religious and cultural realities Christian workers actually
encounter in the field, using the same disciplined, humble attention
to context that sound biblical exegesis requires.

## Opening Case Study: Pastor Mastai in Dar es Salaam

Wrogemann opens his entire book not with theory but with sustained,
careful ethnographic description: a visit to the Kimara Lutheran
Parish in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania, where Reverend Willy Samuel Mastai,
a young pastor, spends an entire day receiving a steady stream of
visitors seeking pastoral care, healing prayer, and exorcism. Wrogemann
narrates the scene in careful, unhurried, non-judgmental detail — the
building's architecture, the visitors waiting for hours, a
middle-class woman receiving prayer for healing, a young man wanting
only a blessing, an older woman the pastor recognizes as a repeat
visitor apparently believed to be afflicted by a spiritual affliction.

Wrogemann's point in dwelling so long on pure description, before
introducing any interpretive framework, is itself methodologically
significant: an outside observer's first responsibility is patient,
careful attention to what is actually happening in a given cultural
and religious setting, resisting the temptation to immediately
categorize, judge, or theologize about it. The book goes on to
present this same scene from multiple angles — questions a European
observer might critically ask about Pastor Mastai's ministry, and,
turning the lens around, questions Pastor Mastai himself might ask
when he in turn experiences German culture — modeling a genuinely
reciprocal, two-directional hermeneutical encounter rather than a
one-way Western analysis of an African "other."

## Hermeneutics as a Theory of Signs

Wrogemann grounds his approach to intercultural hermeneutics in
semiotics — the theory of signs — arguing that genuine cross-cultural
understanding depends on grasping how a given culture's symbols,
rituals, and everyday behaviors function as meaningful signs within
that culture's own semiotic system, not as self-evident actions whose
meaning any outside observer can simply read off their surface
appearance. His book's stated central hypothesis is that
"understanding is only possible when the interdependency between the
concept of understanding and the understanding of culture becomes
clear" — that is, one cannot separate the general philosophical
question of what it means to understand anything at all from the
specific, concrete question of understanding a particular culture's
own meaning-making systems; the two inquiries are inseparable.

## A Fivefold Structure

Wrogemann organizes the book's larger argument around addressing, in
turn: semiotics itself, the concept of understanding, the category of
"the other" (how one culture perceives and constructs a different
culture as other), the phenomenon of religious symbols specifically,
the concept of culture, and the symbolic language embedded in
everyday cultural coherencies. Later sections extend this theoretical
base into concrete case studies of contextual theology across the
African continent — deliberately limiting the geographic scope to
allow sustained depth rather than superficially surveying many
continents at once — examining, in turn, contextual theologies from
African mainline church theologians, contextual theological concepts
developed by African women, contextual theologies among African
evangelicals, and grassroots-level African contextualization. A
recurring theme across all of these case studies is Wrogemann's
insistence that a given contextual theology is shaped not simply by
its African setting in the abstract, but by the specific, situated
discourse position of whoever is actually formulating it — meaning
"African theology" is never a single, uniform thing, any more than
"Western theology" is.

## Historical Models of Missionary Understanding

Wrogemann further traces several distinct historical models Christian
missions have used, often for centuries at a time, to understand the
relationship between gospel, culture, and the peoples missionaries
encountered: a colonial model (exemplified in early Latin American
mission history) that largely rejected or was indifferent to
indigenous culture; an individual-conversion model (illustrated
through the Moravian Brethren's missions) focused on personal
transformation somewhat apart from the surrounding culture; a model
treating the missionary encounter as fundamentally about "ennobling"
a supposedly deficient foreign culture; an indigenization model
seeking to preserve and build on existing cultural forms; and an
appropriation model in which the receiving culture itself is
understood as a genuinely significant, active contributor to the
whole process of cultural and theological encounter, not merely a
passive recipient of a message formed elsewhere. Wrogemann's own
sympathies clearly lie with this last model, which alone treats
cultural difference as a potential resource for deeper theological
understanding rather than merely an obstacle to be managed, rejected,
or corrected.

## Missiological Reflection

Wrogemann's intercultural hermeneutics functions as a direct extension
of the interpretive discipline this whole module cultivates for the
biblical text into the equally demanding task of interpreting living
human cultures and their religious practices. His opening refusal to
rush past careful description straight to judgment or theological
categorization — modeled at length in the Pastor Mastai case study —
mirrors the "observation before interpretation" discipline covered in
this module's file on inductive Bible study, applied now to people and
practices rather than texts. And his fivefold historical survey of
missionary models (colonial, individual-conversion, ennoblement,
indigenization, appropriation) gives Christian workers a vocabulary
for recognizing which largely unexamined inherited model might be
shaping their own instinctive reactions to unfamiliar cultural and
religious practices they encounter in the field.
