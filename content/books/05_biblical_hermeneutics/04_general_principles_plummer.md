# General Principles for Interpreting the Bible

## Overview

This file synthesizes selected material from Robert L. Plummer's *40
Questions About Interpreting the Bible* (Kregel Academic), focusing on
his general interpretive principles — beginning deliberately with
prayer rather than technique — and his survey of major historical and
contemporary approaches to biblical interpretation, including
grammatical-historical exegesis, canonical criticism, and the
canonical process approach.

## Approach the Bible in Prayer

Plummer insists that sound biblical interpretation, while it can be
described and taught through principles, is ultimately "better caught
than taught" — modeled through sustained exposure to skilled
interpreters rather than mastered as a purely technical checklist. His
first and most foundational principle is neither a linguistic nor a
historical rule but a posture: approaching the Bible in prayer. Citing
Jeremiah 17:9 (the human heart's capacity for self-deception) and
Romans 1:18-23 (humanity's default tendency to suppress even natural
revelation), Plummer argues that sin distorts every human faculty
involved in interpretation — emotions, will, and reason alike — such
that even believers indwelt by the Spirit must remain alert to their
own capacity for self-deceived misreading.

Plummer holds up Psalm 119 (traditionally attributed to David) as a
model of this posture, citing the psalm's repeated prayers for divine
insight and guidance rather than confident reliance on the psalmist's
own unaided understanding — verses asking God to "teach me your
decrees," "open my eyes that I may see wonderful things in your law,"
and "give me understanding." Plummer further cites Martin Luther's
own commentary on this same psalm, in which Luther observes that David
repeatedly asks God to "teach," "instruct," and "lead" him despite
already knowing the text of Moses and other Scripture well —
precisely so as not to "seize upon them pell-mell with his reason and
become his own teacher," a self-sufficient posture Luther warns
breeds "factious spirits" who treat Scripture as though it were merely
human literature requiring no prayer or Spirit-dependence to
understand rightly.

## Major Approaches to Biblical Interpretation

Plummer surveys several significant historical and contemporary
approaches interpreters have taken to relating a text's original,
historically situated meaning to its ongoing theological significance
for the church today:

**Grammatical-historical interpretation**, associated with the
influential eighteenth-century biblical scholar Johann Philipp
Gabler, seeks first to establish what a text meant in its own
historical and linguistic setting, on the theory that systematic or
dogmatic theologians would then take up biblical theology's
conclusions and articulate their contemporary significance as a
distinct, subsequent task. Plummer notes that many modern scholars
lament the resulting separation — and at times outright competition —
between biblical and systematic theology this division has tended to
produce, a divide the broader "theological interpretation of
Scripture" movement has more recently sought to overcome.

**Canonical criticism**, tracing back to Brevard Childs and the
so-called Yale School of literary criticism (though Childs himself
resisted the "canonical criticism" label as just one more sterile
academic method), treats the Bible's completed canon, read within the
context of confessional Christian faith, as the proper boundary within
which biblical texts and themes should be studied — deliberately
setting aside speculative questions about hypothetical earlier literary
sources or historical influences behind a text in favor of attending
to the actual, completed books as they stand within the church's
received canon. Plummer notes a genuine strength (attending to a
text's final canonical shape and its relationship to other canonical
books) alongside a real limitation critics have raised: legitimate
historical and source-critical questions can be too easily set aside
under this approach.

**The canonical process approach**, associated with Bruce Waltke,
shares canonical criticism's starting point in the completed canon but
adds an explicitly developmental dimension: a text's own original
meaning is respected, even as later biblical revelation is understood
to deepen and clarify that meaning progressively as the canon itself
expanded. Waltke's own description captures this: a text's
"intention became deeper and clearer as the parameters of the canon
were expanded" — paralleling how redemption itself unfolds
progressively across biblical history, so that a given Old Testament
text's meaning is not simply frozen at the moment of its writing but
genuinely enriched (without being contradicted) by its place within
the larger, later-completed canonical whole.

## Missiological Reflection

Plummer's insistence on beginning with prayer rather than technique
offers an important corrective for any missionary training that risks
treating hermeneutics as purely a technical skill set — word studies,
genre awareness, historical background — detached from spiritual
dependence on the same Holy Spirit who inspired the text in the first
place. His survey of grammatical-historical, canonical, and canonical-process
approaches, meanwhile, equips Christian workers to recognize
that responsible interpreters can legitimately emphasize different
(though not necessarily contradictory) starting points — a text's own
original historical setting, its place within the completed canon, or
the progressive deepening of its meaning across canonical history —
without any one approach alone exhausting everything a text has to
offer a careful, prayerful reader.
