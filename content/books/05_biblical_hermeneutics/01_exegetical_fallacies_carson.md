# Exegetical Fallacies: Common Errors in Word Study and Logic

## Overview

This file synthesizes D. A. Carson's classic *Exegetical Fallacies*
(Baker Academic), a widely used seminary textbook cataloguing the most
common errors Bible interpreters — trained and untrained alike — fall
into. This file focuses on Carson's treatment of word-study fallacies,
the category he addresses first and considers among the most
pervasive, because they are so easy to commit with good intentions and
so persuasive when repeated from a pulpit.

## Why This Study Matters

Carson opens by naming the problem's real cost: exegetical fallacies
are "painfully frequent" among precisely those whose God-given
responsibility is the faithful proclamation of Scripture. The issue is
not confined to untrained readers; seminary-trained preachers and
respected commentators commit these same errors regularly, often
because a particular fallacious move produces a memorable, spiritually
stirring sermon point — the persuasiveness of an error is no guarantee
of its truth, and a moving illustration built on faulty exegesis
still misleads a congregation about what the text actually says.

## The Root Fallacy (Etymological Fallacy)

Among the most enduring errors is the root fallacy: the assumption
that a word's meaning is fundamentally determined by its etymology —
its root or component parts — rather than by its actual use in
context. Carson's illustrative examples are memorable: preachers
routinely claim that because the Greek word for "apostle"
(apostolos) is related to the verb "to send" (apostello), the
"root meaning" of apostle must be "one who is sent" — treating
etymology as though it fixes present meaning. Carson counters with
English parallels: the word "nice" derives from the Latin *nescius*,
meaning "ignorant," yet no English speaker today, calling someone
"nice," means to imply they are ignorant. Similarly, "good-bye" derives
from a contraction of "God be with you," but the phrase's history
plays no role in its present, purely social meaning. Word meanings
change and drift; a word's history is a fact about its past, not a
reliable guide to its present sense in a given text.

Carson develops a more technical illustration from the Greek word
*hyperetes*, translated "servant" in 1 Corinthians 4:1. A once-popular
scholarly tradition, traced back to R. C. Trench, argued that because
*hyperetes* is morphologically related to a Greek verb meaning "to
row," its "basic meaning" must be "rower" — leading some
commentators to render Paul's self-description as something like "an
under-rower" or "assistant rower," implying a picture of lowly, menial
service. Carson shows this etymological chain to be linguistically
worthless: whatever the word's ancient morphological history,
*hyperetes* is simply never actually used to mean "rower" in surviving
classical literature, and in the New Testament it functions as an
ordinary word for "servant," often barely distinguishable from the
more common word *diakonos*. The lesson generalizes: what a word
*meant* at some point in its history, or what its component parts
might suggest about its origin, is simply not the same question as
what a word *means* in the passage actually being interpreted — and
conflating the two is the root fallacy's essential error.

## Semantic Anachronism

A related but distinct error, semantic anachronism, occurs when a
later use of a word is read back into an earlier text — assuming an
author intended a meaning the word had not yet acquired at the time of
writing. Carson's most memorable example concerns the English word
"dynamite," etymologically derived from the Greek *dynamis* ("power").
Preachers sometimes render Romans 1:16 ("I am not ashamed of the
gospel, for it is the [dynamite] of God unto salvation") as though
Paul were somehow anticipating or gesturing toward modern explosives —
a double error, Carson notes, compounding the root fallacy (assuming
ancient meaning is fixed by a word's modern derivative) with outright
anachronism (Paul could not possibly have had a nineteenth-century
invention in mind). Carson adds that even as a rhetorical illustration
of "great power," dynamite is a poor fit for Paul's actual point: dynamite
destroys and tears down, while the power Paul describes is
resurrection power, aimed at wholeness and the completion of
salvation — the association misleads about the very quality of divine
power it is meant to illustrate. A comparable case: noting that the
Greek word behind "cheerful" in "God loves a cheerful giver" (2
Corinthians 9:7) is *hilaron*, and concluding that God particularly
loves a "hilarious" (uproariously laughing) giver, imports a modern
English sense onto a word whose actual first-century range of meaning
was simply "cheerful" or "willing."

## Missiological Reflection

Carson's word-study fallacies matter directly for cross-cultural
Bible teaching and translation work: missionaries and Bible teachers,
often working with original-language tools and lexicons for the first
time, are especially susceptible to over-relying on etymology or
root-word analysis as a shortcut to meaning, precisely because such
analysis can feel more rigorous and "objective" than attending
carefully to how a word actually functions across its range of
contextual uses. The corrective discipline Carson models throughout is
patient attention to usage — how a specific author actually deploys a
word across a body of text — rather than reconstructed etymological
history, however linguistically interesting that history may be in its
own right.
