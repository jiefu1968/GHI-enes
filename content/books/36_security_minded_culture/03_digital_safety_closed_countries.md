# Digital Safety and Communication for Closed Countries

## Overview

Combines two direct sources: Media Impact International's field
article "Cybersecurity in Global Missions," and "Your Guide for
Communicating With Missionaries in Closed Countries," plus general
phishing-defense principles adapted for individual (non-IT-admin) use.
Together they cover both the mindset needed for digital security in
ministry, and concrete, practical rules for anyone communicating with
a missionary serving in a restricted-access country.

## The Right Mindset: A Culture of Security, Not Fear

A widely-cited MENA field survey (Media Impact International, 2017)
found that of 30 key ministries surveyed, more than half had staff or
seekers experience arrest, harassment, imprisonment, expulsion, or
worse because of cybersecurity breaches -- underscoring that this is
not a theoretical risk. Scripture rejects both extremes in response:
living in fear to the point of missing real gospel opportunity, and
dismissing cybersecurity altogether as unspiritual worry (2 Timothy
1:7 -- God has given a spirit of power, love, and self-control, not
fear). The guiding principle: *paranoia is the opposite of a true
security culture; a true security culture requires a clear head,
pragmatism, personal self-control, and dependence on God.*

Four components of that culture:

- **Culture and clarity** -- security is an ongoing process of reading
  one's environment, not a box that gets checked once. It does not
  require living in constant alertness or fear -- just clear,
  consistent thinking about real risks as they arise.
- **Pragmatism** -- the more "secure" something is made, the less
  usable it typically becomes. A "best practice" borrowed from a
  different context or threat model may do nothing to reduce the
  actual risk in front of you (e.g., encrypting a hard drive does
  nothing against a threat actor who is scanning public social media
  posts, not seizing devices). Effective security starts by mapping
  who the actual threat actors are, what they want, and how they
  operate -- not by applying generic rules.
- **Self-control** -- most digital security incidents in ministry
  contexts trace back to personal choices, especially around social
  media use, more than to sophisticated technical attacks.
- **Dependence on God** -- genuine gospel advance in high-risk regions
  may sometimes call for risk beyond what would ordinarily be
  prudent; wisdom and dependence on God, not fear or recklessness,
  should govern that decision.

### Mapping Threat Actors

A simple, reusable framework: for a given context, identify **who**
the relevant opposition actors are, **what** their actual goal is
(e.g., expelling foreign religious workers, identifying local
believers), and **how** they typically pursue it (e.g., scouring
public social media for usable evidence). Only once this is mapped
does it become clear which precautions actually reduce risk versus
which are generic "best practices" that do not apply to this
particular threat.

## Practical Rules for Communicating With a Missionary in a Closed Country

These rules apply to phone calls, letters, email, blog comments, care
packages, and social media -- anything that could be seen by a
monitoring government or hostile actor:

- Ask the missionary directly what communication guidelines apply to
  their specific country before writing.
- Never use the words missions, missionary, ministry, evangelism,
  witnessing, Bible distribution, outreach, gospel, or Jesus by name in
  open channels; avoid naming the host country's dominant religion or
  any local church by name.
- Keep explicitly Christian language and Scripture quotation light and
  occasional, not a constant feature of every message.
- Never criticize the host government or its policies in writing.
- Never name other missionaries in the country, and never identify or
  hint at who might be interested in Christianity locally.
- Avoid church return-addresses or labels on mail; never send prayer
  letters, church bulletins, Bibles, or other overtly ministry-branded
  material through the post.
- Never repost, forward, or publish a missionary's letters, emails, or
  messages online without their explicit permission.
- Get the missionary's permission before sharing their contact
  information with anyone else, and make sure that person also
  understands and follows these same guidelines.

## Application Focus

Building the habit of asking "who would see this, and what could it
cost someone else" before sending or posting anything related to a
missionary serving in a sensitive context; distinguishing genuinely
risk-reducing precautions from generic security theater by mapping
actual local threat actors; and equipping senders, families, and
supporting churches -- not just the missionary -- with the same
communication discipline, since a single careless message from a
well-meaning supporter can compromise a whole field team.
