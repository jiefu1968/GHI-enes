# Social Engineering and Human Hacking: Recognizing Manipulation

## Overview

Synthesized from several standard references in the field -- Kevin
Mitnick and William L. Simon's "The Art of Deception," Christopher
Hadnagy's "Social Engineering: The Science of Human Hacking" and
"Social Engineering: The Art of Human Hacking," Joe Gray's "Practical
Social Engineering," and Hadnagy's "Unmasking the Social Engineer" --
this framework explains how social engineers manipulate people rather
than systems, and gives missionaries the same vocabulary security
professionals use to recognize it. The core insight, from Mitnick
himself (once one of the world's most notorious social engineers): it
is almost always easier to trick a person into revealing information
than to break into a system directly. In a missions context, "the
system" is often a house church network, a list of contacts, or a
missionary's own travel pattern -- and "tricking a person" can mean an
informant posing as a genuine seeker.

## The SE Pyramid: How an Engagement Is Built

Hadnagy's "SE Pyramid" describes the stages a social engineer works
through, largest investment first:

1. **OSINT (Open Source Intelligence)** -- information gathered from
   publicly available sources: social media, news, job boards, church
   websites, prayer letters, photos with visible location data. This
   stage gets the most time of any stage, because everything after it
   depends on what is learned here.
2. **Pretext development** -- building a plausible cover story and
   identity based on what the OSINT revealed.
3. **Attack plan** -- the three W's: what is the goal, when is the best
   time, who needs to be involved or available.
4. **Attack launch** -- executing the plan, ideally from an outline
   rather than a rigid script, since real conversations are unpredictable.
5. **Reporting** -- documenting what worked, for the social engineer's
   own future use (or, for an intelligence service, for a case file).

Recognizing this sequence helps a missionary notice its early stages:
someone showing unusual familiarity with details that were never
directly shared is a sign that OSINT has already happened before the
first real conversation.

## Core Techniques and Their Definitions

- **Pretexting** -- impersonating someone or inventing a "context for
  the contact": a plausible reason for approaching the target. A
  self-described new believer showing up with unusually specific
  knowledge of a ministry's schedule, or a "researcher" asking detailed
  questions about who attends a house church, may be pretexting.
- **Phishing** -- fraudulent messages (usually email) designed to get
  a target to reveal information, open a file, or click a link.
  Untargeted phishing is sent broadly; **spear phishing** is
  researched and customized to one specific person or organization
  using OSINT, and is the leading vector in targeted attacks against
  organizations. **Whaling** is spear phishing aimed at senior
  leaders/decision-makers specifically, exploiting the trust and
  access that comes with seniority.
- **Vishing** -- the voice-call version of phishing: a call built
  around a pretext, relying on improvisation and rapport-building in
  real time, harder to pull off convincingly than email but with an
  immediate payoff for the attacker.
- **Baiting** -- leaving something enticing (a USB drive, a "leaked
  document") for a target to find and use, counting on curiosity to
  bypass caution.
- **Dumpster diving** -- physically retrieving discarded documents or
  materials to piece together information most people assume is gone
  once thrown away.
- **OSINT** itself, as a standalone risk: publicly visible social
  media posts, photos with embedded location metadata, and even
  friend/follower lists can let a hostile actor map who is connected
  to whom long before any direct contact is attempted.

## Reading People: The Human Element

Hadnagy's "Unmasking the Social Engineer" focuses on the other side of
the same coin -- how skilled social engineers read and build rapport
with targets, and by extension, how to notice when it is being done to
you. Key categories of nonverbal communication worth being attentive
to: **kinesics** (body movement, gestures, posture), **proxemics**
(use of physical distance/space), **touch**, and **facial
expressions** -- clusters of behavior (not any single gesture in
isolation) that can signal discomfort, deception, or rehearsed rather
than spontaneous responses. The practical takeaway for missionaries is
not to become suspicious of everyone, but to notice when someone's
warmth and interest feels performed rather than natural, especially
when paired with unusually persistent or specific questions.

## Why Someone Would Target a Missionary

In closed and creative-access contexts, the "why" is rarely financial.
The goal is usually identification and mapping: who is a believer, who
is a leader in a house church network, who else is connected to whom.
A single compromised contact list or a successful pretext conversation
can put an entire local network at risk -- which is why OSINT
discipline (what gets posted, tagged, or geolocated) and healthy
skepticism toward unusually well-informed new contacts matter as much
as any password policy.

## Application Focus

Helping missionaries and mentors recognize the stages and techniques
of social engineering as they might appear in ministry contexts;
building healthy verification habits (confirming a new contact's story
independently, being cautious with unsolicited specificity) without
becoming paranoid or closed off to genuine seekers; and understanding
that the same OSINT discipline that protects against social
engineering also protects the people a missionary is discipling.
