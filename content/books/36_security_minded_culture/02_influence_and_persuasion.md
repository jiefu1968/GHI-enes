# Influence and Persuasion: Cialdini's Six Principles

## Overview

Synthesized from Robert B. Cialdini's "Influence: The Psychology of
Persuasion" -- cited across nearly every social engineering text as
the psychological foundation of the field -- these six principles
explain why certain approaches reliably get people to comply with
requests, whether the requester's intent is honest or not. For
missionaries, the value is two-fold: recognizing when these levers
are being pulled by someone with bad intent (an informant, a
manipulative contact), and understanding that the same levers operate,
neutrally, in ordinary cross-cultural relationship-building and should
be used with integrity rather than exploited.

## The Six Principles

1. **Reciprocation** -- people feel a strong obligation to repay what
   has been given to them, even by someone they dislike or distrust.
   A "reciprocal concession" (asking for something large, being
   refused, then asking for something smaller) works especially well:
   the second request feels like a concession the other person
   negotiated, so they feel responsible for the agreement and follow
   through. Unsolicited favors can create pressure to reciprocate with
   information or trust that would not otherwise have been given.
2. **Commitment and consistency** -- once someone takes a small,
   voluntary stand, internal and social pressure pushes them to stay
   consistent with it, even when a much larger request follows later.
   Agreeing to something small and seemingly unconnected can make a
   later, bigger request far harder to refuse.
3. **Social proof** -- "when in doubt, follow the crowd." People judge
   a behavior as more correct in proportion to how many others appear
   to be doing it already. This can be faked (a "snowball," where no
   one individually knows anything, but everyone assumes everyone else
   does) -- worth remembering when a new contact claims "many others
   are already involved" as a reason to trust or join something
   quickly.
4. **Liking** -- people say yes more readily to those they like, and
   liking is reliably increased by physical attractiveness (via a
   "halo effect" that unconsciously attributes other good qualities to
   attractive people), similarity (shared opinions, background,
   interests), sincere or insincere compliments, and repeated
   pleasant contact. A stranger who seems to share remarkably similar
   interests or background very quickly may be doing this deliberately.
5. **Authority** -- people defer to perceived authority, often based
   only on symbols (titles, uniforms, confident language) rather than
   substance. Cialdini's two-question check before deferring to any
   authority: is this person a genuine authority in this specific
   situation, and how honest can I expect them to be given what they
   stand to gain from my compliance?
6. **Scarcity** -- opportunities feel more valuable, and more urgent
   to act on, the more limited or time-bound they appear. The fear of
   losing an opportunity motivates more strongly than the promise of
   an equivalent gain -- which is exactly why manufactured urgency
   ("this window is closing," "I need an answer today") is such a
   common manipulation tactic.

## A Note on Ethical Use

These principles are not inherently manipulative -- reciprocity,
consistency, social proof, likability, legitimate authority, and
genuine urgency are all part of how real trust and community form
across any culture. The distinction is intent and honesty: using them
to build a genuine relationship is simply good cross-cultural
relational wisdom; using them to extract information, compliance, or
access from someone under false pretenses is manipulation. Missionaries
benefit from knowing both sides -- how to build trust well, and how to
recognize when someone else is using these same levers against them or
the people they serve.

## Application Focus

Recognizing manufactured urgency, fabricated consensus, or
unsolicited favors as potential red flags rather than simply being
polite or naive about them; understanding why authority and similarity
cues are so persuasive, especially from someone claiming shared faith
or shared background; and using these same principles with integrity
when building legitimate relationships of trust in a new community.
