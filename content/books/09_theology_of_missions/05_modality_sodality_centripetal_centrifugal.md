# Structures and Direction of Mission: Modality/Sodality and Centripetal/Centrifugal Movement

Two conceptual pairs from mission theology help explain *how* mission gets organized (modality/sodality) and *which direction* it moves (centripetal/centrifugal).

## Ralph Winter's modality and sodality
Ralph Winter, a key architect of the modern frontier-missions and unreached-peoples movement, observed that throughout church history two different kinds of structures have always existed side by side, and both are legitimate and necessary:

- **Modality** — the structured, all-ages, all-stages community that everyone belongs to simply by virtue of church membership: the local congregation, the diocese, the denomination. It is inclusive by design — every believer, regardless of calling or specialization, has a place in it.
- **Sodality** — a voluntary, focused structure that people join for a specific purpose, alongside their modality membership: a mission agency, a monastic order, a parachurch ministry, a short-term team. Sodalities are typically narrower in membership (often self-selected, sometimes with specific vows or commitments) and organized around a specific task rather than general church life.

Winter's key point: tension between local churches (modality) and mission agencies (sodality) — over funding, authority, or "ownership" of mission — is not a sign that something has gone wrong; it reflects two different, biblically legitimate kinds of structure that have coexisted since at least the New Testament (the Jerusalem church as modality, Paul's missionary bands as something like an early sodality) and throughout mission history (monastic orders, then Protestant mission societies, functioning as sodalities alongside established churches).

## Centripetal and centrifugal movement
These terms describe the *direction* mission moves in different parts of the biblical story:

- **Centripetal** ("moving toward the center") — in much of the Old Testament, the nations are drawn *toward* Israel and Jerusalem (the prophetic vision of nations streaming to Zion, Isaiah 2:2-4). Israel's calling was substantially to *be* a light so compelling that others would come and see (though there are also centrifugal moments — Jonah being sent to Nineveh is a notable exception).
- **Centrifugal** ("moving out from the center") — the New Testament, especially post-resurrection, reverses the primary direction: the church is *sent out* to the nations (Acts 1:8, "to the ends of the earth") rather than primarily waiting for the nations to come in.

This shift matters for strategy: a centripetal mindset produces hospitality-and-attraction-based ministry (see Module 1, Biblical Hospitality) — building something compelling enough that outsiders are drawn in. A centrifugal mindset produces sending-and-going strategy — church planting among unreached peoples, cross-cultural missionary deployment (Module 10). Healthy mission theology holds both together rather than treating one as more "biblical" than the other; the Bible itself moves through both phases and arguably still calls for both today (a compelling, hospitable local church *and* an actively sent missionary force).
