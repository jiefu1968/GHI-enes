# Missio Dei: Mission Belongs to God First

The single most important shift in twentieth-century mission theology was the move from thinking of mission as something the church does *for* God to recognizing mission as something that originates *in* God and that the church is invited to join.

## The core claim
Mission is not primarily a church activity, program, or strategy. It is an attribute of God's own character and action in the world. The church does not "have" a mission that it then carries out; rather, God has a mission (missio Dei — "the sending of God"), and the church exists as one of the instruments of that mission, not its source.

## The Trinitarian shape
The classic formulation traces a sending pattern through the Godhead:
- The **Father sends the Son** into the world (John 20:21 — "As the Father has sent me, I am sending you").
- The **Father and the Son send the Holy Spirit** (John 14:26, 15:26; Acts 1:8; 2:1-4).
- The **Spirit-empowered church** is sent into the world, continuing a movement that began in God, not in the church's own initiative.

This means mission theology is not a sub-branch of ecclesiology (a study of what churches should do) but flows out of the doctrine of God himself — the Father is a sending Father, the Son is a sent and sending Son, the Spirit is a sent and empowering Spirit.

## Why this reframing matters practically
- It relativizes any one church, denomination, or agency's ownership of "the mission" — everyone is a participant, not a proprietor.
- It grounds motivation in God's character rather than in guilt, obligation, or human strategic cleverness — we join what God is already doing rather than persuading a reluctant God to bless our plans.
- It makes room for God's mission activity that precedes and exceeds any human missionary's arrival in a place (common grace, the witness of creation, prior movements of the Spirit) — the missionary is joining a story in progress, not starting one from zero.

## A caution
"Missio Dei" language has sometimes been stretched, particularly in mid-20th-century ecumenical theology, to blur or dissolve the specific call to gospel proclamation into a generalized doctrine of God's activity in history and culture regardless of explicit Christian witness. A biblically grounded missio Dei keeps proclamation of Christ and the formation of Christ-confessing communities central to what "joining God's mission" means (see Module 2, Essential Teachings, and Module 18, Christian Theology, for the doctrinal guardrails).
