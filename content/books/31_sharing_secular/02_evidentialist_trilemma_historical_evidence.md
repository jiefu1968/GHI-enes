# The Evidentialist Approach: The Trilemma and Historical Evidence

Alongside Keller's more narrative, worldview-level apologetic (file 01), a distinct and complementary tradition of **evidentialist apologetics** (associated with Josh McDowell's *Evidence That Demands a Verdict* and similar historical-evidential works) argues Christianity's truth claims directly from historical evidence — useful for conversation partners whose objections are specifically historical or evidential rather than existential or narrative-based.

## The Trilemma: Lord, Liar, or Lunatic
Most famously articulated by C.S. Lewis (though with earlier roots in the 19th-century preacher "Rabbi" John Duncan and Watchman Nee), the Trilemma responds directly to the common move "I accept Jesus as a great moral teacher, but not his claim to divinity" — arguing this option is not actually available given what Jesus claimed about himself.

The argument's structure: if Jesus's claims to deity were false, there are only two possibilities — he knew they were false, or he did not.
- **Liar** — if Jesus knew his claims were false, he was engaged in a deliberate, sustained deception with eternal stakes for his followers, making him not a great moral teacher but something closer to a con man of "monstrous proportions" — yet this sits awkwardly next to the near-universal recognition (even among skeptics like John Stuart Mill) that Jesus's ethical teaching is of the very highest order. It is difficult to reconcile "supreme moral teacher" with "knowing, high-stakes liar."
- **Lunatic** — if Jesus sincerely believed his own divine claims but was simply wrong, this requires a specific kind of delusion (comparable, as Lewis puts it, to "the man who says he is a poached egg") that sits uneasily beside the calm, coherent, and profound teaching the Gospels record him delivering under pressure, including at his own trial and execution.
- **Lord** — the remaining option: his claims were true.

**The point of the argument is not to prove the conclusion by elimination alone** — it depends on independently establishing that Jesus did, in fact, make the exalted claims the Gospels attribute to him (which is itself a historical question, addressed by manuscript and textual evidence below) — but it closes off the common escape route of treating Jesus as a merely respected teacher while declining his more radical claims about himself.

## Manuscript reliability of the New Testament
A frequent secular objection ("we can't trust the Bible historically") is addressed evidentially by examining:
- **Early dating** — the timing between the events described and the earliest surviving manuscripts and source material is far shorter than the timescale typically required for legendary embellishment to develop and displace eyewitness memory, especially with witnesses to the contrary still alive to challenge inventions.
- **Content that argues against legendary development** — the Gospels include details that would be highly unusual to invent (women as the first witnesses to the resurrection, in a culture where women's testimony carried little legal weight; the disciples' own cowardice and doubt prominently recorded; a crucified, publicly shamed messiah, which was a scandal rather than an obviously appealing invention within first-century Jewish messianic expectation).
- **Manuscript quantity and consistency** — compared with other ancient historical texts routinely accepted as reliable by historians, the New Testament has a substantially larger number of surviving manuscript copies and a shorter gap between the originals and the earliest copies we possess.

## Fulfilled prophecy and the resurrection as historical claims
Evidentialist apologetics also treats Old Testament messianic prophecy fulfilled in Jesus, and the resurrection itself, as claims open to historical investigation rather than purely matters of private faith — arguing (through a "minimal facts" style approach common in this tradition) that certain bare facts are widely agreed upon even among non-evangelical scholars (Jesus's death by crucifixion, the disciples' sincere belief that they encountered the risen Jesus, the rapid emergence and willingness of the disciples to suffer and die for this specific claim rather than recant), and that the resurrection remains the explanation best fitting this evidence compared to rival naturalistic theories (hallucination, theft of the body, legendary development over time).

## When to use which approach
The evidentialist approach (this file) tends to land well with conversation partners who frame their skepticism in explicitly historical or evidential terms ("how do we know the Gospels are reliable," "what's the actual evidence for the resurrection") — common among analytically-minded, STEM-educated, or academically-oriented secular people, including many Chinese international students trained in rigorous empirical disciplines. Keller's narrative/worldview approach (file 01) tends to land better with conversation partners whose resistance is more existential or cultural (a sense that religion is culturally regressive, restrictive, or simply irrelevant to a modern life) rather than specifically about historical evidence. Reading which kind of resistance is actually present in a specific conversation — evidential or existential — helps choose the more fruitful starting point, and the two approaches are not in tension; they simply answer different underlying questions.
