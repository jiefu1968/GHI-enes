# Nietzsche's Own Critique of Secular Morality, and Discovered vs. Created Meaning

Two further arguments from Keller's *Making Sense of God* deepen the "borrowed capital" theme (already covered via Tom Holland's historical thesis) with a sharper philosophical edge, and address the specific felt need for meaning rather than only the question of moral values.

## The "Ghost at the Atheist Feast": Nietzsche's own critique of secular humanism
It's tempting to think the strongest critique of secular humanism's moral confidence comes from religious apologists. In fact, one of the sharpest critiques comes from Friedrich Nietzsche himself — an atheist writing specifically *against* the secular humanism of his own day.

Nietzsche's argument: the moral package modern secular culture holds onto — the equal dignity of every person, compassion for the weak, universal human rights — historically arose from a specifically Christian metaphysical claim: that a personal God made every individual to have loving communion with him, giving every person (not just the powerful or the socially useful) inherent worth. Remove that metaphysical foundation, Nietzsche argued, and the moral conclusions built on it lose their actual grounding — they become, in Nietzsche's view, inherited habits without a foundation, "religious values" retained after "ditching divinity," in literary critic Terry Eagleton's phrase. Nietzsche predicted that a genuinely consistent secular culture would eventually find morality itself becoming "a problem" — harder and harder to justify or motivate without appeal to something beyond material nature, tending ultimately toward mere power ("whatever party is in power keeps itself there") rather than genuine moral obligation.

**Why this matters more than the historical "borrowed capital" argument alone**: Tom Holland's historical thesis (that Western secular values are traceable to Christian origins) can be met with "so what — even if it originated there, the values stand on their own now." Nietzsche's philosophical critique is sharper: he argues the values *cannot* actually stand on their own within a strictly materialist framework, because a purely natural, evolved universe supplies no standpoint from which one arrangement of matter (a compassionate society) can be called objectively better than another (a cruel one) — both are simply "part of life," in Nietzsche's phrase, with no transcendent vantage point available to rank them. Notably, Nietzsche's own answer (radical self-creation of values, the "Übermensch") is itself, on examination, still a moral vision smuggling in judgments about what is "noble" versus "base" — meaning even Nietzsche could not escape the very move he diagnosed as impossible without a transcendent foundation.

**Conversational use**: this argument works well specifically with secular humanists who hold moral convictions with genuine passion (human rights, compassion, justice) but resist any specifically religious grounding — inviting them to examine, in Nietzsche's own terms, what actually justifies those convictions within their own stated worldview, rather than simply asserting the historical Christian origin of the values as an external critique.

## Discovered meaning vs. created meaning
A second, distinct argument addresses the search for meaning in life directly, arguing that a *discovered* meaning (meaning that exists independently of you and that you find, characteristic of religious worldviews) is more rational, more communal, and more durable in practice than *created* meaning (meaning you personally construct for yourself, characteristic of secular modernity's "make your own meaning" ethos).

- **More rational**: philosopher Thomas Nagel's own analysis shows that any chain of "why does this matter" questions about a self-created meaning eventually terminates in "it doesn't, ultimately" once the entire universe's eventual end is factored in — sustaining a sense of meaning in a purely secular frame requires *not* following this chain of reasoning to its actual conclusion, which is a kind of practiced non-rationality rather than a fully examined position.
- **More communal**: philosopher Charles Taylor observes that once meaning becomes a purely private, self-created matter, society loses any shared, articulable standard for calling anything genuinely wrong (you can say an act "isn't your chosen meaning," but you lose the standing to say it is actually wrong) — self-created meaning tends toward fragmentation and an inability to mount a shared moral case for justice (contrast Martin Luther King Jr.'s explicitly theological, not self-created-meaning-based, appeal for civil rights).
- **More durable**: Viktor Frankl's Holocaust-era research found that prisoners whose sense of meaning was tied to something transcending their immediate circumstances (family, career, personal happiness) collapsed when those circumstances were destroyed, while those with meaning anchored beyond present circumstance (often religious conviction) proved more resilient — because a meaning that depends entirely on life going well is, by definition, vulnerable to life not going well.

## Conversational application
This argument doesn't require first proving Christianity's exclusive truth — it starts from a secular person's own felt experience of searching for meaning, and shows that the "make your own meaning" framework common in secular culture struggles on its own terms (rational consistency, social/moral force, and resilience under suffering) in ways a discovered, transcendent meaning does not — opening a door to ask what kind of meaning could actually meet those three tests, before moving to the specifically Christian answer.
