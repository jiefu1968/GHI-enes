# Sacraments, the Eucharist, and the Transubstantiation Debate

## The seven sacraments vs. two ordinances
The Catholic Church recognizes seven sacraments as channels of grace instituted by Christ: Baptism, Confirmation, Eucharist, Reconciliation (confession), Anointing of the Sick, Holy Orders (ordination), and Matrimony. Most evangelical traditions recognize only two — Baptism and the Lord's Supper — understanding these as ordinances Christ specifically commanded the church to observe, rather than as one of seven grace-conferring sacramental channels alongside a broader sacramental system.

## The Eucharist: three evangelical positions, not one
It's a common oversimplification to describe "the evangelical view" of the Lord's Supper as if there were only one — historically there are three distinct Protestant positions, each worth knowing when a Catholic conversation partner asks what evangelicals actually believe instead of transubstantiation:

1. **Memorial view (associated with Zwingli)** — the bread and cup remain entirely unchanged in substance; the meal is a symbolic act of remembrance and proclamation of Christ's sacrifice, not a means of grace conferring Christ's actual presence.
2. **Spiritual presence (associated with Calvin)** — the elements remain bread and wine, but Christ is truly, spiritually present in the meal by the work of the Holy Spirit — a real presence, but not a physical or substantial change in the elements themselves.
3. **Sacramental union (associated with Luther)** — Christ's body and blood are truly present "in, with, and under" the bread and wine — a strong view of real presence, though Lutherans typically avoid the term "consubstantiation" and resist over-philosophizing the exact mechanism, in contrast with the Catholic scholastic precision of transubstantiation.

## Transubstantiation itself
Catholic teaching holds that during the Eucharistic prayer, the *substance* (the underlying reality) of the bread and wine is changed into the substance of Christ's Body and Blood, while the *accidents* (the physical appearances — taste, texture, appearance) remain entirely unchanged. This draws on an Aristotelian substance/accident philosophical framework applied to the biblical texts most commonly cited in support: Christ's words of institution ("This is my body," Matt 26:26-28), the Bread of Life discourse (John 6:51-58), and Paul's account of the Supper (1 Cor 11:23-29).

## Where the real disagreement sits
The evangelical objection is not that these texts are unimportant or symbolic-only by definition — Calvin and Luther both affirmed real presence in some sense — but whether the biblical language requires or even permits a full metaphysical change of substance while the appearances remain, versus a real spiritual presence or a purely commemorative meaning. This is a case where precision matters: telling a Catholic conversation partner "evangelicals just think it's a symbol" flattens Luther's and Calvin's actual positions and misrepresents much of historic Protestant eucharistic theology.

## Justification, continued: what "faith working itself out" looks like differently
Building on Module 1's justification summary: the Eucharist itself illustrates the deeper disagreement about *ongoing* grace. In Catholic theology the Eucharist is understood as actually conferring grace that nourishes and sustains the soul's justification across a lifetime — consistent with justification as an ongoing process. In evangelical theology, the Lord's Supper nourishes faith and proclaims the gospel but does not itself confer or add to a justification already fully accomplished at the moment of faith — consistent with justification as a completed, once-for-all declaration.
