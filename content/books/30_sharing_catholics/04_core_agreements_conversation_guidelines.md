# Core Agreements and Practical Guidelines for Gospel Conversations with Catholics

## Core agreements — start here, not with the differences
Before any disagreement is discussed, it is worth naming clearly and often what evangelicals and Catholics already share, since a conversation that leads with difference tends to produce defensiveness rather than openness:
- The **Trinity** — one God in three persons
- The full **deity of Christ**
- The bodily **resurrection** of Christ
- Christ's **second coming**
- A **final judgment**
- The absolute **necessity of God's grace** for salvation

These are not minor overlaps — they are the central claims of Nicene Christianity, and a Catholic conversation partner holds them as firmly as an evangelical does. Leading with this shared ground establishes that the conversation is a family disagreement about the *mechanism* of salvation and the *source* of authority, not a dispute between two entirely different religions.

## Summary of principal differences
| Topic | Catholic | Evangelical |
|---|---|---|
| Authority | Scripture + Tradition + Magisterium | Scripture alone (*Sola Scriptura*) |
| Justification | Grace-enabled, lifelong cooperation | By grace through faith alone (*Sola Fide*) |
| Sacraments | Seven | Usually two ordinances |
| Mary | Marian dogmas held as required belief | Honored, without later dogmas |
| Saints | Intercession requested | Christ alone mediates |
| Papacy | Universal teaching authority | Not recognized |
| Purgatory | Yes | No |
| Prayer for the dead | Yes | Generally no |

## Eight practical guidelines for the actual conversation

1. **Begin with shared beliefs** (see Core Agreements above) before addressing any difference — this is not a rhetorical trick, it's simply accurate and sets a respectful tone.
2. **Represent Catholic teaching accurately** — misstating what Catholics actually believe (e.g., "Catholics worship Mary," "Catholics think they earn their way to heaven by works alone") is both unloving and ineffective, since an informed conversation partner will simply stop trusting the accuracy of anything else you say.
3. **Distinguish official doctrine from popular practice** — a specific person's actual beliefs and practices may differ significantly from the Catechism's official teaching in either direction; ask what *they* believe rather than assuming the textbook position.
4. **Ask questions before debating** — understanding where a specific person's confidence for salvation actually rests (their own effort, the sacraments, Christ, some combination) is more valuable up front than presenting a rehearsed argument against a position they may not even hold personally.
5. **Keep Christ at the center** — the conversation's goal is a person's relationship with Jesus Christ, not a debate victory over the Catholic Church as an institution.
6. **Avoid caricatures or disrespectful language** — toward the Catholic Church, its practices, or its people; this is both a matter of Christian charity and of basic persuasive effectiveness.
7. **Use Scripture carefully and in context** — proof-texting without context is a weak methodology regardless of which side uses it, and an informed Catholic conversation partner may know the wider context of a passage better than the evangelical citing it.
8. **Remember that individuals vary in belief and practice** — a devout, catechized Catholic, a cultural/nominal Catholic, and a Catholic influenced by charismatic renewal movements may hold quite different actual positions despite sharing the same institutional label.
