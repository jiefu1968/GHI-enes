# Gauging Where a Person Actually Is: The Spectrum of Distancing from Faith

Not every baptized Catholic who has drifted from active practice has drifted the same distance. Michael Paul Gallagher (an Irish Jesuit theologian who spent decades working with young people navigating unbelief) and researcher André Godin/Carron's studies on French-Canadian Catholicism identified a useful, more granular way of thinking about religious distancing than a simple "practicing / lapsed" binary — helpful for gauging where a specific person actually stands before deciding how to engage them.

## Eight patterns of "distancing" among baptized Catholics
Carron's research differentiates eight recognizable stages, roughly progressing from mild to complete religious disengagement:

1. **Abandonment of regular Mass and sacrament attendance** — practice stops, but identity and belief may remain largely intact.
2. **Alienation from the institutional Church** — either actively (definite objections to the Church) or passively (simply drifting with a secularizing culture).
3. **Non-belonging with the Church as a community** — ranging from passive nominal identification to a more conscious, sometimes angry, severing of ties.
4. **Fading of Christian values in actual practice** — belief no longer meaningfully shapes behavior.
5. **Collapse of the credibility of Christian meaning for life** — some rejection of Church teaching or the gospel's claims themselves.
6. **Withdrawal of belonging in faith to Jesus Christ** — the point Gallagher considers unbelief in the strict sense, not merely institutional distance.
7. **Rejection of a personal God** — either agnostic suspension of judgment or outright atheistic denial.
8. **Apathy over all religious questions** — a complete absence of interest in faith as a live possibility at all, the furthest point on the spectrum.

**Why this matters practically**: most non-practicing American Catholics encountered in ordinary life fall into stages 1-2 — lapsed in practice, but not yet at the point of active disbelief or complete apathy. This is significant, hopeful information: a person who has stopped attending Mass is very often not the same as a person who has rejected God or lost all interest in faith — the conversational approach for each is quite different, and assuming the more extreme end (stage 7 or 8) for someone actually at stage 1 or 2 can miss real openness that's still present.

## Gallagher's three groups of young people
Gallagher also observed three broad groups among young people raised Catholic, useful as a simpler working typology alongside the eight-stage spectrum:

1. **Those for whom "the bottom fell out"** of inherited faith and practice — often not through deliberate rejection but because family, Church, and school simply proved inadequate to help them find a living faith of their own; Gallagher describes them as victims of a kind of cultural dislocation rather than willful unbelievers.
2. **"Seekers and finders"** — those who found a fresh, often community-based focus for genuine faith, frequently through a small group or community pursuing real Christian commitment together.
3. **The "in-betweens"** — still nominally practicing, sometimes irregularly, having neither formally left nor genuinely entered personal conviction; an inherited tradition is held onto without being actively experienced as nourishing, leaving open the live question of what would move this person from inherited religion toward personal conviction.

## Two diagnostic insights worth carrying into any specific conversation
- **"A communication gap, not a generation gap."** Much apparent generational drift from faith is better understood as a failure of communication between inherited forms of faith and a changed cultural context, not necessarily a rejection of the faith's actual content once it's re-communicated in terms that connect.
- **"Over-sacramentalized and under-evangelized."** Gallagher's memorable diagnosis of much nominal Catholic experience: extensive exposure to sacramental practice without a corresponding depth of actual gospel proclamation and personal encounter with Christ — meaning the person in front of you may have had abundant religious *exposure* without ever having really heard or engaged the gospel's actual claim on them personally. This reframes the evangelistic task: not necessarily introducing something entirely foreign, but often naming and deepening something the person has had exposure to but never actually engaged as a personal, living reality.
