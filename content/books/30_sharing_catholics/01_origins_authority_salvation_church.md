# Catholic-Evangelical Comparison: Origins, Authority, Salvation, and Church

## Historical origins
Roman Catholicism understands itself as the direct historical continuation of the church founded by Christ, tracing an unbroken line of authority through apostolic succession (the claim that bishops today stand in an unbroken chain of ordination back to the apostles) and the teaching authority of the Church itself. Evangelical Protestantism traces its distinct identity to the 16th-century Reformation, when reformers sought to correct what they saw as doctrines and practices that had drifted from Scripture over the preceding centuries. Neither side denies a shared origin in the early church — the disagreement is about which tradition faithfully preserved and which one departed from that shared inheritance.

## Authority: the central hinge issue
This is arguably the single question from which most other differences follow:
- **Catholic**: authority rests on a three-legged stool — Scripture, Sacred Tradition, and the Magisterium (the Church's teaching office, centered on the Pope and bishops in communion with him). Scripture is not read or interpreted apart from this living tradition and teaching authority.
- **Evangelical**: *Sola Scriptura* — Scripture is the final, supreme authority. Tradition, creeds, and church history are valued and instructive, but always subordinate to and correctable by Scripture itself.

Nearly every other disagreement in this module (Mary, saints, purgatory, sacraments, papal authority) is downstream of this authority question — Catholic positions typically appeal to Tradition and Magisterial teaching as a source of binding doctrine alongside Scripture; evangelical positions typically ask whether a given teaching can be established from Scripture alone.

## Salvation and justification
- **Catholic**: salvation begins by grace (this is not a "salvation by works" system, contrary to a common evangelical caricature), but continues through faith, the sacraments, ongoing cooperation with grace, and perseverance — justification includes both forgiveness of sin and actual inner renewal, understood as a lifelong process rather than a single completed legal event.
- **Evangelical**: justification is God's declaration of righteousness credited to the believer through faith alone (*sola fide*) based on Christ's righteousness, a decisive legal declaration rather than a process — good works and sanctification follow as fruit and evidence of that justification, not as its basis or means.

**Common ground worth naming explicitly in conversation**: both traditions affirm that salvation is possible only through Jesus Christ, that grace is indispensable (no one is saved by unaided human effort), and that humanity is genuinely sinful and needs saving. The real disagreement is narrower and more precise than "grace vs. works" — it is about the *mechanism and timing* of justification: a single forensic declaration received by faith alone (evangelical) versus an ongoing, sacramentally-mediated process of grace-enabled cooperation (Catholic).

## The Church
- **Catholic**: a visible, institutionally unified Church marked by apostolic succession, with a papacy and episcopal (bishop-based) structure as constitutive of the Church's identity, not merely its organizational convenience.
- **Evangelical**: the Church is the community of believers united under Christ, expressed through a variety of legitimate governance models (congregational, presbyterian, episcopal-but-not-papal) — visible unity through a single institutional hierarchy is not considered essential to the Church's true nature.

## Practical note for gospel conversations
Distinguish carefully between **official doctrine** and **popular practice** — many baptized Catholics have limited familiarity with the Magisterium's actual teaching and practice a folk-Catholic blend shaped more by family tradition and popular devotion than catechetical instruction. Assuming either deep doctrinal knowledge or its complete absence is equally likely to misjudge the actual person in front of you.
