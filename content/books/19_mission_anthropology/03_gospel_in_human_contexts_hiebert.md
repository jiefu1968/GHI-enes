# The Gospel in Human Contexts: Anthropological Explorations for Contemporary Missions (based on Paul Hiebert's framework)

## Overview

This later framework revisits and updates missionary anthropology for
a globalized world, arguing that mission can no longer be understood as
a one-directional movement from a Western "sending" culture to a
non-Western "receiving" culture, but must be reconceived as glocal
mediation — work that happens simultaneously at global and local
levels, in a world where cultures constantly interact, blend, and
reshape one another. Missionaries, in this account, function less like
messengers delivering a fixed package and more like mediators
constantly navigating between global structures and local particularity.

## Part One: Theoretical Foundations

The opening section traces how views of contextualization itself have
changed over time — from noncontextualization (assuming the gospel
message and its Western cultural packaging are inseparable) through
minimal and uncritical contextualization (adapting form while risking
theological compromise) to critical contextualization, an approach that
takes both biblical authority and cultural particularity seriously,
evaluating each cultural practice against Scripture rather than either
rejecting or accepting a culture's forms wholesale. Missional theology
is presented as operating simultaneously at multiple levels — theology
is never done in a vacuum, but always emerges from particular cultural
and historical conditions, which raises the ongoing question of how a
single gospel message relates to genuinely diverse theological
articulations across cultures without collapsing into either
relativism or a single culturally-bound "correct" theology.

## Part Two: Exegeting Humans

Just as missionaries are trained to exegete texts, this section argues
they must also learn to exegete people — understanding not just what a
culture believes but how anthropological understanding of humans has
itself changed over time (from early, often condescending anthropology
that ranked cultures hierarchically, to more recent anthropology
attentive to cultural particularity and internal logic on its own
terms). A systems approach is proposed as a corrective to purely
reductionist analysis (explaining human behavior by breaking it into
smaller mechanical parts) — instead treating a person or culture as an
interconnected system where biological, psychological, social, and
spiritual dimensions all interact and mutually shape one another, more
like an organism than a machine. Research methods for actually studying
people well in the field are addressed practically, giving missionaries
concrete tools for the kind of sustained observation and structured
inquiry good cultural exegesis requires.

## Part Three: Mission as Intercultural Mediation

The book's most distinctive contribution reframes missionary identity
itself. "Glocal mediation" describes how missions today operates
simultaneously within global flows (economic, digital, migratory) and
intensely local particularities — a missionary is never purely local or
purely global, but constantly mediating between the two. Missionaries
as global mediators extends this further: in an interconnected world,
a missionary's role increasingly involves connecting disparate
local contexts to global networks of resources, ideas, and
relationships, rather than simply transplanting a message from one
fixed culture into another. This reflects the practical reality of a
world with diaspora communities, transnational digital communication,
and rapid urbanization, where "sending" and "receiving" cultures are
far less cleanly separated than earlier mission theory assumed.

## Application to cross-cultural and diaspora ministry

This framework speaks directly to contexts like the global Chinese
diaspora, international students, and urban migrant communities — cases
where a single "local" culture with fixed, stable boundaries barely
exists anymore, and where effective ministry increasingly requires
navigating global and local dynamics simultaneously rather than
assuming a clean, one-way movement from a sending culture to a
receiving one. Understanding critical contextualization as a middle way
between rigid non-contextualization and uncritical syncretism gives
missionaries a defensible theological method for the constant, real-time
judgment calls contextualization requires in genuinely mixed, globalized
settings.

## Discussion prompts for training

- How does viewing your own ministry context as "glocal" rather than
  purely local change how you think about your role as a missionary?
- Where do you see critical contextualization actually being practiced
  well in your context — evaluating a cultural practice against
  Scripture rather than simply accepting or rejecting it wholesale?
- What would it mean, practically, to describe yourself as a "mediator"
  connecting local believers to global resources and networks, rather
  than simply a messenger delivering a fixed message?
