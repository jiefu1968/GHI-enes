# Transforming Worldviews: How People Change at the Deepest Level (based on Paul Hiebert's framework)

## Overview

This framework — Paul Hiebert's most comprehensive treatment of the
concept, completed and published posthumously — argues that lasting
conversion and discipleship require change not just in beliefs and
behavior but in worldview: the largely unconscious, foundational
assumptions a culture makes about reality, time, causality, and the
moral order. Surface changes in belief or behavior that leave the
underlying worldview untouched tend to be shallow and reversible;
genuine transformation reaches down to this deeper level.

## Defining Worldview

Worldview is treated as operating beneath conscious awareness — the
assumed, rarely-questioned framework through which a culture perceives
reality, prior to and shaping any explicit belief system built on top
of it. Several components make up this framework: cognitive themes (how
a culture organizes and categorizes knowledge, forms categories, and
reasons — including different cultural logics of causality and
relationship), affective themes (a culture's characteristic emotional
tone and aesthetic sensibilities), and evaluative themes (a culture's
deep moral intuitions about right, wrong, and the order things ought to
have). Individual versus group orientation is treated as one of the
most consequential worldview themes: whether the self is fundamentally
understood as an autonomous individual or as inseparable from a
network of group relationships shapes nearly everything else in a
worldview, including how sin, salvation, and identity are naturally
understood.

## Category Formation, Logic, and Time

Different cultures form conceptual categories differently — what
counts as one "kind of thing" as opposed to another varies across
cultures in ways that are easy for an outsider to miss. Cultural logics
also vary: some cultures reason primarily through relational logic
(where truth is validated through relationship and trust) rather than
purely propositional logic, and causality itself is understood
differently depending on whether a culture emphasizes personal,
spiritual causation or impersonal, mechanical causation. Time is
treated as a particularly consequential worldview theme: some cultures
experience time as a straight, forward-moving line pointed toward a
future to be planned and controlled, while others experience time as
cyclical, always returning rather than progressing — a difference with
major implications for how a culture relates to tradition, ancestors,
planning, and eschatological hope.

## Levels of Culture

A layered model distinguishes levels of cultural knowledge, from the
most visible and concrete (material culture — physical objects) through
patterns of behavior, signs and symbols, rituals, myths, and belief
systems, down to worldview at the deepest and least visible level.
Change introduced only at the surface levels (adopting new material
culture or behaviors) without corresponding change at the worldview
level tends to be unstable, prone to reverting or producing syncretism,
since the deep assumptions that originally shaped the old behavior
remain intact underneath the new surface.

## Ethnosemantic Methods

Practical methods are offered for actually discovering a culture's
worldview through careful linguistic and conceptual analysis —
examining how a culture's own vocabulary organizes domains of meaning
(kinship terms, moral categories, spiritual beings) as a window into
otherwise invisible worldview assumptions. Comparative case studies
(examining, for instance, tribal, Hindu, modern Western, Islamic, and
Chinese belief systems side by side) illustrate how differently
cultures answer the same deep worldview questions: how the world
began, how humans relate to spiritual beings and forces, how land and
fertility are understood, and how a community understands its
relationship to outsiders.

## Application to Discipleship and Conversion

The book's central practical argument is that discipleship must
eventually engage a convert's worldview directly, not just their
explicit beliefs — a new believer can affirm every correct doctrine
while still operating, at the worldview level, with untransformed
assumptions about time, causality, and community that quietly distort
how that doctrine is actually lived out. Effective long-term
discipleship, in this view, requires patiently surfacing these deep
assumptions and helping a believer examine them in light of Scripture,
rather than assuming worldview change happens automatically alongside
correct belief.

## Application to cross-cultural and diaspora ministry

For missionaries working in Chinese, Hindu, Muslim, tribal, or
secular-modern contexts, this framework provides both diagnostic
categories (which worldview theme — time, group orientation, causality,
moral order — is most likely to remain unconverted even after a
profession of faith) and a method (ethnosemantic analysis of local
vocabulary and categories) for actually investigating a specific host
culture's worldview rather than assuming it in advance.

## Discussion prompts for training

- Which worldview theme (time, individual/group orientation, causality)
  do you suspect is hardest for believers in your context to actually
  transform, even after sincere conversion?
- How would surface-level behavior change (new religious practices)
  without worldview change produce instability or syncretism in a new
  believer, according to this framework?
- Choose one key term in your target language and try an ethnosemantic
  analysis — what does its place among related words reveal about
  underlying worldview assumptions?
