# Christ-Centered Biblical Counseling: Theology and Methodology (based on Kellemen & Viars's edited framework)

## Overview

This framework presents biblical counseling as flowing directly from
sound theology — the conviction that how a counselor understands God,
Scripture, humanity, and salvation necessarily shapes how they help
people navigate suffering and sin. Organized in two major movements —
a practical theology of biblical counseling and a practical
methodology for actually conducting it — the book insists counseling
technique divorced from careful theology inevitably imports whatever
theology (often secular or therapeutic) fills the vacuum left by
theological neglect.

## A Practical Theology of Biblical Counseling

Biblical counseling is grounded in specific doctrinal convictions:
Scripture's sufficiency for addressing the fundamental issues of the
soul (without claiming Scripture addresses every practical or clinical
detail a counselor might need), a robust theology of suffering and sin
that avoids collapsing every difficulty into either purely spiritual
or purely psychological categories, and a Christ-centered framework in
which the gospel itself — not merely general moral instruction — forms
the foundation for lasting change. Counseling is treated as one
expression of the church's broader disciple-making mission rather than
a specialized technical service disconnected from ordinary spiritual
formation and pastoral care.

## A Practical Methodology of Biblical Counseling

The book organizes actual counseling practice around a series of "life's
ultimate questions" a counselor helps a person work through — questions
of identity, suffering, sin and temptation, relationships, and
purpose — treating counseling as a structured process of helping
someone reinterpret their life story through the gospel rather than a
single technique applied uniformly to every problem. Later chapters
turn specifically to congregational, church-based counseling
ministry — addressing how local churches, not merely individual
credentialed counselors, can build a culture of "one another" care and
structured counseling ministry among ordinary members, extending
biblical counseling's reach well beyond formally trained specialists.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

This framework's insistence that counseling technique cannot be
theologically neutral applies directly to cross-cultural counseling
contexts: a Western therapeutic technique imported without theological
examination may carry hidden individualistic or naturalistic
assumptions foreign to both Scripture and a missionary's host culture.
Its emphasis on building church-wide counseling culture, rather than
depending solely on rare credentialed specialists, also directly suits
resource-limited or persecuted-church contexts where professional
counseling access is minimal.

## Discussion prompts for training

- How would you help a new believer reinterpret a specific painful
  life circumstance through the gospel, rather than through generic
  moral advice or secular therapeutic categories alone?
- What would it look like to build a "one another" counseling culture
  among ordinary church members in your own ministry context, rather
  than depending solely on a single trained counselor?
- Where might an imported Western counseling technique carry hidden
  theological assumptions your host culture, or Scripture itself,
  would actually challenge?
