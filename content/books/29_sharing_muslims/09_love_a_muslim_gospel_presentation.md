# Love a Muslim: Overcoming Prejudice and Presenting the Gospel of Grace

## Overview

This file synthesizes *Love a Muslim*, a two-part manual whose first
section confronts inherited Christian prejudice against Muslims and
whose second section works through how to present the Christian
gospel to a Muslim audience, including a term-by-term comparison of
key theological concepts (sin, salvation, sanctification) across the
two faiths.

## Confronting Inherited Prejudice

The book opens with an unusually direct confession: despite living in
an age of unprecedented access to knowledge, Western Christians remain
"painfully ignorant" of what Islam actually teaches, and often show
little inclination to learn — a state of affairs the author calls a
sad indictment against a church commissioned to take the gospel to
all peoples, made more urgent by immigration bringing Muslim
neighbors directly into Western communities and daily contact. The
author documents a deep-rooted intolerance toward Muslims among many
Western Christians, traceable in part to the Crusades, still visible
even in respected contemporary church history writing. As a case in
point, the author quotes (and critiques) a passage from a widely used
one-volume church history that frames Muhammad's prophetic
self-understanding as a product of a "sickly" epileptic childhood and
declares Islam the greatest enemy Christianity has ever faced — a
characterization the author holds up as an example of exactly the
kind of uncritical, prejudiced framing Christians should learn to
recognize and reject, even while maintaining genuine theological
disagreement with Islamic teaching.

## A Biblically Grounded Starting Point for Engagement

Turning to how the gospel should actually be presented to Muslims, the
author insists the first task is getting the church's own foundation
right: a settled confidence that Scripture is the authoritative,
sufficient guide for Christian thought and practice regarding
Muslims, not merely a theoretical doctrinal statement. The author is
candid that this project does not pretend to religious neutrality: it
explicitly holds that Islam, evaluated against Scripture, is a false
religion, precisely because Christian conviction holds Jesus Christ
to be God's ultimate self-revelation — citing Jesus' own claims ("I
and my Father are one," John 10:30; "I am the way, the truth, and the
life; no one comes to the Father except through me," John 14:6) and
Peter's declaration that salvation exists in no other name (Acts
4:12). The author frames this starting conviction not as license for
contempt toward Muslims as people, but as the non-negotiable
theological premise from which any genuinely Christian approach to
Islam must proceed.

## Comparing Sin, Salvation, and Sanctification

The book's most distinctive practical contribution is a structured,
concept-by-concept comparison intended to help Christians see exactly
where Islamic and Christian teaching genuinely diverge rather than
merely appearing similar on the surface:

**Sin.** For the Muslim, the author argues, sin is understood
primarily in terms of predestination and specific bad deeds weighed
in a moral ledger; for the Christian, sin is fundamentally relational
— opposition to and disobedience against the divine will, falling
short of God's own holy standard — carrying full personal
responsibility before God for both attitude and action, not merely
tallied deeds.

**Salvation.** In the Islamic framework as the author presents it,
salvation is achieved solely through submission and good works, with
final outcome entirely at God's discretion and no settled assurance
of eternal life — access to Paradise conceived largely in sensory,
this-worldly terms. The Christian understanding, by contrast, is
salvation as *recovery* of what sin has lost: redemption and rescue
accomplished not by the sinner's own effort (which the author holds
to be impossible) but by God's own initiative in Christ, satisfying
the righteous demands of his holiness through Christ's perfect
sacrifice, received by grace through faith rather than earned by
merit, and including a genuine new spiritual birth rather than merely
external rule-following.

**Sanctification.** The Muslim concept of holy living, on the
author's reading, remains oriented around obedience to external law
and the accumulation of merit through good works and religious
ritual. The Christian concept of sanctification, by contrast, is
framed as the ongoing outworking of a new inward spiritual life
already given to the believer — a process of becoming, not a
transaction for earning standing with God.

## Missiological Reflection

*Love a Muslim* models a posture some readers may find in tension:
unambiguous rejection of the prejudice and caricature that has long
colored Western Christian writing about Islam and Muhammad,
paired with an equally unambiguous theological conviction that Islam,
evaluated by Scripture, does not offer the salvation Christianity
proclaims. The book's own argument is that these two positions are
not actually in tension — that genuine love for Muslims as people,
and honest confidence in the uniqueness of the gospel, can and should
coexist, and that Christian history has too often gotten this balance
wrong in the direction of prejudice rather than of doctrinal
compromise. Readers should weigh the book's comparative theological
claims (particularly its characterization of Islamic soteriology) as
one Christian apologetic reading, useful for clarifying where
Christian doctrine actually differs from Islamic teaching, rather than
as a neutral academic description with which all Muslims or Islamic
scholars would agree.
