# A Christian Apologetic Case on Qur'anic Theology, the Trinity, and Jihad

## Overview

This file synthesizes a Christian apologetics book examining Islam "in
light of the Bible," organized around a series of comparative
doctrinal questions: the reliability of the Qur'an versus the Bible,
the nature of God in Islam versus Christianity, the Trinity, the
deity of Christ, and the concept of jihad. This material represents
one strand of Christian apologetic writing on Islam — critical of
Qur'anic and Islamic legal tradition from an evangelical Protestant
standpoint — and readers should note that Muslim scholars and many
academic Islamicists would dispute several of its specific claims,
particularly regarding abrogation and jihad, which remain genuinely
contested within both Islamic scholarship and Western academic study
of Islam. The value of this material for the curriculum lies in
showing the *kind* of case this apologetic tradition makes and the
specific Qur'anic passages and Islamic doctrines it engages, not in
presenting its conclusions as uncontested consensus.

## Framing the Central Disagreement

The book opens by naming what it takes to be an irreconcilable
theological core dispute: Christians hold the Bible to be God's Word
inspired by the Holy Spirit, while Muslims hold the Qur'an, revealed
through the angel Gabriel to Muhammad, to be God's final revelation
and the standard by which earlier revealed books — corrupted, in the
traditional Muslim view, by their followers — must be judged. The
author argues that comparing how each scripture treats Jesus, sin,
atonement, and salvation reveals genuinely contradictory claims, and
that this ideological conflict cannot simply be talked away — while
also acknowledging that when politics and fanaticism attach themselves
to either religion, they generate a vicious cycle of hatred that
forecloses the possibility of reasoned dialogue altogether.

## What Is Islam? Creed and Deed Together

Contrary to a simplification some Christians hold, the author insists
Islam is not merely a matter of external deeds without corresponding
belief content — Islamic faith combines *both* creeds (the six
fundamentals: belief in one God, in all prophets and messengers, in
all revealed scriptures, in angels, in the Day of Judgment, and in
predestination) *and* deeds (the Five Pillars: confession of faith,
prayer, fasting, pilgrimage, and almsgiving). A key theological point
the author draws from this structure: because a Muslim's eternal
fate depends on Allah's sovereign will and mercy weighed against a
record of good and bad deeds, orthodox Islam itself, by the author's
reading, lacks any settled doctrine of assurance of salvation —
producing what the author characterizes as a pervasive undercurrent
of anxiety among sincere Muslims about their ultimate standing before
God, in contrast to the assurance Christian theology locates in grace
received by faith rather than a final accounting of merit.

## The Qur'an-versus-Bible Reliability Debate

The author engages a very common feature of Muslim-Christian polemic:
the claim that the Bible has been textually corrupted, allegedly
demonstrated whenever biblical teaching about Jesus, sin, and
salvation conflicts with Qur'anic teaching. The author's
methodological counter-argument is that this reasoning simply assumes
the Qur'an's own reliability as the standard against which the Bible
is measured, without independently establishing the Qur'an's textual
reliability by external historical or documentary evidence — a
circularity the author argues does not survive ordinary standards of
textual criticism applied evenhandedly to both scriptures.

## God in Islam and the Doctrine of the Trinity

The author surveys competing scholarly theories on the etymology of
"Allah" (most simply, a contraction of the Arabic *al* and *ilah*,
"the god," with cognates across other Semitic languages) before
turning to the doctrine most commonly cited by Muslims as evidence of
Christian polytheism: the Trinity. The author argues that the Qur'an's
repeated rejection of "three gods" and of Jesus as a divine son
misunderstands the actual content of Trinitarian doctrine, which has
never held that God has a literal consort or that Christ's sonship is
physical or biological. The author points to Jesus' own citation of
the Shema ("the Lord our God is one," echoing Deuteronomy 6:4) as
evidence that Trinitarian Christianity has, from its earliest
centuries and across denominational lines, actively opposed
polytheism as heretical rather than embracing a functional
tritheism — a distinction the author argues is frequently lost in
popular-level Muslim-Christian polemic on both sides.

## The Deity of Christ

Citing Samuel Zwemer's observation that the deity and atoning work of
Christ sits at the center of most Islamic polemic against
Christianity, the author works through the Qur'anic treatment of
Jesus as one of five major prophets (alongside Noah, Abraham, Moses,
and Muhammad) and addresses the recurring Muslim objection that
calling Jesus "Son of God" implies a physical, biological relationship
between God and Mary. The author's response is that this objection
misreads the theological, not biological, sense in which Christian
doctrine has always used the title — a distinction with real
apologetic weight if a Muslim conversation partner is in fact reading
"Son of God" in the literal-biological sense the Qur'an explicitly and
repeatedly denies (e.g., "How could He have a son when He has no
consort?").

## Jihad and Contested Political Claims

The book's treatment of jihad and what it terms "the Islamic strategy
for the West" is its most contested and least academically settled
material. The author argues that classical Muslim jurists divided the
world into two realms — the abode of peace (Islamic-governed
territory) and the abode of war (the rest of the world) — and reads
specific verses of the Qur'an's ninth chapter as commanding aggressive
warfare against non-Muslims who refuse either conversion or payment of
a religious tax. It is important for readers of this curriculum to
know that this reading of jihad and of the classical doctrine of
abrogation (the idea that later, more militant Qur'anic verses
override earlier, more peaceable ones) is disputed by many Muslim
scholars and by specialists in Qur'anic studies, some of whom argue
that the classical abrogation doctrine has been selectively and
inconsistently applied throughout Islamic history for political
purposes having little to do with the Qur'an's actual internal logic.
The author's claims about a coordinated, decades-old "Islamic
strategy for the West" — drawn from a single 1974 Muslim World League
conference resolution — should likewise be read as one polemical
interpretation rather than an established historical consensus about
contemporary global Islam, which is itself vastly more religiously,
politically, and nationally diverse than any single strategic
narrative can capture.

## Missiological Reflection

This material is useful for understanding one significant and
long-standing school of Christian apologetic engagement with Islam —
one that takes doctrinal disagreement with real seriousness and
supplies specific Qur'anic citations Christian workers may encounter
raised by Muslim conversation partners. At the same time, its most
polemical claims (particularly around jihad and an alleged unified
Western strategy) should be held with appropriate caution and weighed
against the broader, more variegated realities of contemporary global
Islam described elsewhere in this module — most Muslims a Christian
worker will actually meet are shaped far more by the everyday,
practically oriented concerns of folk Islam than by the geopolitical
and legal-theological questions this apologetic tradition emphasizes.
