# Joyful Witness in the Muslim World

## Overview

This file synthesizes Evelyne A. Reisacher's *Joyful Witness in the
Muslim World* (Baker Academic), which reframes Christian witness among
Muslims around the theme of joy — a deliberate corrective to a
mission literature Reisacher found dominated by narratives of danger,
suffering, and loss. Reisacher, a French Lutheran-background scholar
of Islamic studies, draws the book's structure from her own decades of
"ordinary encounters" with Muslims, examining joyful witness across
seven distinct spheres of contemporary life: personal attachment,
cyberspace, the arts, environmental concern, care for the needy, urban
life, and direct theological conversation.

## Toward Joyful Witness

Reisacher opens by naming the problem directly: the first book she
read on mission in the Muslim world left her more discouraged than
hopeful, chronicling arrests and tragic deaths among a small handful
of Muslim-background believers in a country without an evangelical
church; friends reacted to her own desire to witness to Muslims as
though she had announced a sentence to hard labor. Intrigued by how
consistently joy was absent from mission discourse about the Muslim
world, she investigated biblical and theological writing on joy more
broadly, finding far more material than she expected — an
observation she notes even surprised the twentieth-century Swiss
theologian Karl Barth, who remarked on how frequently joy, delight,
and rejoicing appear throughout the Old and New Testaments.

## Muslim-Christian Attachment

Reisacher traces her own path into Islamic studies not to academic
interest but to an ordinary teenage friendship: a Muslim neighbor
girl, curious about Reisacher's "Jesus loves you" T-shirt, became a
genuine friend with whom she eventually read the Bible and prayed.
Reisacher is explicit that she did not experience this friendship
through a primarily religious lens — her friend was, to her, first a
neighbor and friend, only secondarily someone with a Muslim father.
She contrasts this with language she later encountered at Christian
mission conferences ("we evangelize Muslims") that she found subtly
objectifying, reducing individuals to a religious label — a caution
she carries through the whole book, since, as she puts it, people
form attachments with people, not with religious categories.

## Connecting with iMuslims

Reisacher treats cyberspace as the first practical environment for
joyful witness precisely because social media has transformed how
Muslims connect with each other and the world, overcoming earlier
barriers (traditional hospitality norms and sex segregation that once
made it difficult for outsiders to access the inner circles of
conservative Muslim communities and homes). Her vivid opening
illustration — imagining the resurrection morning reported via tweet
and hashtag, going viral despite official censorship — frames digital
space as a genuinely new mission field with its own distinct dynamics
of virality, informality, and reduced gatekeeping compared to
face-to-face cultural barriers.

## Joyful Witness through Art

Reisacher tells of Samira, the only Muslim-background follower of
Christ in a small Paris church, who confided her discouragement and
loneliness in the congregation — but also her comfort in Bible verses
rendered in elegant Arabic calligraphy hanging near the pulpit, a gift
from a visiting Lebanese pastor, which she described as her emotional
"oxygen supply" for sustaining her Christian life in that setting.
Reisacher argues that mission literature has historically
underemphasized the arts in favor of purely verbal communication, even
though art carries deep significance in both Christian faith and
Islamic culture — making it a natural, multisensory point of
connection Christians often hesitate to use.

## Thinking Green in Sharing the Gospel

Reisacher recounts a conversation with a Muslim woman equally alarmed
by environmental degradation and its likely human causes, sharing
plans to change her own habits to reduce harm to the earth — an
encounter Reisacher treats as a natural, unforced opening to share her
own theology of creation and God's ongoing relationship to the
natural world. She situates this within the broader modern
environmental movement (from the first Earth Day in 1970 to two
hundred million participants across 141 countries twenty years later)
as evidence that shared ecological concern is a genuinely widespread,
cross-religious point of contact rather than a niche interest.

## Caring for the Needy

Reisacher recounts an elderly veiled woman in Cairo who knocked on the
door of friends' home Reisacher was housesitting, asking for food
after her son's rooftop accident had cost her a day's wages as a
cleaner supporting six children alone since her husband's death.
Reisacher's simple gift of a carton of eggs, met with the woman's
tearful gratitude, becomes an occasion to reflect on the Lord's Prayer
petition for "daily bread" and on how easily consumerism obscures the
reality that many people, on any given day, genuinely lack it.
Reisacher insists that in Christian witness among Muslims, caring
deeds are not optional extras to verbal proclamation but intrinsically
carry the joy at the heart of the gospel itself.

## Meeting Muslim Urbanites

Noting that over half the world's population is now urban, with
projections of 70 percent urbanization by 2050, Reisacher surveys the
enormous diversity of Muslim-majority urban contexts she has
personally visited — bustling traditional souks in Istanbul and Xi'an
alongside high-tech Muslim professionals, Muslim "hipsters" in Tunis,
Sufi shrines and modern mosques coexisting in Delhi, extremes of
poverty and luxury in Cairo and Dubai, and pluralistic multi-faith
urban life in Beirut, Singapore, Brussels, and London. Her practical
recommendation is direct: rather than only reading about Islam,
Christians should walk the actual streets of Muslim-majority cities to
grasp this diversity firsthand.

## Theological Conversations

Reisacher closes with her own experience running a weekly Bible table
on a Paris university campus with an InterVarsity-style student group.
Most passing students showed no interest, but some, noticing Arabic-
language books on her table, assumed she was distributing Qur'ans and
were surprised to find Arabic Bibles instead — an unexpected point of
connection that led many Muslim students to stop regularly for
extended conversations about faith. Her key observation, borne out
over two years of these weekly encounters, is that Muslims are
generally not shy about discussing faith and religion directly — a
Christian worker does not need to apologize for initiating a
religious conversation, since many Muslim conversation partners will
welcome it as readily as, or more readily than, many secular Western
counterparts would.

## Missiological Reflection

Reisacher's seven spheres function collectively as an argument against
a narrow model of Muslim evangelism as verbal doctrinal debate alone.
Digital connection, artistic expression, shared environmental concern,
practical care, urban life, and direct theological conversation are
presented not as separate strategies to choose among but as
complementary, mutually reinforcing dimensions of a single sustained
posture: joyful, unhurried, relationally rooted witness that takes
seriously both the full humanity of the Muslim friend encountered and
the genuine biblical warrant for joy, even amid the real risks and
losses that mission work in the Muslim world can involve.
