# Sharing Your Faith with a Muslim: The Qur'an's Own Witness to Christ

## Overview

This file synthesizes *Sharing Your Faith with a Muslim*, a
scholarly apologetic work that builds its case for Christian witness
not primarily from the Bible outward but from the Qur'an's own
text and classical Islamic commentary inward — arguing that a careful,
unbiased reading of the Qur'an's own statements about Jesus and the
Bible actually undermines, rather than supports, the popular Muslim
claim that the Bible has been corrupted and that the Qur'an denies
Christ's divine glory. The book draws extensively on classical
Qur'anic commentators (Fakhar al-Din Razi, Ibn Khalkan, al-Ghazali)
and on older Western scholarship of Islam (W. Muir) alongside its own
argument.

## The Bible and Early Islam: A Mixed Christology in the Qur'an

The book's opening argument is that Christology — how the person and
work of Christ should be understood — sits at the very heart of the
Qur'an's engagement with Christianity, and that the Qur'an itself
contains what the author calls a "two-nature" tension in how it
depicts Jesus: certain passages point toward his divine majesty and
unique miraculous life, while others emphasize his ordinary
prophetic status alongside other messengers of God. The author's
central methodological complaint about typical Muslim-Christian
dialogue is that Muslim interlocutors tend to foreground only the
Qur'anic passages emphasizing Jesus' servant status while explaining
away the passages pointing to his greater glory — leaving, in the
author's assessment, even a fair-minded student of the Qur'an
genuinely uncertain about who the text actually holds Jesus to be.

## Does the Qur'an Actually Accuse Christians of Corrupting Scripture?

Addressing the widespread Muslim claim that Jews and Christians
corrupted their own scriptures, the author makes a precise textual
distinction: the Qur'an's accusations of scriptural tampering and
"twisting of tongues" fall specifically on certain unfaithful Jews,
while explicitly recognizing that other Jews possessed the authentic
book of Moses (Sura 7:159). Citing the nineteenth-century Islamicist
W. Muir, the author argues that no passage in the entire Qur'an casts
any suspicion whatsoever on Christians specifically tampering with
either their Gospel text or their copies of Jewish scripture — the
strongest Qur'anic charge against Christians being merely that they
had "forgotten a part" of what they were reminded of, meaning they
had fallen into doctrinal or practical error, not that their actual
scriptural text had been physically altered. The author's conclusion
is that the later, fully developed Muslim doctrine of wholesale
Bible corruption is a post-Qur'anic theological development that
does not actually follow from — and in the author's reading
positively contradicts — the Qur'an's own more limited and specific
claims.

## Muhammad's Alleged Presence in the Bible

The book also addresses the second common basis for Muslim suspicion
of Bible reliability: Qur'anic passages (notably Sura 7:157) claiming
that the coming of an illiterate prophet was already foretold in the
Torah and Gospel available to Muhammad's contemporaries. Early Muslim
scholars, following this Qur'anic cue, searched the Bible for
confirming prophecy, landing especially on Deuteronomy 18:15 (a
promised prophet "like unto" Moses), arguing Muhammad qualified as a
prophet like Moses by virtue of shared Semitic ("brotherly") descent
and by bringing a comparable law. The book treats this identification
as a considerably later interpretive overlay on the biblical text
rather than a reading that would have been recognizable to the text's
original audience — part of the broader case that traditional Muslim
claims about the Bible's content depend on interpretive moves made
centuries after the Qur'an's own composition, not on the Qur'an's
own explicit content.

## Wahi and Ilham: Two Kinds of Islamic Inspiration

Drawing on the medieval theologian al-Ghazali, the author explains a
distinction foundational to how Muslims themselves understand
revelation: *wahi*, the highest form of inspiration, delivered via a
known angelic intermediary (specifically Gabriel, in the Qur'an's own
case) directly to prophets; and *ilham*, a lesser form of inspiration
received from an unknown source in an unknown way, associated with
saints and mystics rather than prophets. Some classical Islamic
scholars, the author notes (citing Ibn Khalkan), claimed this
angel-mediated *wahi* status as a feature unique to the Qur'an among
all revealed scriptures — a claim the book's broader argument
implicitly challenges by pointing to the Qur'an's own description of
Jesus as "the Word from God," a title the author argues implies a
category of revelation considerably higher than ordinary prophetic
inspiration.

## Jesus Christ, the Word of God, According to the Qur'an Itself

The book's central textual argument turns on Sura 3:45, in which the
angel announces to Mary that God gives her good tidings of "the Word
from Himself," named Jesus, "exalted both in this world and the world
to come." The author surveys how classical Muslim commentators (such
as Fakhar al-Din Razi) have worked to minimize the force of this
title — reading "the Word from Him" as a mere honorific comparable to
calling a brave man "the essence of bravery," rather than as a
substantive claim about Jesus' unique nature — while arguing that this
minimizing interpretation strains against the passage's own plain
sense, which the author reads as far closer to the Johannine
"Word became flesh" (John 1:14) than most Muslim commentary is willing
to acknowledge.

## Missiological Reflection

This book's distinctive apologetic strategy — arguing from the
Qur'an's own text and classical Muslim commentary tradition rather
than only from the Bible — offers Christian workers a resource for
engaging Muslim conversation partners on ground the partner already
holds authoritative, potentially opening productive conversation
precisely because it does not require a Muslim interlocutor to accept
biblical authority as a starting premise. As with the more polemical
apologetic material covered elsewhere in this module, readers should
recognize that the book's specific interpretive claims about
Qur'anic Christology are contested by Muslim scholars, who have their
own long, sophisticated exegetical tradition defending the classical
readings this book argues against; the value for Christian workers
lies in understanding the specific Qur'anic texts and classical
commentary at stake, which frequently do surface in real
Muslim-Christian conversation, rather than in treating this
apologetic reading as settled fact.
