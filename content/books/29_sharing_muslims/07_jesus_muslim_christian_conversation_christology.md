# Jesus in Muslim-Christian Conversation: A Dialogue on Christology

## Overview

This file synthesizes Mark Beaumont's *Jesus in Muslim-Christian
Conversation* (Regnum/Wipf & Stock), an academic work structured as an
extended imagined dialogue between two representative
conversationalists: Ibrahim, a Sunni Sufi Muslim academic who has
studied Christianity in the Western world, and Paul, an Evangelical
Protestant who has lived in the Muslim world and taught religious
studies in the West. Beaumont, Research Associate at London School of
Theology, states that Paul reflects his own experience without being
identical to him, while Ibrahim represents a composite of Muslims he
has known rather than any single individual. The dialogue moves
chronologically through Jesus' life as narrated in both the Qur'an and
the Gospels, from conception to promised return, engaging Qur'anic
commentary, hadith, and modern biblical scholarship (including
skeptical scholars like Bultmann and Crossan) along the way.

## Born of Mary: The Virginal Conception

The conversation opens on common ground: both the Qur'an and the
Gospels affirm Jesus' conception in Mary while she was still a virgin.
Ibrahim presents the Qur'anic account (Sura 3:37-40, via the story of
Zechariah's own miraculous prayer for a son) as evidence of God's
unconstrained creative power, requiring no ordinary human process —
while also noting the Qur'an's acknowledgment that most prophets are
born through normal means. Paul highlights how legends of Jesus
speaking as an infant, found in apocryphal (non-canonical) gospel
texts, resurface within the Qur'an's own infancy narrative, raising
a historical-critical question about the Qur'an's textual sources
that Ibrahim resists but does not fully rebut. Both agree that some
contemporary Muslims and Christians alike no longer hold to a strictly
biological virginal conception without a human father, while both
also seek to defend the traditional account within their own
traditions.

## Miracle Worker

Ibrahim presents miracles (including those of Moses) as Qur'anic
"signs" pointing to God rather than to the miracle-worker's own
divine nature — a reading directly contested by Paul, who documents
that even skeptical modern biblical scholars generally accept Jesus'
historical reputation as a healer, and notes that Jesus expected his
own followers to continue healing in his name. Paul raises a
pointed textual question: given how directly the Qur'an draws on New
Testament material elsewhere, why does the Qur'an's bare mention of
Jesus' healing ministry never develop into the fuller healing
narratives found throughout the Gospels? Ibrahim, for his part, warms
to more modern Muslim approaches that emphasize Jesus primarily as a
healer, seeing this framing as a point of potential convergence
between the two traditions.

## Teacher of Love for God and Neighbor

Drawing on the 2007 "A Common Word" document signed by 158 Muslim
scholars calling on Christians to unite around shared teaching on
loving God and neighbor, Ibrahim argues the Qur'an shares this same
spirit even if it does not state the teaching as directly as the
Gospels do. Paul presses the point further: the Qur'an itself commands
only love for God, not explicitly for neighbor (that teaching comes
instead from hadith material), and love for *enemies* specifically is
altogether absent from both the Qur'an and hadith — whereas love for
enemies sits at the very heart of Jesus' recorded teaching, a claim
Paul notes even the most skeptical New Testament scholars accept as
authentically dominical. Ibrahim counters with Muhammad's own
forgiveness of the Quraysh tribe after their military defeat as a
comparable act of mercy, though the two examples differ in kind — one
a general ethical command, the other a specific historical act of
clemency toward a defeated rival.

## Proclaimer of the Kingdom of God

Ibrahim draws a parallel between Jesus' warnings against wealth and
similar Qur'anic emphasis on almsgiving, and between Jesus' call to
readiness for God's coming kingdom and the Qur'an's warnings to be
ready for the Day of Judgment. A harder question Ibrahim raises: how
could Jesus commend the faith of a Roman soldier while apparently
avoiding direct confrontation with Roman political rule? Paul's
answer — that Jesus valued the religious freedom Rome afforded Jews
and did not wish to provoke rebellion that would jeopardize it — opens
onto a broader and only partially resolved conversation about the
relationship between Jesus' kingdom teaching and worldly political
power, a question with obvious resonance for contemporary
Christian-Muslim political contexts as well.

## Word and Spirit of God

Ibrahim notes the Qur'an describes both John the Baptist and Jesus as
"a word from God," arguing that this description is not unique to
Jesus among God's messengers. Paul counters that because Jesus, on
the Qur'an's own account, uniquely brought the Gospel (Injil) as a
distinctive revealed message, his designation as God's Word carries a
weight not shared by the other prophets. The conversation turns to the
distinctively high Christology of John's Gospel specifically — Ibrahim
suggests John may reflect the theological development of a later
"Johannine community" further from the historical record than the
other three Gospels, while Paul defends the Fourth Gospel's own
integrity as an authentic, if theologically developed, testimony to
how Jesus' first disciples actually experienced him as the Word made
flesh.

## Son of Mary, Son of Man

The Qur'an's characteristic title for Jesus, "son of Mary," is read by
Ibrahim as a deliberate reminder that Jesus shared Mary's ordinary
human nature without any admixture of divine nature — a corrective, on
the traditional Muslim reading, to later Christian followers who
elevated Jesus to the same status as God. Paul observes that the
Gospels themselves use "son of Mary" only once, suggesting the title
carried a different, more limited significance for Jesus' earliest
followers than the significance the Qur'an later assigns it.

## Messiah and Redeemer

Ibrahim notes, accurately, that the Gospels never record Jesus
directly calling himself "Messiah" — likely, in Paul's assessment,
because Jesus deliberately declined the term's traditional military
connotations while still redefining what messianic identity would
mean, centrally by laying down his life as a sacrifice for others'
sin (drawing on Isaiah 53's suffering servant). Ibrahim, respecting
Jesus as an exemplary model of patient endurance under opposition,
resists the sacrificial reading, preferring to understand Jesus as a
martyr for truth rather than a redemptive sacrifice — citing other
Christian scholars who read Isaiah 53 as referring instead to the
Maccabean martyrs rather than to Jesus, a genuine point of ongoing
scholarly dispute that the dialogue leaves unresolved rather than
forcing to a tidy conclusion.

## Raised from Death and Returning in Power

On the resurrection, Paul anchors the discussion in the apostle
Paul's own transformative encounter with the risen Jesus — decisive,
in Beaumont's Paul's view, because it converted a former opponent of
the church into its most energetic missionary. Ibrahim probes hard
questions about the nature of the resurrection body (how could Jesus
pass through walls if truly flesh and blood?) and about varying levels
of belief even among the first disciples. On Jesus' promised return,
Ibrahim raises the hadith tradition that Jesus will return specifically
to "break crosses and kill pigs" — read by many Muslims as a
repudiation of Christian symbols and practice — while Paul argues this
tradition developed only in the ninth century and does not reflect an
original teaching of Muhammad himself, a historical dating question
Ibrahim in turn defends against.

## Missiological Reflection

Beaumont's dialogic format is itself the book's central
methodological contribution: rather than a one-sided Christian
apologetic or a comparative-religion survey delivered by a single
narrator, the format forces sustained, good-faith engagement with the
strongest form of the other side's argument on each specific
Christological question — modeling, for Christian workers among
Muslims, the kind of patient, textually literate back-and-forth that
genuine conversation with an informed Muslim conversation partner
will actually require, rather than a scripted presentation that
assumes the Muslim side is unprepared to respond in kind.
