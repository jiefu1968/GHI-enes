# Engaging Islam: Gaining a Heart for Muslims and Dispelling Misconceptions

## Overview

This file synthesizes the opening chapters of Georges Houssney's
*Engaging Islam*. Houssney, who grew up in North Lebanon and has spent
decades in Arabic and Kurdish Bible translation work and Muslim
evangelism, writes from direct personal experience rather than purely
academic study. This file covers his own journey from resentment
toward Muslims to a settled call to reach them, his effort to dispel
common Western misconceptions about who Muslims are, typical Christian
reactions to Islam, and the psychological and spiritual factors behind
religious conversion in both directions.

## Gaining a Heart for Muslims: A Personal Journey

Houssney frames the whole book around a single conviction: to engage
Islam as a system, Christians must engage Muslims as people, following
Paul's self-understanding as "Christ's ambassador" (2 Corinthians
5:20). He is candid that this was not his natural starting point.
Raised in North Lebanon amid multiple religions, sects, and
worldviews, he describes resentment toward Islam and Muslims brewing
in his heart as a boy. Two turning points reshaped that resentment
into calling: first, receiving a Gospel of Matthew while playing
soccer in the street and later coming to faith at a Baptist mission
center at age twelve, an experience that began to change how he saw
Muslims even though he had previously responded to conflict with
violence; second, and more decisively, being brought along at age
fourteen on an Operation Mobilization team doing door-to-door
witnessing to Muslims — an experience he initially resisted before
recognizing it as God's specific call on his life. He would go on to
spend eight consecutive summers doing this door-to-door work across
northeastern Lebanon, describing direct gospel engagement with
Muslims as the central goal and vision of his life and ministry.

## Who Muslims Are Not: Dispelling Common Misconceptions

Houssney presents what he calls the ten misconceptions about Muslims
he most commonly encounters among Christians with little direct
contact with them. Two examples illustrate his approach. First, the
idea that "Islam is a monolithic religion" — in reality, Houssney
argues, Islam is as internally diverse as Christianity, with real
ethnic, national, tribal, linguistic, and sectarian differences among
Muslims; most Western perceptions are shaped by a small, highly
visible minority of Muslims presented in media coverage, when in
practice a Muslim from one context can differ from another as much as
a Russian Orthodox Ukrainian differs from a Texan Pentecostal. Second,
the assumption that "all Muslims are Arabs" — in fact, over eighty
percent of the world's Muslims are not Arab at all, even though the
Arab League's twenty-two member countries span an enormous arc from
Morocco and Mauritania in the west, through Egypt and Sudan, north
through Syria and Iraq, and south through the Arabian Peninsula and
the Horn of Africa. Houssney's broader point is that Western
Christians' mental picture of "the Muslim" is frequently a caricature
assembled from media coverage of a narrow, unrepresentative slice of
the actual global Muslim population.

## Christian Reactions to Islam: A Generational Shift

Houssney tracks a striking generational change through a simple
recurring exercise: for years, when speaking at American churches, he
would ask how many in the audience had ever personally met a Muslim.
In 1982, at a Kansas church of roughly a thousand people, only four
hands went up. In subsequent decades the same question in similar
settings typically produces a majority of raised hands. He attributes
this shift to several converging factors: Muslims are among the most
rapidly growing minority populations in America; globalization and
education have diversified the professional and socioeconomic profile
of Muslim communities Americans actually encounter; and general
American awareness of Muslims — some long-settled across generations,
some recent immigrants arriving through chain migration — has grown
substantially. The practical implication Houssney draws is that
Christian unfamiliarity with Muslims, while still common, is no
longer inevitable — most churchgoers today do have some path to
direct personal contact if they seek it out.

## The Religious Life of Muslims: Obedience Without Assurance

Houssney illustrates the character of ordinary Muslim religious life
through a four-hour conversation with a Pakistani imam who had
invited him home after a lecture. When Houssney shared his testimony
of a transformed, personal relationship with God through Christ, the
imam interrupted: "My business is not to believe or understand
anything… As a Muslim I am commanded to obey and do Allah's bidding."
The imam had no interest in the possibility of an intimate personal
relationship with God, naming instead the Five Pillars of Islam as
the entirety of what God expected of him. Houssney likens this
posture to his own experience of Lebanese military training, where
recruits were told "you obey, no questions asked" — an analogy he uses
to characterize Islamic religious life more broadly as oriented around
required ritual obedience rather than a sought relationship, tracing
this ritual structure back to a hadith attributing the "five things"
of Islam directly to Muhammad.

## Spiritual Factors in Religious Conversion

Houssney treats conversion — in either direction — as a genuinely
complex phenomenon rather than a simple matter of which religion's
arguments are more persuasive. Some converts are rebels defying family
tradition by moving in the opposite direction; some are bored with
inherited tradition and drawn to novelty; some undergo a conversion
Houssney considers spiritually genuine; and some convert for entirely
this-worldly reasons — he recounts a journalist who converted to Islam
purely to gain access to Mecca for a story, later admitting to
Houssney that it had been "a white lie." On the mission field, this
complexity cuts both ways: it can be genuinely difficult to discern
whether someone's conversion to Christianity is spiritually authentic
or motivated by access to jobs, humanitarian assistance, a visa, or
marriage — a dynamic Houssney treats honestly rather than either
denying it exists or using it to dismiss the validity of Muslim-
background conversions in general.

## Missiological Reflection

Houssney's opening chapters function as a sustained argument that
accurate factual knowledge about Islam and genuine relational
engagement with Muslims are not separable tasks: dispelling
misconceptions about who Muslims are matters precisely because it
clears space for Christians to actually meet and listen to Muslim
neighbors as individuals rather than as representatives of a
media-constructed caricature, while his own testimony underscores
that a heart for Muslim evangelism is often built gradually, through
direct exposure, rather than arriving fully formed.
