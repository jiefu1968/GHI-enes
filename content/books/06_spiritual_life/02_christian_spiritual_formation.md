# Christian Spiritual Formation: An Integrated Approach to Wholeness (based on Diane Chandler's framework)

## Overview

This framework's distinctive contribution is treating spiritual
formation as inherently multidimensional rather than narrowly focused
on devotional practices alone. Growth in Christ is described as
touching the whole person — spirit, emotions, relationships, intellect,
vocation, and physical health — with stewardship of these dimensions
treated as itself a spiritual discipline. For missionary training, this
integrated view guards against a common distortion where "spiritual"
growth is measured only by prayer and Bible reading while emotional
health, relational patterns, and even physical health are treated as
separate, unspiritual categories.

## The Birthplace of Christian Spiritual Formation

Formation is grounded historically and theologically in the classical
Christian conviction that transformation into Christlikeness is the
goal of the Christian life, not merely correct belief or external
religious behavior. This framing matters for missionary training
because it resists a purely informational model of discipleship
(transferring correct doctrine) in favor of a formational model
(cultivating a whole person increasingly conformed to Christ) —
a shift with real implications for how training curricula are designed
and evaluated.

## The Love of God and the Work of the Spirit

The foundation for all growth is God's initiating love, received and
responded to, with the Holy Spirit as the active agent of
transformation rather than the believer's own effort as the primary
engine of change. This ordering — grace and Spirit-empowerment first,
human effort as responsive cooperation rather than the initiating
force — shapes how each subsequent dimension of formation is addressed.

## Emotions and Relationships

Emotional health is treated as integral to spiritual maturity, not a
secondary or merely psychological concern — unprocessed emotional wounds
are understood to distort a person's capacity to relate to God and
others well, regardless of how much correct doctrine they hold.
Relational formation follows directly: since Christian maturity is
inherently relational (loving God and neighbor), a person's actual
relational patterns — conflict style, capacity for intimacy, patterns
of avoidance or enmeshment — are treated as legitimate territory for
spiritual formation rather than being outside its scope. This has
direct relevance for missionary self-care and team dynamics: cross-cultural
teams under stress often surface exactly these unresolved emotional and
relational patterns, and a formation model that names this explicitly
gives missionaries language and permission to address it rather than
spiritualizing over it.

## Intellect and Vocation

The intellectual dimension addresses how a believer's mind — their
thinking, worldview, and capacity for discernment — is itself formed
and re-formed in Christ, resisting an anti-intellectual strand
sometimes present in devotional Christianity. Vocation addresses work
and calling as a dimension of formation in its own right: how a
believer understands and lives out their work (whether formal ministry
or otherwise) as part of their formation, not a separate "secular" realm
disconnected from spiritual growth.

## Physical Health and Stewardship

Physical health is named explicitly as a legitimate dimension of
spiritual formation — the body's care treated as stewardship rather
than either neglect (common among overworked missionaries) or vanity.
Stewardship more broadly extends this logic to time, resources, and
gifts, treating how a believer manages what they've been given as
itself a spiritual practice rather than a separate practical concern.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

This integrated model offers a useful diagnostic grid for evaluating a
missionary's overall health, not just their devotional consistency: a
worker who prays faithfully but is relationally isolated, emotionally
unprocessed, physically depleted, or vocationally disengaged is not, by
this framework, spiritually healthy — all six dimensions need ongoing
attention. This directly informs how mentors and team leaders can ask
better diagnostic questions than "how's your quiet time," addressing
burnout and attrition risk factors before they become severe.

## Discussion prompts for training

- Which of the six dimensions (spirit, emotions, relationships,
  intellect, vocation, physical health) is currently most neglected in
  your own life, and why?
- How would you explain to a new believer that emotional health is not
  a separate, "unspiritual" category from their walk with God?
- What would it look like for a mentor to ask about all six dimensions,
  rather than only asking about someone's devotional habits?
