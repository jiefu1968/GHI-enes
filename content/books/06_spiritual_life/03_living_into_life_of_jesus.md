# Living into the Life of Jesus: A Process for Christian Character Formation (based on Klaus Issler's framework)

## Overview

This framework organizes Christian character formation around a
three-part logic: understanding the process by which formation
happens, understanding the divine resources God provides to make that
process possible, and applying both to the specific, unglamorous gaps
of daily life where character is actually tested. Its central concern
is bridging the common gap between believing sound doctrine and
actually living a transformed life — addressing why so many believers
who affirm the right things still struggle to consistently live them
out.

## Part One: A Process for Christian Formation

Formation is described as a process rather than a single decisive
event — meaning growth in Christlike character happens gradually,
through repeated practice and formation of habits, rather than through
willpower alone or a single moment of decision. A key diagnostic move
here is naming specific "gaps" between what a believer intellectually
affirms and what they actually do under real pressure (anger,
temptation, fear, fatigue) — the process of formation is essentially
the work of closing these gaps over time through practiced, Spirit-
enabled habit rather than through repeated resolutions of willpower that
tend to fail under stress.

## Part Two: Essential Divine Resources of Formation Grace

Formation is not treated as a self-improvement project the believer
carries out alone — specific divine resources are identified as
necessary and available: grace that empowers rather than only forgives,
the ongoing work of the Holy Spirit, and habits of attention to God's
presence that make moment-by-moment dependence realistic rather than
merely theoretical. This matters for missionary training because it
resists two opposite errors: passive fatalism (assuming character
change is entirely up to God and requires no personal effort) and
exhausting self-effort (assuming character change is a project the
believer must grind out through sheer discipline). The framework's
answer is a cooperative model — real human practice, genuinely
empowered by real divine grace.

## Part Three: Following Jesus Daily in Our Gaps

The final section moves from theory to specific, everyday
situations where the gap between belief and behavior is most acutely
tested — how Jesus himself modeled reliance on the Father rather than
independent human strength alone, offering a pattern for ordinary
believers navigating fatigue, temptation, conflict, and fear. A
recurring point is that Jesus' own humanity is not incidental to this
discussion: his example of depending on the Father, rather than
functioning purely from independent divine power, is presented as the
pattern for how ordinary believers can depend on the same divine
resources in their own daily gaps.

## Why "Gaps" Is a Useful Diagnostic Category

Naming specific gaps (the difference between what I say I believe about
patience, and how I actually react when someone cuts in line; between
what I affirm about God's provision, and how I actually behave under
financial stress) gives missionaries and mentors a much more concrete
tool for spiritual conversations than a vague question like "how is
your walk with God." Asking "where's the gap right now" invites honest,
specific diagnosis rather than a generic spiritual status report.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

Missionary life produces its own distinctive set of gaps — between
patience professed and patience practiced under cross-cultural
misunderstanding, between trust in God's provision professed and
anxiety practiced under genuine financial or safety uncertainty on the
field. Training workers to name their specific gaps, rather than
offering only general spiritual encouragement, gives mentors and peer
groups a much more actionable framework for ongoing character
formation during and after deployment.

## Discussion prompts for training

- Name one specific "gap" between what you affirm about God and how
  you actually behave under a particular kind of stress. What would
  closing that gap look like in practice, not just in theory?
- How does Jesus' own reliance on the Father (rather than independent
  strength) reshape how you think about your own dependence on God in
  daily ministry pressure?
- Why might "cooperative" formation (real practice, real grace) be a
  healthier posture for a missionary than either passive fatalism or
  exhausting self-effort?
