# A Popular Survey of Bible Doctrine: Foundations for Every Believer (based on Norman Geisler's framework)

## Overview

This framework aims at accessibility — presenting core Christian
doctrine in plain, non-technical language for lay believers rather than
seminary students, while still covering the same major categories
(Scripture, God, creation, humanity, sin, salvation, the church, last
things) that more academic systematic theologies address. Its
distinctive contribution for training purposes is the emphasis it puts
on epistemology first: before stating what Christians believe, it asks
how anyone can know truth at all, and how special revelation relates
to general categories like truth, knowledge, and meaning.

## Truth, Knowledge, and Revelation

Before any specific doctrine, groundwork is laid on why truth claims
are even possible — addressing questions about the nature of truth,
knowledge, meaning, and language themselves. This matters directly for
cross-cultural apologetics: many contemporary conversation partners
(secular, postmodern, or influenced by certain strands of Buddhist
thought) will question whether any truth claim about God can be made
at all before they'll engage the content of the claim. Equipping
missionaries to address this epistemological layer — rather than
jumping straight to "here's what the Bible says" — prevents a common
frustration where gospel conversations stall on unspoken assumptions
about whether truth or meaning are even coherent categories.

## Inspiration, Inerrancy, and Canon

A substantial section addresses how the Bible came to be trusted as
God's Word — the process of inspiration (God working through human
authors to produce a fully authoritative text), inerrancy (the Bible's
trustworthiness in what it affirms), and canonicity (how the 66 books
came to be recognized as authoritative Scripture, as distinct from
other ancient religious writings). This section functions as a toolkit
for handling common objections: "how do you know the Bible wasn't
changed," "why these books," "doesn't this contradict that."

## The Nature and Attributes of God

God's attributes are organized into categories: metaphysical attributes
intrinsic to his nature (such as eternity, immutability, omnipresence),
moral attributes describing his character (holiness, justice, love),
and attributes describing his activity in relation to creation
(providence, judgment, mercy). The Trinity is treated as the
capstone of this section — one God eternally existing as three
distinct persons, illustrated (with the biblical text at Jesus' baptism
as a key example) by all three persons being simultaneously present and
active in a single narrative event. Properly distinguishing types of
divine attributes gives missionaries a clearer vocabulary for
addressing common confusions: for instance, explaining why God's
unchangeableness doesn't mean he is uninvolved or unresponsive, a
frequent point of confusion when engaging fatalistic worldviews.

## Creation, Preservation, and Providence

Creation is treated not just as a past event but as an ongoing
relationship: God preserves what he has made in existence moment by
moment, and providentially governs history toward his purposes. This
distinction (creation vs. preservation vs. providence) is useful when
addressing folk-religious or animistic worldviews that assume the
material world runs independently of any deity once made, or that
attribute ongoing events to impersonal fate rather than a personal,
active God.

## Application to missionary training

Because this framework begins with epistemology rather than assuming
shared assumptions about truth, it models a pattern missionaries can
imitate: before presenting doctrinal content to a new context, first
identify what the audience already assumes about whether truth,
meaning, and revelation are even possible categories, and address that
layer explicitly rather than skipping past it.

## Discussion prompts for training

- How would you address a conversation partner who says "that might be
  true for you, but not for me" before even discussing the content of
  the gospel?
- Why does distinguishing God's metaphysical attributes (his nature)
  from his moral attributes (his character) matter for cross-cultural
  teaching?
- How might explaining providence help a listener from a fatalistic
  worldview see God as personally involved rather than distant?
