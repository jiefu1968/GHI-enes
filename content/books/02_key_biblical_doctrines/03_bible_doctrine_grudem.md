# Bible Doctrine: A Comprehensive Systematic Outline (based on Wayne Grudem's framework)

## Overview

This is the most comprehensive of the systematic frameworks in this
module, organized into six major parts that trace a logical order:
the Word of God, the doctrine of God, the doctrine of man, the
doctrine of Christ, the doctrine of applied redemption, and the
doctrine of the church. Its usefulness for missionary training lies
precisely in that comprehensiveness and order — it functions as a full
curriculum map that other, shorter treatments (like the other three
books in this module) can be located within.

## Part I & II: Scripture and God

The doctrine of Scripture is anchored in two claims: the Bible's
authority and inerrancy (it is fully trustworthy in everything it
affirms), and its clarity, necessity, and sufficiency (it can be
understood by ordinary readers, is required for saving knowledge of
God, and provides everything needed for faith and life without
requiring supplementary revelation). This has direct missiological
weight: it grounds why Bible translation and vernacular Scripture
access are central mission priorities rather than optional extras, and
why oral Bible storying (used with non-literate populations) is not a
lesser substitute for "real" Bible study but a legitimate application
of Scripture's clarity and sufficiency. The doctrine of God covers his
incommunicable attributes (qualities God does not share with creatures,
like infinity and unchangeableness) and communicable attributes
(qualities reflected, in creaturely measure, in humanity — knowledge,
wisdom, love, holiness), the Trinity, creation, providence, prayer, and
the reality of angels, Satan, and demons.

## Part III & IV: Humanity and Christ

Humanity is treated as created in God's image (male and female jointly
reflecting that image, each with dignity and complementary roles) and
as fallen into sin, which is defined comprehensively — not just
individual wrongdoing but a condition affecting the whole person and
all human relationships. The doctrine of Christ covers his person
(fully God and fully man in one person) and his atoning work: why the
cross was necessary, what it accomplished (satisfying justice, bearing
sin's penalty, reconciling humanity to God), and his bodily resurrection
and ascension as historical, not merely symbolic, events.

## Part V: The Application of Redemption (the Ordo Salutis)

This section is one of the most practically important for missionary
training because it lays out, step by step, how salvation is applied
to an individual over time: common grace (God's non-saving kindness
extended to all people, which explains genuine goodness found outside
the church); election (God's choice, prior to any human merit);
the effectual call of the gospel; regeneration (the Spirit's work of
giving spiritual life); conversion (the human response of faith and
repentance); justification (being declared righteous) and adoption
(being made God's child); sanctification (progressive growth in
holiness); perseverance (God's preservation of genuine believers); and
glorification (final bodily resurrection and perfection). Teaching this
full sequence — rather than collapsing everything into a single
"decision moment" — helps missionaries disciple new believers with
realistic expectations about ongoing struggle and growth, rather than
implying that conversion should immediately produce a struggle-free
life.

## Part VI: The Church

The final section covers the church's nature (the gathered community
of believers, not merely a building or organization), the ordinances of
baptism and the Lord's Supper, and by implication church government and
mission — the church as the primary vehicle through which the gospel
continues to spread and disciples continue to be formed.

## Application to missionary training

Because this framework moves in careful logical order from Scripture
to God to humanity to Christ to salvation to church, it can function as
a checklist: when teaching a new believer or an indigenous leader,
missionaries can verify that no major doctrinal area has been skipped
or left implicit, which matters especially in oral cultures where
teaching sequence and repetition substitute for a leader's ability to
consult a written systematic theology independently later.

## Discussion prompts for training

- Why does the doctrine of Scripture's "sufficiency" matter for
  deciding what oral Bible storying can and cannot substitute for?
- How would you explain the difference between justification and
  sanctification to a new believer who expects instant moral
  perfection after conversion?
- What role does "common grace" play in explaining genuine goodness you
  observe in people from other religious backgrounds?
