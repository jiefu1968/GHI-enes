# Essential Truths of the Christian Faith: A Beginner's Systematic Primer (based on R.C. Sproul's framework)

## Overview

This framework is designed as an entry point into systematic theology
for believers who feel intimidated by doctrine — organized as short,
focused units that each answer one core question (What is revelation?
What is the Trinity? What is justification?) rather than one long
continuous argument. Its underlying conviction is that ordinary
Christians, not just scholars, need theological depth: doctrine is
presented not as academic hairsplitting but as nourishment ("theology
feeds the soul") that Scripture itself commands believers to pursue
diligently.

## Why Christians Avoid Doctrine

Before laying out doctrine itself, this approach diagnoses common
reasons believers stay theologically shallow: assuming childlike faith
means uncritical faith; fear that careful study leads to doubt;
"easy believism" that reduces salvation to a one-time decision with no
ongoing growth; retreating from the world instead of engaging it
thoughtfully; avoiding controversial topics out of conflict-aversion;
an anti-rational cultural mood that distrusts careful reasoning;
worldliness crowding out spiritual priorities; substituting emotional
devotion for actual study; and simple laziness or disobedience to the
biblical command to grow in knowledge. Naming these causes explicitly
is itself a teaching tool — missionaries can use this list diagnostically
with trainees who resist theological study, helping them recognize
which specific excuse they're making.

## Revelation as the Starting Point

Doctrine begins with how God makes himself known: general revelation
(creation and conscience, available to all people everywhere) and
special revelation (Scripture, God's self-disclosure in specific,
verbal form). The distinction matters cross-culturally — general
revelation explains why every culture has some intuition of the divine
and moral order, while special revelation explains why the specific
content of the gospel must still be proclaimed rather than assumed to
be already known. This framework also addresses paradox and apparent
contradiction directly (how can God be three and one; how can Christ be
fully God and fully man), teaching that some doctrines are properly
mysterious without being logically contradictory — an important
distinction for cross-cultural apologetics, since many worldviews
(Buddhist, folk-religious) are comfortable with paradox in ways that
can either help or confuse gospel communication depending on how
carefully the difference between "paradox" and "contradiction" is
taught.

## Canon and Authority

Attention is given to how the Bible's authority is grounded — the
process by which the canon (the recognized list of authoritative books)
was formed, and why the church recognized rather than created biblical
authority. For missionaries working among people who ask "why these
books and not others" (a common question from Muslim and skeptical
dialogue partners), having a clear, non-defensive account of canon
formation is essential apologetic preparation.

## Application to missionary training

The diagnostic list of reasons believers avoid theology doubles as a
self-assessment tool for missionary candidates themselves: workers who
haven't examined their own resistance to careful doctrine are poorly
equipped to teach it cross-culturally, especially in oral or
low-literacy contexts where clear, well-organized doctrinal teaching
(rather than vague spiritual sentiment) is what indigenous leaders will
need to reproduce.

## Discussion prompts for training

- Which of the ten listed reasons for avoiding doctrine do you
  recognize in yourself, and what would addressing it look like
  practically?
- How would you explain the difference between a biblical "mystery"
  and a logical "contradiction" to someone from a culture comfortable
  with paradox?
- If a Muslim dialogue partner asked you why Christians trust these
  particular 66 books, how would you respond without becoming
  defensive?
