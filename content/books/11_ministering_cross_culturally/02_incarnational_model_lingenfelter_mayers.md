# The Incarnational Ministry Model (Lingenfelter & Mayers)

Sherwood Lingenfelter and Marvin Mayers' "Ministering Cross-Culturally" argues that effective cross-cultural ministry is less about mastering theory and more about a set of concrete, learnable relational habits — modeled on the incarnation itself: Christ did not simply send information about God from a distance, he "became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14), entering fully into a specific culture, language, and set of social constraints.

## The core relational skills

**1. Learn your role in the new society, not the role you had at home.**
A missionary who was a respected professional, pastor, or expert at home may need to accept the role of a child, student, or novice in the new setting — socially, linguistically, and practically. Resisting this (insisting on being treated as the expert one was at home) blocks the very relationships ministry depends on.

**2. Adjust to local patterns of authority and decision-making.**
Home-culture assumptions about who decides what, how decisions get made (individually vs. by consensus vs. by an elder/authority), and how quickly, often do not transfer. Learning to work within — not around — the host culture's actual authority structure is a discipline, not a compromise.

**3. Accept a season of dependence as normal, not a failure.**
Early cross-cultural life often involves genuine dependence — on language helpers, on being shown how ordinary tasks work, on others' patience with basic mistakes. Resisting or resenting this dependence (trying to "get back to competence" as fast as possible) undercuts the relationships that dependence itself can build, since being cared for is also a way of being known.

**4. Prioritize relationship with people over completing tasks on a home-culture timetable.**
A ministry plan, project deadline, or agenda is not wrong to have, but when it consistently overrides the time relationships actually take to build, it communicates (regardless of intent) that people are instrumental to the missionary's goals rather than the actual point of being there.

**5. Practice patience with ambiguity.**
Cross-cultural life involves constant not-knowing — social cues you can't read yet, decisions you don't understand the reasoning behind, timelines that shift without explanation. Tolerating this ambiguity without forcing premature clarity (or complaining about it to the host community) is itself a relational skill.

**6. Practice genuine self-sacrifice.**
Not performative hardship, but a real, ongoing willingness to give up comfort, preference, and being right, for the sake of the relationship and the ministry — modeled directly on Philippians 2's description of Christ's self-emptying.

**7. Communicate acceptance and love in locally legible ways.**
A gesture of care that makes perfect sense in the missionary's home culture may not read as care at all in the host culture (see Module 28, Intercultural Communication, on nonverbal systems) — learning the host culture's actual "love languages," not just performing one's own, is necessary for the message of care to actually land.

**8. Remain a permanent learner, never fully transitioning to "expert."**
Even missionaries with decades of field experience are wise to hold their cultural understanding as provisional and still-learning, rather than assuming years on the field have made them a finished cultural insider — the moment a missionary stops asking questions and starts only explaining, something has usually gone wrong.

## Why this belongs alongside the more theoretical modules
Modules 26 (Intercultural Intelligence), 28 (Intercultural Communication), and this module all describe overlapping territory from different angles — CQ and communication theory explain *why* cross-cultural friction happens; the incarnational model here is the *daily practice* that actually builds trust once you understand why. Theory without these habits produces missionaries who can accurately diagnose cultural friction but never build the relationships that let them minister through it.
