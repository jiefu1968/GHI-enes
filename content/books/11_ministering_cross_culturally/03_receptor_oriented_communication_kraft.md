# Receptor-Oriented Communication (Kraft, "Communicating Christ Cross-Culturally")

Charles Kraft's foundational contribution to missionary communication theory can be stated in a single, deceptively simple principle: **communication is measured by what the receptor actually understood, not by what the sender intended to say.**

## Why this reframes the missionary's task
A message that was theologically precise, carefully translated, and delivered with full sincerity can still fail completely as *communication* if it was heard by the receptor as something different from — or opposite to — what the sender meant. Kraft's point is not that content doesn't matter; it's that faithful communication requires actively verifying and adjusting for what actually landed, rather than assuming that saying something correctly is the same as communicating it.

## Sender-oriented vs. receptor-oriented communication
- **Sender-oriented communication** asks: "Did I say the true and correct thing, using accurate words and theological precision?" Success is measured by the sender's own standard, independent of the audience.
- **Receptor-oriented communication** asks: "What did the actual listener, in their actual cultural and linguistic frame, understand me to say?" Success is measured by the effect achieved in the receptor, not the accuracy of the sender's intention.

This does not mean truth is relative to the listener — it means the *same true content* requires different vehicles (forms, illustrations, vocabulary, structure) to actually arrive intact in different cultural receptors, exactly as a dynamic-equivalence Bible translation (see Module 28's translation-pitfalls content) restructures a sentence to preserve *meaning* rather than preserving *word order*.

## Implications for gospel presentation
- **Test understanding, don't just deliver content.** Asking a listener to explain back what they understood (in their own words) reveals gaps invisible from the sender's side — this is uncomfortable for a missionary trained to see teaching as a one-way delivery of correct information, but it is the only reliable way to know if communication, not just speech, actually occurred.
- **Choose illustrations and vocabulary from the receptor's world, not the sender's.** A parable, image, or word choice that communicates powerfully in the missionary's home culture may communicate something different, or nothing at all, in the receptor's — this connects directly to the three-worldview framework (file 01): the same gospel truth needs a different door depending on whether the receptor's felt need is guilt, shame, or fear.
- **Contextualization (Module 23) is receptor-oriented communication applied to theology and church practice** — the same underlying principle (meaning must be reconstructed in the receptor's frame, not merely transmitted in the sender's) that Kraft applies to communication generally.

## A caution this framework itself requires
Receptor-oriented communication asks "what did they understand," not "what did they want to hear" — the goal is accurate transfer of the actual gospel content into the receptor's frame, not audience-pleasing dilution of that content. Kraft's model is a communication discipline in service of faithful proclamation, not a license to change what is being communicated based on what the receptor would prefer.
