# The Three Worldviews: Guilt-Innocence, Shame-Honor, Fear-Power (Georges' "3D Gospel")

Jayson Georges' widely-used framework argues that every culture leans toward one (or a blend) of three basic moral/religious worldviews — three different answers to the question "what is fundamentally wrong with humanity, and what would fix it?" Most Western missionary training presents the gospel through only one of these three lenses by default, without realizing the other two are just as biblical and often far more resonant in a given culture.

## 1. Guilt-Innocence
- **The felt problem**: I have broken a law or standard; I am guilty and deserve punishment.
- **The felt need**: acquittal — a clear conscience, a "not guilty" verdict.
- **Gospel framing**: Christ's death as legal substitution — he bears the penalty for our violated law, we are declared righteous (justification), the record of debt is cancelled (Col 2:14).
- **Cultural home**: much of Western Europe and North America, and much of historic Reformation theology, which was itself shaped in a culture with strong Roman-law heritage.

## 2. Shame-Honor
- **The felt problem**: I have broken a relationship or violated the social order; I am exposed, disgraced, cut off from the community's approval.
- **The felt need**: restored honor and belonging — being welcomed back, having one's status and face restored.
- **Gospel framing**: Christ bears our shame and disgrace on the cross (Heb 12:2, "who for the joy set before him endured the cross, despising the shame") and is exalted/vindicated (Phil 2:9-11) so that those united to him share in his honor and are welcomed into God's family, no longer strangers and aliens (Eph 2:19).
- **Cultural home**: much of East Asia, the Middle East, the Mediterranean world, and Latin America — arguably the dominant lens of the biblical world itself, including much of first-century Jewish and Greco-Roman culture.

## 3. Fear-Power
- **The felt problem**: I am vulnerable to malevolent spiritual forces — curses, evil spirits, fate, the power of the ancestors or the unseen world.
- **The felt need**: protection, deliverance, and access to a greater power than the forces I fear.
- **Gospel framing**: Christ's death and resurrection as decisive victory over the spiritual powers — "having disarmed the rulers and authorities, he put them to open shame, by triumphing over them" (Col 2:15); believers are given authority over the enemy (Luke 10:19) and delivered from the fear of death itself (Heb 2:14-15).
- **Cultural home**: much of Sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, and folk-religious contexts worldwide (including folk Buddhist, folk Muslim, and Chinese folk-religious contexts with strong ancestor/spirit concerns).

## The full gospel answers all three at once
These are not competing gospels or three different atonement theories to pick from — the New Testament holds guilt-innocence, shame-honor, and fear-power language together, often in the same passage (Colossians 2:13-15 alone touches all three: debt cancelled, powers disarmed, shame put on open display — theirs, not Christ's). The practical missiological point is not "which theory is correct" but "which lens is this specific person or people group actually feeling the weight of sin and need through" — and presenting the gospel in a way that speaks to that felt need first, without losing the other two dimensions of the full biblical picture.

## A common missionary blind spot
A missionary trained entirely in a guilt-innocence framework, presenting the gospel to a shame-honor or fear-power culture using only legal/courtroom language ("you have broken God's law and deserve punishment"), can produce technically accurate but strangely unmoving presentations — because the felt problem being described ("you're guilty") isn't the problem the listener actually feels most acutely ("I'm disgraced" or "I'm afraid of what's out there"). Learning to hear which worldview is operating, and to present the full gospel through that door first, is a core cross-cultural ministry skill.
