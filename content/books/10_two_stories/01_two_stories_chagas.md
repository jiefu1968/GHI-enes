# The Two Stories Method: Natural Gospel Conversations (based on J.F. Chagas's framework)

## Overview

This is a practical conversational method for initiating and carrying a
gospel conversation naturally, without it feeling like a rehearsed
sales pitch. The core idea is that a gospel conversation moves through
two connected stories — "my story" (personal testimony) and "Jesus'
story" (a short biblical narrative, often chosen for its parallel to
the listener's own need) — bridged by a natural transition, and closed
with an invitation rather than a forced conclusion. The whole approach
is built around ordinary social interaction rather than a formal
evangelistic event, which makes it especially transferable to everyday
missionary life and to training new believers who don't yet have
polished communication skills.

## Stage 1: Getting Connected

The method begins before any spiritual topic is raised at all — with
ordinary initiative to start a conversation with a stranger or
acquaintance, grounded in attentive listening rather than an agenda.
The instruction is to greet people warmly wherever the missionary
happens to be, ask genuine questions about their life, and earn the
right to be heard by listening well first. The bridge into personal
testimony is meant to arise naturally out of whatever the person has
just shared — noticing a point of resonance between their situation
and the missionary's own past experience, rather than switching topics
abruptly.

## Stage 2: My Story (Personal Testimony)

The testimony is structured around three simple movements: life before
encountering Christ, the encounter itself, and life since. The
instruction is to describe the "before" honestly in terms of character
and inner experience rather than a list of sins, describe the moment
of encounter in plain, non-religious language rather than church
jargon, and describe the "after" in terms of concrete, observable
change — not a claim of instant perfection, but a real difference in
attitude and relationships. Believers who came to faith as children and
lack a dramatic "before/after" conversion story are encouraged to
instead testify to a specific struggle Christ has helped them work
through since — the method is explicit that the testimony's purpose is
not to impress but to make Jesus, not the storyteller, credible and
interesting to the listener. A crucial clarification: the testimony
itself is not the gospel — it's the doorway that earns interest in
hearing about Jesus directly.

## Stage 3: The Transition

The pivot from personal story to Jesus' story is handled as a single
observation, not a hard subject change: pointing out that the real
subject of the testimony was never the missionary but Christ, and that
what Christ did in one life he has been doing for people across
history — which opens the door to telling a specific biblical story
that mirrors the listener's own situation.

## Stage 4: Jesus' Story (Biblical Storytelling)

Because a large share of the world's population processes truth more
naturally through story than through abstract propositions, this stage
uses a short, memorable biblical narrative rather than a doctrinal
explanation. Sound storytelling technique matters here: keep the story
short enough to be easily retold (roughly a page or less), use everyday
vocabulary instead of specialized religious terms, limit the number of
names and details to only what's necessary (so the story stays
memorable and reproducible), stay strictly accurate to the biblical
text without embellishing beyond what's written, and understand the
story in its wider narrative context before simplifying it. Rather than
memorizing a script word-for-word, the missionary is meant to internalize
the story well enough to tell it naturally, in their own voice. A story
often used for this stage is the account in Mark 2 of a paralyzed man
whose friends lower him through a roof to reach Jesus — chosen because
it illustrates Jesus addressing a deeper need (forgiveness) before
addressing the visible one (physical paralysis), which resonates with
listeners carrying burdens they can't fix on their own.

## Stage 5: Discussion and Application

After telling the story, a set of open-ended questions helps the
listener process and personalize it rather than simply receive
information passively: what they liked or found compelling, what
troubled or confused them, what the story reveals about God's
character, and what part of it applies specifically to their own life.
Asking the listener to retell the story to others is itself part of the
method — reproducibility is treated as a goal from the very first
telling, not an afterthought.

## Stage 6: Closing the Approach

The conversation closes by connecting the two stories back together —
drawing out the shared theme of carrying burdens that can't be
resolved alone — and moving toward a direct, low-pressure invitation:
naming Jesus' offer of rest and forgiveness in ordinary language, asking
whether the listener would like to hear more or pray together, and
proposing a concrete way to stay in contact regardless of their answer.
The emphasis throughout this final stage is warmth and respect for the
listener's autonomy rather than pressure toward an immediate decision.

## Application to cross-cultural and oral-culture ministry

Because roughly two-thirds of the world learns better through story than
through proposition, this method is particularly well suited to oral
cultures, low-literacy populations, and any context where formal
doctrinal presentation would feel foreign or intimidating. It is also
highly reproducible: because the testimony is the missionary's own life
and the biblical story is deliberately kept short and simple, a brand
new believer can realistically learn to repeat both stages within days
of their own conversion — which matters directly for multiplication
and indigenous leadership development, rather than requiring years of
training before someone can share their faith at all.

## Discussion prompts for training

- What is a specific moment of struggle in your own life, other than a
  dramatic "before/after" conversion, that could become an honest,
  retellable testimony?
- Practice crafting a one-page version of a Gospel story (such as the
  paralytic in Mark 2) using only the names and details that are
  strictly necessary — what did you cut, and why?
- How would you adapt the "closing" invitation questions in this method
  for a culture where direct personal questions about belief are
  considered impolite from someone you've just met?
