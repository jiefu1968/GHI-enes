# Goleman's Five-Component Model of Emotional Intelligence

Daniel Goleman popularized emotional intelligence (EQ) as a set of learnable competencies distinct from cognitive intelligence (IQ) — arguing that EQ predicts real-world effectiveness, including in leadership and relationships, at least as strongly as IQ does. His model breaks EQ into five components, the first two intrapersonal, the last three increasingly interpersonal.

## 1. Self-awareness
The ability to accurately notice and name one's own emotions as they arise, rather than being swept along by them unrecognized. This sounds simple but is a genuine skill: many people can describe a physical sensation ("my chest feels tight") without accurately naming the underlying emotion (anxiety? anger? grief?), and an inaccurate label leads to a mismatched response.

## 2. Self-regulation
The ability to manage disruptive emotions and impulses rather than being controlled by them — not suppressing emotion, but choosing a response rather than reacting automatically. This is the skill that lets a person feel real anger or fear without that emotion dictating their next action.

## 3. Motivation
The ability to channel emotion toward goals, sustaining effort and optimism through setbacks rather than being derailed by short-term frustration or discouragement. Goleman ties this to resilience — the capacity to recover from disappointment and keep moving toward a meaningful goal.

## 4. Empathy
The ability to accurately read others' emotional states — noticing what someone else is feeling, often from cues other than their explicit words. This component requires real caution in cross-cultural application (see file 03) — the *skill* of empathy (accurate reading) is universal, but the display rules that signal emotion vary by culture, so accurate empathy requires cultural literacy, not just attentiveness.

## 5. Social skill
The ability to manage relationships — building rapport, handling disagreement constructively, influencing and inspiring others. This component draws on the first four: a person cannot reliably manage relationships well without first managing their own emotional state and reading others accurately.

## Why the order matters
Goleman's ordering is not arbitrary — the first two (self-awareness, self-regulation) are prerequisites for the later three. A person who cannot yet name their own anger accurately, or is swept along by it, will struggle to sustain motivated effort, read others with any accuracy, or manage relationships well. Practically, EQ development usually needs to start with the intrapersonal components before the interpersonal ones can improve reliably.
