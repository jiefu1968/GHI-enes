# The Mayer-Salovey-Caruso Ability Model, and Bar-On's Emotional-Social Intelligence Model

Alongside Goleman's five-component model (file 01), two other frameworks are widely used in EQ research and assessment, each contributing a distinct angle.

## The Mayer-Salovey-Caruso (MSCEIT) ability model
John Mayer and Peter Salovey (who coined the term "emotional intelligence" academically, before Goleman popularized it) frame EQ strictly as a set of *abilities* — closer to a cognitive skill than a personality trait — organized into four branches, often visualized as a ladder from more basic to more complex:

1. **Perceiving emotion** — accurately reading emotional signals in faces, tone of voice, body language, and situations (the most basic branch; without accurate perception, the others cannot function well).
2. **Using emotion to facilitate thought** — harnessing mood and emotion to aid reasoning, creativity, and decision-making (e.g., recognizing that a slightly anxious mood sharpens attention to detail, or that a positive mood aids brainstorming).
3. **Understanding emotion** — grasping emotional causes, how emotions change and blend over time, and the difference between similar-feeling emotions (distinguishing frustration from grief, or correctly naming a blend of relief and guilt after a hard decision).
4. **Managing emotion** — regulating one's own emotional state and constructively influencing others' emotional states (the most complex branch, building on all three below it).

The key contribution of framing EQ as an *ability model*: like a cognitive skill, it can be tested with actual performance tasks (does this person correctly identify the emotion in this face or situation?) rather than only self-report, and — importantly — it can be trained and improved through practice, the same way any skill can.

## Bar-On's Emotional-Social Intelligence (ESI) model
Reuven Bar-On's model, developed independently around similar ideas, adds explicit **interpersonal and stress-management competencies** that the more intrapersonally-weighted Goleman and MSCEIT models treat more lightly:

- **Assertiveness** — expressing feelings, beliefs, and needs directly and appropriately, without being either passive or aggressive.
- **Interpersonal relationship** — the ability to establish and maintain mutually satisfying relationships characterized by give-and-take and genuine closeness.
- **Stress tolerance** — the capacity to withstand adverse events and stressful situations without being overwhelmed, by actively coping rather than simply enduring.
- **Impulse control** — resisting or delaying an impulse, drive, or temptation to act, particularly under provocation.

## Why missionaries benefit from knowing more than one model
No single model is "the" correct account of EQ — each highlights a genuinely useful angle. MSCEIT's ability framing is useful for training (EQ as a practicable skill, not a fixed trait you either have or lack). Bar-On's stress-tolerance and impulse-control competencies map directly onto missionary resilience and culture-shock management (file 03, and Module 6, Caring for Self and Others). Goleman's five-component model (file 01) remains the most widely recognized vocabulary for discussing EQ in leadership and team contexts (Module 20). Using whichever vocabulary fits the pastoral or training conversation at hand, rather than insisting on one "correct" model, serves the missionary better than treating EQ as a single settled theory.
