# Old Testament Survey: The Pentateuch

Each book below is analyzed using ten standard Bible-survey categories:
introduction and outline, structure and major divisions, book chart
summary, summary of content, theme/purpose/application message, key
chapters, key passages, main teaching, selected related scriptures, and
distinctive features.

---

## GENESIS

### Introduction and Outline
Genesis, traditionally attributed to Moses, is the book of beginnings —
of the universe, humanity, sin, nations, and the covenant people through
whom God's redemptive plan will unfold. Outline: primeval history (1-11)
and patriarchal history (12-50).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The book is structured around ten "generations of" (toledot) formulas
that mark major transitions, and divides broadly into universal history
(creation through Babel) and the narrower focus on one family — Abraham,
Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph — chosen to carry God's covenant promise
forward.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Creation and Fall | 1-3 | Creation, the image of God, sin's entrance |
| Primeval history | 4-11 | Cain and Abel, the Flood, Babel, the nations |
| Abraham | 12-25 | The call, covenant, and testing of faith |
| Isaac and Jacob | 25-36 | Covenant continuation, deception, wrestling with God |
| Joseph | 37-50 | Betrayal, providence, preservation in Egypt |

### Summary of Content
Genesis opens with God's ordered creation and the crisis of human
rebellion, tracing sin's spread through Cain, the Flood, and Babel. The
narrative narrows to Abraham, called out of Mesopotamia and given
covenant promises of land, descendants, and blessing to the nations,
promises tested and confirmed through Isaac's near-sacrifice. Jacob's
deceptive rise and transformative encounters with God extend the
covenant to twelve sons, and Joseph's betrayal, exaltation in Egypt, and
eventual reconciliation with his brothers preserve the covenant family
during famine, setting the stage for Exodus.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: God sovereignly initiates and preserves a covenant people through
whom he will bless all nations despite human sin and failure. Purpose:
to establish Israel's origins and covenant identity, and to explain how
sin entered a good creation. Application: readers learn to trust God's
sovereign faithfulness to his promises even amid human failure and
apparent setbacks.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 3 (the Fall); chapter 12 (the Abrahamic covenant call); chapter
22 (the testing of Abraham); chapter 50 (Joseph's forgiveness and
providence).

### Key Passages
Genesis 1:26-27 (humanity in God's image); Genesis 3:15 (the first
gospel promise); Genesis 12:1-3 (the Abrahamic covenant); Genesis 50:20
("you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good").

### Main Teaching
God's redemptive purpose advances through covenant promises kept
despite human sin, deception, and apparent failure, culminating in the
preservation of the family through whom all nations will be blessed.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Romans 4 (Paul's use of Abraham's faith); Galatians 3:8 (the gospel
preached in advance to Abraham); Hebrews 11:8-22 (Genesis figures as
examples of faith).

### Distinctive Features
Genesis contains the Bible's only creation and Fall narratives, more
named genealogies than any other book, and the longest sustained
single narrative (the Joseph story, chapters 37-50) in the Pentateuch.

## EXODUS

### Introduction and Outline
Exodus narrates Israel's dramatic deliverance from Egyptian slavery, the
giving of the Law at Sinai, and the establishment of the tabernacle as
the place of God's dwelling among his people. Outline: deliverance from
Egypt (1-18), the covenant at Sinai (19-24), the tabernacle instructions
and construction (25-40).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The book moves geographically and theologically from bondage in Egypt,
through the wilderness journey to Sinai, to covenant-making and
tabernacle construction, with the golden calf incident (32-34)
interrupting the tabernacle instructions to test the covenant's
survival.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Deliverance | 1-18 | Bondage, Moses' call, the plagues, the Exodus, Sinai journey |
| Covenant | 19-24 | The Ten Commandments, the Book of the Covenant |
| Tabernacle | 25-40 | Instructions, the golden calf, construction, God's glory filling it |

### Summary of Content
Israel's oppression in Egypt sets the stage for Moses' call at the
burning bush and the ten plagues that break Pharaoh's resistance,
climaxing in the Passover and the Exodus itself, followed by the crossing
of the Red Sea. At Sinai, God establishes covenant with Israel through
the Ten Commandments and further case law, but the people's idolatry
with the golden calf nearly ends the covenant before Moses intercedes.
The book closes with detailed tabernacle construction, culminating in
God's glory filling the completed structure.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: God redeems his people from bondage and establishes covenant
relationship, dwelling among them. Purpose: to record Israel's
foundational redemption and covenant-making, providing the basis for
Israel's national and religious identity. Application: readers learn
that God redeems by grace before he demands obedience, and that his
presence, not merely rules, is the goal of covenant relationship.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 12 (the Passover); chapter 20 (the Ten Commandments); chapter
32 (the golden calf).

### Key Passages
Exodus 3:14 ("I AM WHO I AM"); Exodus 12:13 (the Passover blood);
Exodus 19:5-6 ("you shall be my treasured possession... a kingdom of
priests"); Exodus 20:1-17 (the Ten Commandments).

### Main Teaching
God redeems his people by grace and power before establishing covenant
obligations, and his ultimate goal is to dwell among his redeemed people.

### Selected Related Scriptures
1 Corinthians 5:7 (Christ as the Passover lamb); Hebrews 8-9 (the
tabernacle as a type fulfilled in Christ).

### Distinctive Features
Exodus contains the Ten Commandments, the most detailed tabernacle
construction instructions in Scripture, and the foundational Passover
narrative that shapes Israel's yearly calendar and self-understanding.

## LEVITICUS

### Introduction and Outline
Leviticus provides the priestly and ceremonial law governing Israel's
worship and holiness as a people living in covenant with a holy God who
now dwells among them. Outline: laws of sacrifice (1-7), the priesthood
(8-10), purity laws (11-15), the Day of Atonement (16), holiness code
(17-27).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The book's structural hinge is chapter 16 (the Day of Atonement),
dividing sacrificial and priestly instruction (1-15) from the "Holiness
Code" applying holiness to everyday ethical and social life (17-27).

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Sacrifices | 1-7 | Burnt, grain, peace, sin, and guilt offerings |
| Priesthood | 8-10 | Ordination of Aaron and his sons, Nadab and Abihu |
| Purity | 11-15 | Clean and unclean food, disease, bodily discharges |
| Atonement | 16 | The Day of Atonement ritual |
| Holiness code | 17-27 | Ethical holiness, festivals, sabbatical/jubilee years |

### Summary of Content
Leviticus details five categories of sacrifice, the ordination of
Aaron's priestly line (and the sobering account of Nadab and Abihu's
unauthorized offering), and extensive purity regulations distinguishing
clean from unclean. The Day of Atonement provides the yearly ritual
covering Israel's sin, and the closing "Holiness Code" extends the
principle of holiness into ethics, sexual conduct, festivals, and
economic practices like the sabbatical and jubilee years.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: a holy God requires a holy people, provided for through
sacrificial atonement. Purpose: to instruct Israel's priests and people
in proper worship and holy living befitting God's presence among them.
Application: readers see the seriousness of sin requiring atonement and
the call to reflect God's holiness in every area of daily life, not
merely ritual observance.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 16 (the Day of Atonement); chapter 19 (practical holiness,
including "love your neighbor as yourself").

### Key Passages
Leviticus 17:11 ("the life of the flesh is in the blood... it is the
blood that makes atonement"); Leviticus 19:18 ("love your neighbor as
yourself"); Leviticus 20:26 ("you shall be holy to me, for I the Lord
am holy").

### Main Teaching
Approaching a holy God requires atonement for sin, and God's redeemed
people are called to reflect his holiness in every dimension of life.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Hebrews 9-10 (Christ's sacrifice fulfilling the sacrificial system);
Matthew 22:39 (Jesus quoting Leviticus 19:18 as the second great
commandment).

### Distinctive Features
Leviticus contains the Bible's most detailed sacrificial regulations,
the origin of the phrase "love your neighbor as yourself," and the
Day of Atonement ritual central to Israel's yearly religious calendar.

## NUMBERS

### Introduction and Outline
Numbers narrates Israel's wilderness wanderings between Sinai and the
plains of Moab, marked by two generational censuses (hence the book's
name) bracketing a story of repeated rebellion and consequent forty
years of wandering. Outline: preparation to leave Sinai (1-10), rebellion
and wandering (11-25), preparation to enter Canaan (26-36).

### Structure and Major Divisions
Two census counts (chapters 1 and 26) frame the book, marking the
transition from the unbelieving exodus generation (who die in the
wilderness) to the new generation prepared to enter the promised land.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Preparation at Sinai | 1-10 | Census, camp organization, departure |
| Wandering and rebellion | 11-25 | Complaints, the spies' report, Korah's rebellion, Balaam |
| Preparation for Canaan | 26-36 | New census, laws, Moses' final instructions |

### Summary of Content
After organizing and numbering the tribes at Sinai, Israel departs for
Canaan but repeatedly rebels — complaining about food, challenging
Moses' leadership (Korah's rebellion), and most consequentially,
refusing to enter the land after the spies' fearful report, resulting
in forty years of wilderness wandering until that unbelieving
generation dies out. The narrative includes Balaam's failed attempts to
curse Israel, and closes with a new generation numbered and prepared,
receiving further instruction before entering the promised land.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: unbelief and rebellion delay God's promises, but his covenant
purposes ultimately still prevail. Purpose: to record the consequences
of Israel's unbelief at Kadesh-barnea and prepare the new generation for
conquest. Application: readers are warned against unbelief and grumbling
against God's provision, and encouraged that his purposes prevail
despite human failure and delay.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 13-14 (the spies' report and Israel's refusal to enter Canaan);
chapter 22-24 (Balaam's oracles).

### Key Passages
Numbers 13:30-31 (Caleb's faith versus the other spies' fear); Numbers
14:22-23 (judgment of forty years' wandering); Numbers 21:8-9 (the
bronze serpent, referenced by Jesus in John 3:14).

### Main Teaching
Persistent unbelief and rebellion delay the enjoyment of God's promises
and bring real consequences, yet God's covenant purposes for his people
ultimately still move forward.

### Selected Related Scriptures
John 3:14-15 (Jesus' reference to the bronze serpent); 1 Corinthians
10:1-11 (Paul's use of the wilderness generation as a warning example).

### Distinctive Features
Numbers contains two full census lists (giving the book its name),
Balaam's unusual oracles (including a speaking donkey), and the
sobering account of an entire generation excluded from the promised
land due to unbelief.

## DEUTERONOMY

### Introduction and Outline
Deuteronomy ("second law") presents Moses' final addresses to the new
generation on the plains of Moab, reviewing Israel's history, restating
covenant law, and urging wholehearted covenant loyalty before entering
Canaan. Outline: historical review (1-4), covenant stipulations (5-26),
covenant renewal, blessings and curses (27-30), Moses' final words and
death (31-34).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The book is widely recognized as following the form of ancient Near
Eastern suzerain-vassal treaties: historical prologue, stipulations,
provision for public reading, witnesses, and blessings/curses — giving
it a formal covenant-renewal structure.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Historical review | 1-4 | Recounting the wilderness journey |
| Covenant law | 5-26 | The Ten Commandments restated, detailed case law |
| Covenant renewal | 27-30 | Blessings for obedience, curses for disobedience |
| Moses' farewell | 31-34 | Joshua commissioned, Moses' song, blessing, and death |

### Summary of Content
Moses reviews Israel's wilderness journey as a warning and encouragement,
then restates the Ten Commandments and extensive case law applying
covenant principles to daily, social, and worship life. He solemnly sets
before Israel the blessings of covenant obedience and curses of
disobedience, calling the nation to wholehearted love and loyalty to
God. The book closes with Moses commissioning Joshua as his successor,
delivering a prophetic song and tribal blessings, and his death within
sight of the promised land he would not enter.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: wholehearted covenant loyalty and obedience bring life and
blessing, while unfaithfulness brings curse. Purpose: to prepare the new
generation for covenant faithfulness as they enter Canaan under new
leadership. Application: readers are called to love God wholeheartedly
and teach his commands diligently to the next generation, not merely
comply externally.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 6 (the Shema, love the Lord your God); chapter 28 (blessings
and curses); chapter 34 (Moses' death).

### Key Passages
Deuteronomy 6:4-5 (the Shema: "the Lord our God, the Lord is one...
love the Lord your God with all your heart"); Deuteronomy 18:15 (the
promise of a future prophet like Moses); Deuteronomy 30:19 ("choose
life").

### Main Teaching
Covenant faithfulness flows from wholehearted love for God, and must be
intentionally taught to each new generation, since blessing or curse
follows directly from the nation's covenant loyalty.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Matthew 4:1-11 (Jesus quoting Deuteronomy three times during his
temptation); Acts 3:22 (Peter identifying Jesus as the promised prophet
like Moses).

### Distinctive Features
Deuteronomy follows the recognizable form of ancient covenant treaties,
contains the Shema (Judaism's central confession of faith), and is the
Old Testament book most frequently quoted by Jesus.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

The Pentateuch's covenant structure (grace preceding law, redemption
preceding obligation) offers missionaries a corrective to
performance-based religious systems common in many host cultures:
Israel's Exodus deliverance came before Sinai's law-giving, modeling
that relationship with God begins with grace, not earned merit. The
holiness code's integration of ritual and ethical holiness (Leviticus
17-27) is especially useful when addressing cultures that separate
"religious" ritual purity from everyday ethics.

## Discussion prompts for training

- How does the Exodus pattern (redemption before law-giving) provide a
  model for presenting grace-based salvation in a culture that assumes
  religious standing must be earned through effort or ritual?
- Numbers shows an entire generation excluded from the promised land
  through unbelief — how would you use this account pastorally with
  believers discouraged by long delays in seeing God's promises
  fulfilled?
- How does the Shema's call to wholehearted love (Deuteronomy 6:4-5)
  challenge a culture that treats religious observance as primarily
  external compliance rather than internal devotion?
