# New Testament Survey: Acts

### Introduction and Outline
Acts is Luke's second volume, continuing his orderly account from the
Gospel of Luke by narrating the birth and expansion of the church from
Jerusalem to Rome. Traditionally attributed to Luke, a physician and
travel companion of Paul (reflected in the "we" passages), it functions
as both history and theological argument for the Spirit-empowered
spread of the gospel. Outline follows Acts 1:8's geographic program:
witness in Jerusalem (1-7), witness in Judea and Samaria (8-12), witness
to the ends of the earth through Paul's missionary journeys (13-28).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The book pivots around key transitional moments: Pentecost (2),
Stephen's martyrdom and the resulting scattering (7-8), Peter's vision
and Cornelius's conversion opening the door to Gentiles (10), and Paul's
conversion and subsequent three missionary journeys, ending with his
arrival and house arrest in Rome.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Jerusalem | 1-7 | Pentecost, early church, Stephen's martyrdom |
| Judea and Samaria | 8-12 | Scattering, Saul's conversion, Gentile inclusion |
| To the ends of the earth | 13-28 | Paul's three journeys, arrest, voyage to Rome |

### Summary of Content
Acts begins with Jesus' ascension and the promise of the Spirit,
fulfilled at Pentecost, followed by the rapid growth and persecution of
the Jerusalem church. Stephen's martyrdom scatters believers, spreading
the gospel to Samaria and beyond, while Saul's dramatic conversion
transforms the church's chief persecutor into its foremost missionary.
Peter's vision and the household of Cornelius formally open the gospel
to Gentiles without requiring circumcision, a decision ratified at the
Jerusalem Council (15). The second half follows Paul's three missionary
journeys across Asia Minor and Greece, his arrest in Jerusalem, and his
extended legal proceedings culminating in a voyage to Rome.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: the Holy Spirit empowers the church to witness to Christ from
Jerusalem to the ends of the earth. Purpose: to demonstrate the
Spirit-directed, unstoppable expansion of the gospel across ethnic and
geographic boundaries, legitimizing the Gentile mission. Application:
readers are called to depend on the Spirit's empowerment for witness
and to embrace the gospel's reach across every cultural and ethnic
boundary.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 2 (Pentecost); chapter 10 (Cornelius's conversion); chapter 15
(the Jerusalem Council); chapter 16 (Paul's call to Macedonia).

### Key Passages
Acts 1:8 (the book's geographic and thematic outline); Acts 2:1-4
(the coming of the Spirit); Acts 15:8-9 (God's cleansing of Gentile
hearts by faith apart from circumcision).

### Main Teaching
Acts teaches that the risen and ascended Christ continues his work
through the Spirit-empowered church, and that the gospel is intended
for every nation without ethnic prerequisite.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Joel 2:28-32 (the prophecy Peter cites at Pentecost); Genesis 12:3
(the promise of blessing to all nations, now being fulfilled).

### Distinctive Features
Acts contains the only New Testament narrative of the church's earliest
decades, records several detailed conversion and defense speeches
(Peter, Stephen, Paul), includes first-person "we" sections suggesting
eyewitness authorship, and ends without narrating Paul's fate, likely
because it was written while his case was still pending.

# New Testament Survey: The Pauline Epistles

## ROMANS

### Introduction and Outline
Paul's most systematic theological letter, written to a church he had
not yet visited, laying out the gospel from universal sin through
justification by faith to sanctification, Israel's place in God's plan,
and practical Christian living. Outline: sin's universal reign (1-3),
justification by faith (3-5), sanctification and the Spirit (6-8),
Israel's past, present, and future (9-11), practical Christian life
(12-16).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The letter moves in careful logical sequence rather than responding to
a specific local crisis, making it the closest thing in the New
Testament to a systematic exposition of the gospel.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Doctrinal | 1-11 | Sin, justification, sanctification, Israel |
| Practical | 12-16 | Christian ethics, church life, greetings |

### Summary of Content
Romans establishes universal human guilt before God (Jew and Gentile
alike), then presents justification by faith in Christ apart from
works of the law, grounding assurance in union with Christ and the
Spirit's work. Chapters 9-11 address God's ongoing faithfulness to
Israel despite widespread Jewish unbelief, and the closing chapters
apply the gospel to practical ethics, church unity, and civic
responsibility.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: the righteousness of God revealed and received by faith. Purpose:
to establish Paul's gospel clearly before his hoped-for visit and
onward mission to Spain. Application: readers find assurance in
justification by faith alone and are called to live transformed lives
in response to God's mercy.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 3 (justification by faith); chapter 8 (life in the Spirit and
assurance); chapter 12 (living sacrifice and transformed living).

### Key Passages
Romans 1:16-17 (the gospel as God's power for salvation); Romans 3:23-24
(universal sin, free justification); Romans 8:28-39 (God's unbreakable
purpose and love); Romans 12:1-2 (spiritual worship and renewed minds).

### Main Teaching
Salvation is entirely by grace through faith in Christ, secures
believers permanently in God's love, and produces transformed ethical
living, not as a means of earning favor but as its natural fruit.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Genesis 15:6 (Abraham's faith counted as righteousness, Paul's key Old
Testament proof text); Habakkuk 2:4 ("the righteous shall live by
faith," quoted in Romans 1:17).

### Distinctive Features
Romans is Paul's longest and most systematically organized letter,
written to a church he had not founded or visited, and has had
outsized historical influence on theological movements including the
Reformation.

## 1 CORINTHIANS

### Introduction and Outline
Written to address serious problems in the Corinthian church — division,
immorality, lawsuits, disorderly worship, and confusion about
resurrection — this letter is intensely pastoral and corrective rather
than systematic. Outline: divisions in the church (1-4), moral and
legal problems (5-6), marriage and singleness (7), Christian liberty and
idol food (8-10), worship disorders (11-14), the resurrection (15),
closing instructions (16).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The letter responds directly to reports Paul received and questions the
church had written to him ("now concerning..."), giving it a
topic-by-topic structure driven by pastoral necessity rather than
systematic theological sequence.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Reported problems | 1-6 | Division, immorality, lawsuits |
| Written questions | 7-16 | Marriage, idol food, worship, resurrection |

### Summary of Content
Paul confronts factionalism built around rival leaders, tolerated
sexual immorality, and believers suing one another in pagan courts. He
addresses questions about marriage and celibacy, food offered to idols
(balancing genuine Christian liberty against loving restraint), proper
conduct and order in worship (including spiritual gifts and the Lord's
Supper), and closes with an extended defense of the bodily resurrection
against those denying it.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: the wisdom of the cross corrects worldly wisdom and division.
Purpose: to restore unity, moral integrity, and right worship practice
in a troubled church. Application: readers learn to prioritize
sacrificial love over personal rights, unity over factionalism, and
resurrection hope over worldly wisdom.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 13 (the love chapter); chapter 15 (the resurrection defense).

### Key Passages
1 Corinthians 1:18 (the message of the cross); 1 Corinthians 13:4-7
(the description of love); 1 Corinthians 15:3-4 (the gospel summary:
Christ's death, burial, and resurrection "according to the Scriptures").

### Main Teaching
Christian unity, ethical purity, and worship order flow from the
wisdom of the cross and are ultimately grounded in the certainty of
bodily resurrection, not human wisdom or status.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Isaiah 29:14 (God confounding worldly wisdom, quoted in chapter 1);
Genesis 2:24 (the "one flesh" union underlying Paul's sexual ethics).

### Distinctive Features
1 Corinthians contains the most detailed New Testament treatment of
spiritual gifts and orderly worship, the classic biblical statement on
love (chapter 13), and the most extensive argument for bodily
resurrection in the New Testament (chapter 15).

## 2 CORINTHIANS

### Introduction and Outline
Paul's most personal and emotionally vulnerable letter, written after a
painful visit and severe letter, defending his apostolic authority and
ministry style against rival "super-apostles" who had undermined him in
Corinth. Outline: comfort and ministry integrity (1-7), the collection
for Jerusalem (8-9), defense of apostolic authority (10-13).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The tone shifts noticeably between the reconciled warmth of chapters
1-9 and the sharper, more defensive tone of chapters 10-13, leading many
scholars to see the letter addressing a partially resolved but still
tense situation.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Comfort and ministry | 1-7 | Reconciliation, true ministry, integrity |
| The collection | 8-9 | Generosity for the Jerusalem church |
| Defense of apostleship | 10-13 | Confronting false apostles |

### Summary of Content
Paul opens by explaining his changed travel plans and the comfort God
gave him amid severe affliction, then describes genuine apostolic
ministry as marked by suffering, transparency, and reliance on God's
power rather than worldly credentials. Two chapters urge generous,
voluntary giving toward the Jerusalem collection. The closing section
sharply defends Paul's authority against rival teachers who boasted in
credentials and eloquence, with Paul instead "boasting" in his
weaknesses.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: genuine ministry is marked by weakness and dependence on God's
power, not worldly credentials. Purpose: to restore Paul's relationship
with the Corinthians and reassert his legitimate apostolic authority.
Application: readers learn that God's power is perfected in weakness,
and that generosity and integrity, not impressive credentials, mark
authentic ministry.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 5 (the ministry of reconciliation); chapter 12 (Paul's thorn in
the flesh and God's sufficient grace).

### Key Passages
2 Corinthians 4:7-9 (treasure in jars of clay); 2 Corinthians 5:17-21
(new creation and the ministry of reconciliation); 2 Corinthians 12:9
("my grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in
weakness").

### Main Teaching
True Christian ministry is validated by suffering, transparency, and
dependence on God's grace rather than by rhetorical skill or worldly
status.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Exodus 34:29-35 (Moses' veiled glory, which Paul contrasts with the
unveiled glory believers now behold, in chapter 3).

### Distinctive Features
2 Corinthians is Paul's most autobiographical letter, offering the
fullest New Testament glimpse into an apostle's personal sufferings,
emotional vulnerability, and the concrete hardships of his ministry.

## GALATIANS

### Introduction and Outline
A sharp, urgent letter confronting "Judaizing" teachers who insisted
Gentile believers must be circumcised and keep the Mosaic law to be
truly saved, threatening to distort the gospel of grace itself.
Outline: Paul's defense of his apostolic authority and gospel (1-2),
justification by faith not law (3-4), freedom and life in the Spirit
(5-6).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The letter moves from personal/historical defense (establishing Paul's
gospel came directly from Christ, not human teachers) to theological
argument (justification by faith, illustrated from Abraham and the
law's temporary purpose) to practical exhortation (Spirit-led freedom
versus both legalism and license).

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Personal defense | 1-2 | Paul's gospel and apostolic authority |
| Doctrinal argument | 3-4 | Justification by faith, the law's purpose |
| Practical application | 5-6 | Freedom, walking by the Spirit |

### Summary of Content
Paul recounts his direct call and gospel's divine origin, and a public
confrontation with Peter over compromised table fellowship with
Gentiles. He then argues from Abraham's example and the law's temporary,
custodial role that justification has always been by faith, not law-
keeping, and that believers are now full heirs and children of God
through the Spirit. The closing chapters warn against using Christian
freedom as license for sin while calling believers to walk by the
Spirit, bearing his fruit rather than the flesh's works.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: justification is by faith in Christ alone, not by works of the
law. Purpose: to correct a serious doctrinal distortion threatening the
Galatian churches' grasp of the gospel. Application: readers are warned
against adding any requirement to faith alone for salvation, and are
called to walk in Spirit-empowered freedom rather than either legalism
or license.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 3 (justification by faith, Abraham's example); chapter 5
(freedom and the fruit of the Spirit).

### Key Passages
Galatians 2:16 (justified by faith, not works of the law); Galatians
2:20 ("I have been crucified with Christ..."); Galatians 5:22-23 (the
fruit of the Spirit).

### Main Teaching
Salvation and standing before God are secured by faith in Christ alone;
adding any requirement of law-keeping distorts and nullifies the
gospel of grace.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Genesis 15:6 (Abraham's faith credited as righteousness, Paul's central
proof text); Habakkuk 2:4 ("the righteous shall live by faith," quoted
in Galatians 3:11).

### Distinctive Features
Galatians is Paul's most polemical letter, opening without his usual
thanksgiving, and contains his most direct autobiographical account of
his conversion and early apostolic ministry alongside the classic list
of the fruit of the Spirit.

## EPHESIANS

### Introduction and Outline
A circular letter (likely intended for multiple churches in the region)
presenting the cosmic scope of God's plan in Christ and the church's
identity as the unified body bringing Jew and Gentile together.
Outline: the believer's position in Christ (1-3), the believer's walk
in unity, holiness, and spiritual warfare (4-6).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The letter's structure moves from doctrine (who believers are in
Christ) to practice (how believers should therefore live) — a pattern
common across several of Paul's letters, but especially pronounced
here, with chapter 4 opening on the phrase "walk in a manner worthy of
the calling."

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Position in Christ | 1-3 | Election, redemption, unity of Jew and Gentile |
| Practice in Christ | 4-6 | Unity, holiness, household duties, spiritual armor |

### Summary of Content
Ephesians opens with a sweeping doxology tracing God's plan from
eternal election through redemption in Christ to the sealing of the
Spirit, followed by Paul's prayer for the readers' spiritual
understanding. Chapter 2 describes salvation by grace through faith and
the reconciliation of Jew and Gentile into one new humanity in Christ.
The second half calls believers to unity, personal holiness, healthy
household relationships, and standing firm in spiritual warfare using
the full armor of God.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: God's eternal plan unites all things in Christ, especially Jew
and Gentile in one church. Purpose: to ground believers' identity and
unity in God's cosmic plan and to call them to live worthy of that
calling. Application: readers find their identity secure in Christ and
are called to pursue unity, holiness, and spiritual readiness for
warfare against evil.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 2 (salvation by grace, Jew-Gentile unity); chapter 6 (the armor
of God).

### Key Passages
Ephesians 2:8-10 (salvation by grace through faith, for good works);
Ephesians 2:14 (Christ as "our peace," breaking down the dividing
wall); Ephesians 6:10-18 (the full armor of God).

### Main Teaching
Believers are saved entirely by grace, united into one body across
former ethnic division, and called to live out that unity and holiness
while standing firm against spiritual opposition.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Genesis 2:24 (marriage union Paul applies typologically to Christ and
the church in chapter 5); Isaiah 59:17 (imagery behind the "armor of
God" in chapter 6).

### Distinctive Features
Ephesians contains some of the New Testament's most exalted, doxological
language, the clearest statement of salvation by grace through faith
apart from works (2:8-9), and the classic passage on spiritual warfare
and the armor of God.

## PHILIPPIANS

### Introduction and Outline
A warm, joyful letter written from prison to a beloved supporting
church, thanking them for their partnership and encouraging joy and
unity despite Paul's circumstances and the church's internal tensions.
Outline: joy in partnership and suffering (1), the example of Christ's
humility (2), pursuing Christ above all (3), contentment and peace (4).

### Structure and Major Divisions
Despite being written from prison, the letter is unified by a
recurring theme of joy/rejoicing appearing throughout each section,
regardless of circumstance.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Joy in partnership | 1 | Paul's imprisonment, the advance of the gospel |
| Christ's example | 2 | The Christ hymn, humility, unity |
| Pursuing Christ | 3 | Paul's testimony, pressing toward the goal |
| Contentment | 4 | Peace, provision, final thanksgiving |

### Summary of Content
Paul rejoices that his imprisonment has actually advanced the gospel,
and urges the Philippians toward unity through humility, pointing to
Christ's self-emptying incarnation and exaltation as the supreme
example. He warns against those trusting in religious credentials,
holding up his own former credentials as loss compared to knowing
Christ, and closes with instruction on contentment, prayer, and
gratitude for the Philippians' financial partnership in his ministry.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: joy and contentment in Christ transcend circumstances. Purpose:
to thank the Philippians for their support, address internal disunity,
and model joyful, Christ-centered perspective amid suffering.
Application: readers learn to find joy independent of circumstance,
pursue humility after Christ's example, and hold worldly credentials
loosely compared to knowing Christ.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 2 (the Christ hymn of humility and exaltation); chapter 3
(Paul's testimony and pursuit of Christ).

### Key Passages
Philippians 1:21 ("to live is Christ, and to die is gain"); Philippians
2:5-11 (the kenosis hymn of Christ's humility); Philippians 4:11-13
(contentment in all circumstances); Philippians 4:6-7 (peace through
prayer).

### Main Teaching
True joy and contentment are found in knowing and imitating Christ's
humility, regardless of external circumstance, status, or suffering.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Isaiah 45:23 (every knee bowing, echoed in the Christ hymn's climax).

### Distinctive Features
Philippians contains one of the earliest and most theologically dense
Christological hymns in the New Testament (2:5-11), and is notable for
its persistent tone of joy despite being written from imprisonment.

## COLOSSIANS

### Introduction and Outline
Written to confront an early syncretistic false teaching combining
Jewish legalism, mysticism, and perhaps proto-Gnostic ideas that
diminished Christ's supremacy, this letter presents the most exalted
New Testament statement of Christ's cosmic preeminence. Outline: the
supremacy of Christ (1-2), the practical implications of life in
Christ (3-4).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The letter's doctrinal argument (Christ's supremacy over creation, the
church, and every spiritual power) directly undergirds its practical
warnings against false asceticism and mysticism, and its household
instructions.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Christ's supremacy | 1-2 | Cosmic Christology, warning against false teaching |
| Life in Christ | 3-4 | Practical holiness, household duties |

### Summary of Content
Paul presents Christ as supreme over all creation, the head of the
church, and the one in whom the fullness of deity dwells bodily,
directly confronting teaching that treated angelic powers, dietary
rules, or special visionary experiences as necessary supplements to
Christ. Because believers are spiritually complete in Christ, chapters
3-4 call them to put off the old self and put on Christlike character,
extending this transformation into household and workplace
relationships.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: Christ is supreme and sufficient over every rival power or
philosophy. Purpose: to refute a specific false teaching diminishing
Christ's adequacy and to ground practical holiness in union with him.
Application: readers are warned against any teaching that adds
requirements to Christ's sufficiency and are called to let his
supremacy shape every relationship.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 1 (the supremacy of Christ); chapter 2 (warning against hollow
philosophy and legalism).

### Key Passages
Colossians 1:15-20 (the Christ hymn: image of the invisible God,
supreme over creation); Colossians 2:9-10 ("in him the whole fullness
of deity dwells bodily... you have been filled in him"); Colossians
3:1-3 (set your minds on things above).

### Main Teaching
Christ alone is sufficient for salvation and spiritual completeness;
no additional philosophy, ritual, or mystical experience is needed
alongside him.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Genesis 1:1 and Proverbs 8:22-31 (wisdom/creation themes echoed in the
Colossians 1 Christ hymn).

### Distinctive Features
Colossians contains one of the New Testament's highest Christological
statements (1:15-20), closely parallels Ephesians in its household
codes, and directly names and refutes a specific local heresy more than
most other Pauline letters.

## 1 THESSALONIANS

### Introduction and Outline
Likely Paul's earliest surviving letter, written to encourage a young,
persecuted church and clarify confusion about believers who had died
before Christ's return. Outline: thanksgiving and Paul's ministry
example (1-3), practical holiness and hope (4-5).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The letter moves from warm personal reflection on Paul's founding visit
and pastoral concern to specific teaching correcting eschatological
confusion and reinforcing practical holiness.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Pastoral reflection | 1-3 | Paul's ministry example, concern for the church |
| Practical teaching | 4-5 | Holiness, the Lord's return, church life |

### Summary of Content
Paul recalls his and his companions' sincere, sacrificial ministry
among the Thessalonians and expresses relief and joy at their continued
faith despite persecution. He then addresses confusion about believers
who have died before Christ's return, assuring the church that the dead
in Christ will be raised and reunited with living believers at his
coming, and closes with practical exhortations toward holy, watchful,
orderly living.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: steadfast hope in Christ's return sustains holy living amid
persecution. Purpose: to encourage a suffering young church and correct
misunderstanding about the fate of believers who die before Christ
returns. Application: readers find comfort regarding believers who have
died and are called to live alertly and holily in light of Christ's
promised return.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 4 (comfort concerning those who have died, the Lord's return).

### Key Passages
1 Thessalonians 4:13-18 (the resurrection and rapture of believers);
1 Thessalonians 5:16-18 ("rejoice always, pray without ceasing, give
thanks in all circumstances").

### Main Teaching
Believers who die in Christ are not lost but will be raised, and this
hope should produce watchful, holy living rather than either despair or
speculation.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Daniel 12:1-2 (resurrection hope echoed in Paul's teaching on the dead
in Christ).

### Distinctive Features
Likely the earliest New Testament document, 1 Thessalonians offers an
unusually personal window into Paul's pastoral affection and contains
one of the clearest New Testament passages on the resurrection and
return of Christ.

---

## 2 THESSALONIANS

### Introduction and Outline
Written soon after 1 Thessalonians to correct a further
misunderstanding — the false claim that the Day of the Lord had already
come — and to address believers who had stopped working in expectation
of Christ's imminent return. Outline: encouragement amid persecution
(1), correction concerning the Day of the Lord (2), exhortation against
idleness (3).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The letter's central theological correction (2) is framed by pastoral
encouragement (1) and practical discipline regarding idle believers (3),
showing how eschatological confusion had produced real behavioral
problems in the church.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapter | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Encouragement | 1 | Comfort amid persecution, coming judgment |
| Correction | 2 | The Day of the Lord has not yet come |
| Exhortation | 3 | Discipline for idleness, steady work |

### Summary of Content
Paul reassures the persecuted church that God will ultimately repay
their afflicters and vindicate the faithful. He corrects the false
teaching that the Day of the Lord had already arrived, explaining that
a specific apostasy and the revealing of "the man of lawlessness" must
precede it. The letter closes with firm instruction to withdraw
fellowship from believers who had abandoned work in misplaced
end-times expectation, urging quiet, disciplined labor instead.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: correct eschatological understanding produces steady, disciplined
living rather than passive speculation. Purpose: to correct
misinformation about the Day of the Lord and address resulting idleness.
Application: readers are warned against date-setting or passive waiting
and called to steady, productive living regardless of end-times
speculation.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 2 (the man of lawlessness and the Day of the Lord).

### Key Passages
2 Thessalonians 2:3-4 (the apostasy and man of lawlessness preceding
the Day of the Lord); 2 Thessalonians 3:10 ("if anyone is not willing to
work, let him not eat").

### Main Teaching
Believers should not be shaken by claims that the Day of the Lord has
already come, and should respond to end-times hope with disciplined,
productive living, not idle speculation.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Daniel 7 and 11 (apocalyptic imagery informing Paul's description of
the man of lawlessness).

### Distinctive Features
2 Thessalonians contains one of the New Testament's most detailed, if
debated, passages on end-times events preceding Christ's return, and
uniquely addresses a specific behavioral problem (idleness) stemming
directly from eschatological confusion.

## 1 TIMOTHY

### Introduction and Outline
A pastoral letter to Paul's younger colleague Timothy, left in charge
at Ephesus, providing instruction on combating false teaching, church
order, and qualifications for leadership. Outline: charge concerning
false teachers (1), instructions for worship and church roles (2-3),
Timothy's personal conduct (4), church discipline and care (5-6).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The letter alternates between doctrinal warning (opposing false
teaching) and practical church order (worship, leadership
qualifications, care for widows), reflecting its purpose as an
administrative and pastoral manual for a functioning church.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| False teaching | 1 | Warning against false teachers and the law |
| Church order | 2-3 | Worship, qualifications for overseers and deacons |
| Timothy's conduct | 4 | Personal godliness, teaching |
| Church discipline | 5-6 | Widows, elders, wealth, final charge |

### Summary of Content
Paul charges Timothy to oppose false teachers preoccupied with
speculative myths and misuse of the law, then provides instructions for
orderly public worship and clear qualifications for church overseers
and deacons. Timothy is urged to model godliness despite his youth, and
the letter closes with instructions on caring for widows, honoring
faithful elders, and warning against the love of money.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: sound doctrine and godly order sustain a healthy church. Purpose:
to equip Timothy to combat false teaching and organize church life
well. Application: readers learn the character qualifications required
of church leaders and the priority of sound doctrine over speculative
controversy.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 3 (qualifications for overseers and deacons).

### Key Passages
1 Timothy 2:5 ("there is one God, and one mediator... Christ Jesus");
1 Timothy 3:1-13 (qualifications for overseers and deacons); 1 Timothy
6:10 ("the love of money is a root of all kinds of evils").

### Main Teaching
Healthy churches require sound doctrine, godly and qualified leadership,
and right priorities regarding wealth and controversy.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Genesis 2 and 3 (background for Paul's discussion of creation order in
chapter 2).

### Distinctive Features
1 Timothy is one of the three "Pastoral Epistles" (with 2 Timothy and
Titus) and contains the New Testament's clearest lists of qualifications
for church overseers and deacons.

## 2 TIMOTHY

### Introduction and Outline
Paul's final letter, written from prison shortly before his execution,
passing the torch of ministry to Timothy with urgent charges to remain
faithful amid suffering, abandonment, and coming apostasy. Outline:
call to endurance (1), the pattern of a faithful minister (2), warning
about the last days (3), Paul's final charge (4).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The letter's deeply personal, valedictory tone (Paul anticipating his
own death) frames each section's charge to Timothy toward faithful
endurance and sound teaching.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapter | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Call to endurance | 1 | Fan into flame the gift of God, don't be ashamed |
| Faithful minister | 2 | Suffer as a good soldier, handle the word rightly |
| Warning | 3 | Difficult times, Scripture's sufficiency |
| Final charge | 4 | Preach the word, Paul's final testimony |

### Summary of Content
Paul urges Timothy not to be ashamed of the gospel or of Paul's
imprisonment, but to guard the sound teaching entrusted to him and
suffer for it as a good soldier of Christ. He warns of increasing
apostasy and moral decline in the last days, grounding Timothy's
defense in the sufficiency and inspiration of Scripture. The closing
charge — "preach the word, in season and out of season" — is followed
by Paul's own reflection that he has finished the race, and personal
requests and greetings.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: faithful endurance in ministry despite suffering and coming
apostasy. Purpose: to pass ministry leadership to Timothy and urge his
continued faithfulness after Paul's death. Application: readers are
called to guard sound doctrine, endure suffering for the gospel, and
trust Scripture's sufficiency for every good work.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 3 (Scripture's inspiration and sufficiency); chapter 4 (Paul's
final charge and testimony).

### Key Passages
2 Timothy 2:15 ("rightly handling the word of truth"); 2 Timothy
3:16-17 (all Scripture is God-breathed and sufficient); 2 Timothy
4:7-8 ("I have fought the good fight... finished the race").

### Main Teaching
Ministers must guard and faithfully proclaim sound, Scripture-grounded
teaching, prepared to suffer for it, trusting Scripture's full
sufficiency against error.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Numbers 16 (Korah's rebellion, referenced as a type of apostasy in
chapter 2's imagery of vessels for honor and dishonor).

### Distinctive Features
2 Timothy is widely regarded as Paul's final letter before his
execution, giving it unusual personal and valedictory weight, and
contains the New Testament's most explicit statement on the divine
inspiration of Scripture (3:16-17).

## TITUS

### Introduction and Outline
A brief pastoral letter to Titus, left to organize the young, morally
challenged churches of Crete, emphasizing sound doctrine expressed
through good works. Outline: qualifications for elders (1), sound
teaching for various groups (2), the basis and fruit of grace (3).

### Structure and Major Divisions
Each chapter pairs doctrinal grounding with its corresponding practical
outworking — qualified leadership prevents false teaching (1), sound
doctrine shapes every age and social group (2), and grace produces good
works (3).

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapter | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Church leadership | 1 | Elder qualifications, silencing false teachers |
| Sound teaching | 2 | Instruction for older/younger men and women, slaves |
| Grace and works | 3 | Salvation by grace, devotion to good works |

### Summary of Content
Titus is instructed to appoint qualified elders across Crete's towns
to counter false teachers and the island's reputation for dishonesty.
Paul provides teaching appropriate to different groups within the
church — older and younger men and women, servants — all grounded in
sound doctrine. The letter closes by grounding good works not in
earning salvation but in grateful response to God's grace already
given.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: sound doctrine produces good works across every group in the
church. Purpose: to help Titus organize healthy church leadership and
teaching on Crete. Application: readers learn that grace, not
legalism, is the true engine of good works, and that qualified,
doctrinally sound leadership protects a church's health.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 2 (sound teaching for all groups); chapter 3 (grace and good
works).

### Key Passages
Titus 1:5-9 (qualifications for elders); Titus 2:11-14 (the grace of
God that trains us to renounce ungodliness); Titus 3:5 ("he saved us,
not because of works... but according to his own mercy").

### Main Teaching
Sound doctrine and qualified church leadership, grounded in grace
rather than legalism, produce genuine good works in every group within
the church.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Deuteronomy (background for Paul's language of God's people being
"zealous for good works," echoing Israel's calling).

### Distinctive Features
Titus is the shortest of the three Pastoral Epistles and contains one
of the New Testament's most concise summaries of grace-based salvation
and its ethical fruit (2:11-14).

## PHILEMON

### Introduction and Outline
Paul's shortest and most personal letter, a private appeal to Philemon
on behalf of Onesimus, a runaway (and likely thieving) slave who had
become a believer under Paul's ministry in prison. Outline:
thanksgiving for Philemon (1-7), appeal for Onesimus (8-21), closing
greetings (22-25).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The letter is a single sustained, tactful appeal rather than having
distinct doctrinal and practical sections, using praise, gentle
pressure, and personal relationship to persuade rather than command.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Verses | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Thanksgiving | 1-7 | Praise for Philemon's love and faith |
| Appeal | 8-21 | Request to receive Onesimus as a brother |
| Closing | 22-25 | Travel plans, greetings |

### Summary of Content
Paul writes on behalf of Onesimus, urging Philemon to receive his
former slave back not merely as property but as a beloved brother in
Christ, offering to personally repay any debt Onesimus owes. Rather
than commanding Philemon by apostolic authority, Paul appeals to their
relationship and to the transformative implications of the gospel for
how believers treat one another.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: the gospel transforms social relationships, including master and
slave, into genuine spiritual family. Purpose: to reconcile Philemon
and Onesimus and model gospel-shaped relationships. Application:
readers see how the gospel reshapes social hierarchies and are called
to extend forgiveness and family-like acceptance to those the world
would exclude.

### Key Chapters
The entire short letter functions as a single unit; verses 15-18
contain its persuasive core.

### Key Passages
Philemon 15-16 ("no longer as a slave but... a beloved brother");
Philemon 18 ("if he has wronged you... charge that to my account").

### Main Teaching
The gospel transforms how believers regard one another across social
and economic hierarchy, calling for reconciliation and family-like
acceptance rather than mere legal or social status.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Leviticus 25 (biblical background on servitude and release, relevant
to the letter's implicit challenge to slavery's assumptions).

### Distinctive Features
Philemon is Paul's shortest preserved letter and his only surviving
purely personal correspondence to an individual about a private matter,
offering a uniquely intimate window into how the gospel was expected to
reshape everyday social relationships.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

Acts models cross-cultural gospel expansion across ethnic and religious
boundaries, directly relevant to any missionary navigating similar
boundary-crossing today. The Pauline epistles collectively model how to
address theological error, church conflict, and ethical formation in
diverse local churches — Galatians and Colossians in particular offer
direct precedent for confronting legalistic or syncretistic
distortions of the gospel that missionaries frequently encounter in
new church plants.

## Discussion prompts for training

- How does the Jerusalem Council's decision (Acts 15) inform how you
  would handle a similar question about which cultural requirements are
  and are not essential to the gospel in your own context?
- Compare Galatians (confronting legalism) and Colossians (confronting
  mysticism/syncretism) — which distortion do you more often encounter
  in your ministry context, and how would Paul's specific argument in
  each letter apply?
- How does Philemon's approach to slavery (transforming the
  relationship rather than immediately abolishing the institution)
  inform how you might address deeply entrenched social structures in a
  new church plant?
