# Old Testament Survey: The Major Prophets

## ISAIAH

### Introduction and Outline
Isaiah, ministering in Judah across roughly forty years spanning
several kings' reigns, delivers a sweeping message of judgment on sin
and the surrounding nations alongside unparalleled promises of future
redemption, comfort, and a coming Messianic servant. Outline: judgment
oracles (1-39), comfort and redemption (40-55), future glory and final
exhortations (56-66).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The book divides broadly into two large movements often compared to the
two Testaments: judgment-dominant chapters (1-39, corresponding roughly
to the Old Testament's length) and comfort/redemption-dominant chapters
(40-66), with an extended narrative interlude concerning Hezekiah
(36-39) forming a hinge between them.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Judgment | 1-35 | Oracles against Judah and the nations, the Day of the Lord |
| Historical interlude | 36-39 | Hezekiah, Sennacherib's invasion, Babylonian envoys |
| Comfort | 40-55 | Comfort for exiles, the Servant Songs, redemption |
| Future glory | 56-66 | New heavens and new earth, final exhortations |

### Summary of Content
Isaiah confronts Judah's sin and hollow religious ritual while
pronouncing judgment on surrounding nations, all set within the larger
framework of the coming "Day of the Lord." A historical section
recounts Hezekiah's crisis during Assyrian invasion, setting up the
book's turn toward comfort: promises of restoration from coming
Babylonian exile, culminating in the Servant Songs describing a
suffering figure who bears his people's sin. The closing chapters
envision ultimate restoration and a new heavens and new earth.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: God judges sin righteously but promises ultimate comfort and
redemption through a coming Suffering Servant. Purpose: to call Judah
to repentance while assuring a remnant of future restoration beyond
judgment. Application: readers see both the seriousness of hollow
religious practice without genuine obedience and the certainty of God's
redemptive comfort, ultimately fulfilled in Christ.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 6 (Isaiah's call and vision of God's holiness); chapter 53
(the Suffering Servant); chapters 65-66 (new heavens and new earth).

### Key Passages
Isaiah 6:3 ("holy, holy, holy is the Lord of hosts"); Isaiah 7:14 (the
virgin birth prophecy); Isaiah 9:6-7 (the child called "Mighty God");
Isaiah 53:5 ("he was wounded for our transgressions").

### Main Teaching
God righteously judges persistent sin and empty religious ritual, yet
provides ultimate redemption through a promised Suffering Servant who
bears his people's iniquity.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Matthew 1:23 (Isaiah 7:14 applied to Christ's virgin birth); Acts
8:32-35 (Isaiah 53 explicitly applied to Jesus by Philip); Luke 4:18-19
(Jesus reading from Isaiah 61 in Nazareth).

### Distinctive Features
Isaiah is the most extensively quoted Old Testament prophetic book in
the New Testament, contains the Servant Songs (42, 49, 50, 53) most
directly fulfilled in Christ's death, and spans the widest historical
and thematic range of any prophetic book.

## JEREMIAH

### Introduction and Outline
Jeremiah, the "weeping prophet," ministered during Judah's final decades
before the Babylonian exile, persistently calling the nation to
repentance while facing severe personal persecution for his unwelcome
message. Outline: calls to repentance (1-25), Jeremiah's conflicts and
Jerusalem's fall (26-45), oracles against the nations (46-51), historical
appendix (52).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The book alternates between poetic prophetic oracles calling for
repentance and narrative accounts of Jeremiah's persecution and
Jerusalem's final years, closing with oracles against foreign nations
and a historical appendix recording the city's actual fall.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Calls to repentance | 1-25 | Warnings, the temple sermon, the new covenant promise |
| Conflicts and fall | 26-45 | Persecution of Jeremiah, Jerusalem's siege and fall |
| Nations | 46-51 | Judgment oracles against surrounding nations |
| Appendix | 52 | Historical account of Jerusalem's destruction |

### Summary of Content
Jeremiah repeatedly warns Judah of coming judgment for persistent
idolatry and covenant unfaithfulness, famously promising a future new
covenant written on the heart rather than external tablets. His
unwelcome message brings severe persecution — imprisonment, a plot on
his life, being thrown into a cistern — while he witnesses Jerusalem's
final siege and destruction by Babylon exactly as he had warned. Oracles
against surrounding nations and a historical appendix confirming
Jerusalem's fall close the book.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: persistent covenant unfaithfulness brings certain judgment, but
God promises a future new covenant of internal transformation. Purpose:
to record Judah's final warnings before exile and preserve the promise
of ultimate restoration through a new covenant. Application: readers
learn the cost of faithful proclamation amid rejection, and the hope of
God's promised internal, heart-level transformation fulfilled in
Christ.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 1 (Jeremiah's call); chapter 31 (the new covenant promise);
chapter 38 (Jeremiah in the cistern).

### Key Passages
Jeremiah 1:5 ("before I formed you in the womb I knew you"); Jeremiah
29:11 ("I know the plans I have for you... plans for welfare and not
for evil"); Jeremiah 31:33 (the new covenant "written on their
hearts").

### Main Teaching
God's covenant patience has limits and persistent unfaithfulness brings
real judgment, yet he promises a future new covenant that transforms
his people internally rather than merely externally.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Hebrews 8:8-12 (Jeremiah 31's new covenant quoted at length and applied
to Christ); Luke 22:20 (Jesus explicitly invoking "the new covenant in
my blood" at the Last Supper).

### Distinctive Features
Jeremiah is the Old Testament's longest prophetic book by word count,
offers unusually detailed autobiographical material about a prophet's
personal suffering and emotional struggle, and contains the pivotal new
covenant prophecy the New Testament identifies as fulfilled in Christ.

## LAMENTATIONS

### Introduction and Outline
A collection of five poetic laments mourning Jerusalem's destruction,
traditionally attributed to Jeremiah, giving voice to grief over the
city's fall while still affirming God's faithfulness. Outline: the
city's desolation (1), God's anger (2), the sufferer's hope amid
affliction (3), the siege's horrors (4), a closing communal prayer (5).

### Structure and Major Divisions
Four of the five chapters are constructed as acrostic poems (following
the Hebrew alphabet), a deliberate literary form suggesting the
completeness of the grief being expressed, with chapter 3 (the
longest and most developed acrostic) forming the book's theological
center.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapter | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Desolation | 1 | Jerusalem's abandonment and shame |
| Divine anger | 2 | God's judgment described in stark detail |
| Hope amid suffering | 3 | Affliction and renewed hope in God's faithfulness |
| Siege horrors | 4 | The famine and destruction of the siege |
| Communal prayer | 5 | A closing plea for restoration |

### Summary of Content
Each poem mourns a different dimension of Jerusalem's fall — its
physical desolation, the theological reality that God himself brought
this judgment, the devastating human cost of the siege — while chapter
3 pivots unexpectedly to affirm that God's mercies are "new every
morning," grounding hope precisely in the middle of unrelieved grief.
The closing chapter offers a communal prayer pleading for God's
remembrance and restoration.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: even amid deserved and devastating judgment, God's faithfulness
and mercy remain the true ground of hope. Purpose: to give the exiled
community a shared, structured language for grieving Jerusalem's fall
without denying God's justice in bringing it. Application: readers
learn a model for honest, structured grief that does not deny God's
justice while still clinging to his covenant faithfulness.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 3 (hope amid affliction, "great is your faithfulness").

### Key Passages
Lamentations 3:22-23 ("his mercies... are new every morning; great is
your faithfulness"); Lamentations 3:24 ("the Lord is my portion...
therefore I will hope in him").

### Main Teaching
Honest, structured grief over judgment and loss can coexist with
genuine hope, because God's faithfulness and mercy remain constant even
in the midst of deserved judgment.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Jeremiah 52 (the historical account of the destruction Lamentations
mourns); 2 Corinthians 1:3-4 (God as the "God of all comfort," echoing
Lamentations' pattern of grief transformed by hope).

### Distinctive Features
Lamentations is composed almost entirely as structured acrostic poetry
(a deliberate literary discipline imposed on raw grief), and its
central affirmation of God's faithfulness ("great is your faithfulness")
has become one of Scripture's most widely known expressions of hope
amid suffering.

## EZEKIEL

### Introduction and Outline
Ezekiel, a priest-turned-prophet exiled to Babylon, receives vivid
visionary experiences proclaiming Jerusalem's coming (and then
accomplished) destruction, followed by promises of Israel's future
restoration and a visionary new temple. Outline: judgment on Judah and
Jerusalem (1-24), oracles against foreign nations (25-32), restoration
promises (33-48).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The book pivots at the news of Jerusalem's actual fall (chapter 33),
shifting from judgment oracles to messages of hope and restoration,
closing with an elaborate visionary description of a future temple and
restored land.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Judgment on Judah | 1-24 | Ezekiel's call, symbolic acts, the glory departing the temple |
| Nations | 25-32 | Oracles against surrounding nations |
| Restoration | 33-48 | The valley of dry bones, new covenant, the new temple |

### Summary of Content
Ezekiel receives a dramatic inaugural vision of God's glory and is
called to warn Jerusalem through both spoken oracles and unusual
symbolic acts (lying on his side for extended periods, shaving his
head), climaxing in a vision of God's glory departing the corrupted
temple. After news arrives that Jerusalem has fallen exactly as
warned, the book turns toward hope: the famous vision of dry bones
brought to life symbolizing national restoration, a promise of a new
heart and spirit, and an extended, detailed vision of a future temple
and restored land.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: God's glory departs from persistent, uncorrected sin, but he
promises future restoration and a transformed heart for his people.
Purpose: to explain Jerusalem's judgment as deserved while assuring
exiles of eventual national and spiritual restoration. Application:
readers learn that God's presence is not to be presumed upon amid
unrepented sin, while also receiving hope for genuine inner
transformation, not just external restoration.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 1 (Ezekiel's inaugural vision); chapter 37 (the valley of dry
bones); chapters 40-48 (the new temple vision).

### Key Passages
Ezekiel 18:20 ("the soul who sins shall die," individual
responsibility); Ezekiel 36:26 ("I will give you a new heart and a new
spirit"); Ezekiel 37:1-14 (the valley of dry bones).

### Main Teaching
God's glory withdraws from persistent, unrepented sin, yet he remains
committed to ultimately restoring his people, giving them a genuinely
new heart rather than merely returning them to their former condition.

### Selected Related Scriptures
John 3:5 (Jesus' language of new birth "of water and the Spirit,"
echoing Ezekiel 36); Revelation 21-22 (a visionary restored city and
temple paralleling Ezekiel's closing vision).

### Distinctive Features
Ezekiel contains some of Scripture's most vivid and unusual prophetic
imagery and symbolic acts, the extended vision of a detailed future
temple (40-48) unmatched elsewhere in Scripture, and the closing verse
naming the restored city "The Lord Is There."

## DANIEL

### Introduction and Outline
Daniel, a Judean exile serving in the Babylonian and Persian courts,
combines court narratives demonstrating God's sovereignty over pagan
empires with apocalyptic visions of future kingdoms and the ultimate
establishment of God's eternal kingdom. Outline: court narratives
(1-6), apocalyptic visions (7-12).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The book divides cleanly between narrative chapters recounting
Daniel's and his friends' experiences in the Babylonian and Persian
courts (1-6) and increasingly complex visionary material revealing the
course of future world empires and God's ultimate kingdom (7-12).

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Court narratives | 1-6 | Daniel's training, the fiery furnace, the lions' den |
| Apocalyptic visions | 7-12 | Four kingdoms, the seventy weeks, the time of the end |

### Summary of Content
Daniel and his three friends maintain covenant faithfulness while
serving in the Babylonian court, famously surviving the fiery furnace
and the lions' den, and interpreting dreams that reveal God's
sovereignty over the rise and fall of pagan empires. The book's second
half shifts to Daniel's own visions, including a sequence of four
successive world kingdoms and the prophecy of "seventy weeks"
concerning Jerusalem's future, culminating in visions of end-time
conflict and the final resurrection.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: God sovereignly rules over the rise and fall of human empires
and will ultimately establish his own eternal kingdom. Purpose: to
encourage exiled Jews that God remains sovereign despite pagan
political dominance, and to reveal the future course of world
history culminating in God's kingdom. Application: readers learn
faithful, uncompromising conduct is possible even under hostile
political power, grounded in confidence in God's ultimate sovereignty
over history.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 3 (the fiery furnace); chapter 6 (the lions' den); chapter 9
(the seventy weeks prophecy).

### Key Passages
Daniel 2:20-21 ("he changes times and seasons; he removes kings and
sets up kings"); Daniel 3:17-18 ("our God... is able to deliver
us... but if not..."); Daniel 7:13-14 ("one like a son of man").

### Main Teaching
God remains sovereign over every human empire and political power, and
faithful obedience is possible and worth maintaining even under
hostile, pagan political dominance.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Matthew 24:15 (Jesus referencing Daniel's "abomination of
desolation"); Revelation 13 (beast imagery drawing directly on
Daniel's visions).

### Distinctive Features
Daniel is written partly in Hebrew and partly in Aramaic, contains some
of the Bible's most detailed long-range predictive prophecy, and
supplies the "son of man" title Jesus applies to himself throughout the
Gospels.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

Daniel's model of uncompromising faithfulness within a hostile pagan
court offers direct precedent for believers serving under
restricted-access or officially hostile political systems today,
demonstrating that faithful presence and quiet excellence (Daniel's
"excellent spirit") can coexist with firm, occasionally costly refusal
to compromise on core convictions. Isaiah's Servant Songs and Ezekiel's
promise of a new heart provide missionaries rich vocabulary for
presenting Christ's atoning work and the Spirit's transformative power
to audiences from a wide range of religious backgrounds.

## Discussion prompts for training

- How does Daniel's pattern of respectful excellence combined with firm
  refusal to compromise (chapters 1, 3, 6) provide a model for
  believers serving under hostile or restricted political systems
  today?
- How would you use Ezekiel's promise of "a new heart" (36:26) to
  explain the necessity of inner transformation, not just outward
  religious reform, to someone from a works-based religious
  background?
- Lamentations models grief that doesn't deny God's justice — how might
  this shape how you pastorally accompany believers experiencing
  suffering that is, at least partly, a consequence of their own or
  their community's choices?
