# New Testament Survey: The Four Gospels

Each Gospel below is analyzed using ten standard Bible-survey categories:
introduction and outline, structure and major divisions, book chart
summary, summary of content, theme/purpose/application message, key
chapters, key passages, main teaching, selected related scriptures, and
distinctive features.

---

## MATTHEW

### Introduction and Outline
Matthew presents Jesus as the promised Jewish Messiah and King, tracing
his legal descent from Abraham and David to demonstrate his rightful
claim to Israel's throne. Traditionally attributed to Matthew (Levi),
the tax collector called by Jesus, and written primarily for a Jewish
audience steeped in Old Testament expectation. Basic outline: the
King's arrival and credentials (1-4), the King's message (5-7), the
King's authority demonstrated (8-10), the King's program and growing
opposition (11-16), the King's preparation of his disciples (17-20),
the King's presentation and rejection (21-27), the King's resurrection
and commission (28).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The Gospel is often noted for its five major discourse sections
(chapters 5-7, 10, 13, 18, 24-25), each closing with a variation of
"when Jesus had finished these sayings" — a structure some see as
deliberately echoing the five books of the Torah, reinforcing Matthew's
presentation of Jesus as the greater Moses and fulfillment of the Law.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Advent of the King | 1-4 | Birth, genealogy, forerunner, temptation |
| Message of the King | 5-7 | Sermon on the Mount |
| Credentials of the King | 8-10 | Miracles, sending of the Twelve |
| Rejection of the King | 11-16 | Growing opposition, parables of the kingdom |
| Instruction of the King | 17-20 | Transfiguration, teaching on discipleship |
| Passion of the King | 21-27 | Triumphal entry, Olivet Discourse, crucifixion |
| Resurrection of the King | 28 | Resurrection, Great Commission |

### Summary of Content
Matthew opens with a genealogy and infancy narrative establishing
Jesus' Davidic and Abrahamic credentials, moves through his baptism and
temptation, and then structures his public ministry around alternating
blocks of narrative and extended teaching discourse. The middle section
traces mounting conflict with religious leaders alongside private
instruction of the disciples, culminating in the journey to Jerusalem,
the Passion week, crucifixion, and resurrection, closing with the
Great Commission to make disciples of all nations.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: Jesus is the promised Messiah-King who fulfills the Law and the
Prophets. Purpose: to present a carefully documented case, especially
to a Jewish audience, that Jesus is the long-awaited Christ and to
instruct the church in kingdom ethics and mission. Application: readers
are called to recognize Jesus' authority as King, to live under the
ethical demands of the Sermon on the Mount, and to participate in his
commission to disciple the nations.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 5-7 (the Sermon on the Mount, the ethical constitution of the
kingdom); chapter 13 (the parables of the kingdom); chapter 28 (the
resurrection and Great Commission).

### Key Passages
Matthew 1:21-23 (the announcement of Immanuel); Matthew 5:17 ("I have
not come to abolish [the Law] but to fulfill it"); Matthew 16:16-18
(Peter's confession and the promise concerning the church); Matthew
28:18-20 (the Great Commission).

### Main Teaching
Matthew teaches that Jesus is the rightful, prophesied King of Israel
whose kingdom, though rejected by many of his own generation, is now
extended to all nations through the church, and that discipleship
means both believing his identity and obeying his ethical teaching.

### Selected Related Scriptures
2 Samuel 7:12-16 (the Davidic covenant Jesus fulfills); Isaiah 7:14 and
9:6-7 (Messianic prophecies echoed in the infancy narrative); Daniel
7:13-14 (the "Son of Man" language Jesus applies to himself).

### Distinctive Features
Matthew contains more Old Testament fulfillment citations ("this was to
fulfill what was spoken...") than any other Gospel, organizes teaching
into five major discourses, and uniquely records the visit of the magi,
Joseph's perspective on the nativity, and detailed resurrection
appearance material including the Great Commission.

## MARK

### Introduction and Outline
Mark is widely regarded as the earliest written Gospel and the shortest,
traditionally linked to John Mark and, through him, to Peter's
eyewitness preaching, likely written for a Roman/Gentile audience
unfamiliar with Jewish custom (which Mark periodically explains).
Outline: the servant's preparation (1:1-13), the servant's ministry in
Galilee (1:14-8:30), the servant's journey toward Jerusalem and passion
predictions (8:31-10:52), the servant's final week, death, and
resurrection in Jerusalem (11-16).

### Structure and Major Divisions
Mark is built around brisk, action-oriented narrative rather than long
discourse, frequently using the word "immediately" to propel the
story forward. A structural hinge occurs at Peter's confession in
8:27-30, after which the tone shifts from public Galilean ministry to
private instruction about the necessity of the cross.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Preparation | 1:1-13 | John the Baptist, baptism, temptation |
| Galilean ministry | 1:14-8:30 | Miracles, teaching, growing fame and opposition |
| Journey to Jerusalem | 8:31-10:52 | Three passion predictions, discipleship teaching |
| Passion week | 11-16 | Triumphal entry, trials, crucifixion, resurrection |

### Summary of Content
Mark moves quickly from John the Baptist's ministry to Jesus' baptism
and temptation, then presents a fast-paced account of Jesus' authority
over sickness, demons, nature, and sin during his Galilean ministry.
After Peter's confession that Jesus is the Christ, the narrative shifts
toward Jerusalem, with Jesus repeatedly predicting his suffering, death,
and resurrection while his disciples struggle to understand. The book
closes with the Passion narrative and a comparatively brief resurrection
account.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: Jesus is the suffering Servant and Son of God whose authority is
demonstrated in action. Purpose: to present a vivid, fast-moving account
of Jesus' life and, especially, his suffering, likely to strengthen
persecuted believers by showing that suffering was central to Jesus'
own mission, not a sign of failure. Application: readers are called to
follow a suffering Messiah with the same costly discipleship he
required of his first followers.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 1 (Jesus' authoritative ministry launch); chapter 8 (Peter's
confession and the pivot toward the cross); chapter 15 (the crucifixion).

### Key Passages
Mark 1:1 (the Gospel's opening claim: "the Son of God"); Mark 8:34-38
(the call to costly discipleship); Mark 10:45 ("the Son of Man came...
to give his life as a ransom for many").

### Main Teaching
Mark teaches that Jesus' identity as the Son of God is demonstrated
through authoritative action, and that following him necessarily
involves the way of suffering and self-denial, not triumphal
expectation alone.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Isaiah 53 (the Suffering Servant, echoed in Mark's portrait of Jesus);
Daniel 7:13-14 ("Son of Man" title Mark uses repeatedly).

### Distinctive Features
Mark is the shortest and, by most reconstructions, the earliest Gospel;
it has almost no infancy narrative, moves at a notably rapid narrative
pace, and (in the earliest manuscripts) ends abruptly at 16:8, a
feature that has generated significant textual-critical discussion
about the original ending.

## LUKE

### Introduction and Outline
Luke, a physician and companion of Paul, addresses his orderly account
to "most excellent Theophilus," explicitly stating his purpose of
providing careful, investigated certainty about the things Christian
believers had been taught. Written for a broad, likely Gentile
audience, it presents Jesus as the perfect Son of Man and Savior for
all people. Outline: prologue and infancy narratives (1-2), preparation
for ministry (3-4), Galilean ministry (5-9), the journey to Jerusalem
(9:51-19:27), Passion week (19:28-23), resurrection and ascension (24).

### Structure and Major Divisions
Luke is distinguished by its long "travel narrative" (roughly 9:51 to
19:27), a substantial central section organized loosely around Jesus'
journey toward Jerusalem, containing much of Luke's unique parable
material not found in the other Gospels.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Prologue and infancy | 1-2 | Birth narratives of John and Jesus |
| Preparation | 3-4 | Baptism, genealogy, temptation, Nazareth rejection |
| Galilean ministry | 5-9:50 | Calling disciples, miracles, teaching |
| Journey to Jerusalem | 9:51-19:27 | Extended travel narrative, unique parables |
| Jerusalem and Passion | 19:28-23 | Triumphal entry, Last Supper, trial, crucifixion |
| Resurrection and ascension | 24 | Resurrection appearances, ascension |

### Summary of Content
Luke opens with unusually detailed infancy narratives centered on
Mary, Elizabeth, and the births of John the Baptist and Jesus, then
follows Jesus' Galilean ministry before devoting an extensive central
section to his journey toward Jerusalem, during which Luke includes
material unique among the Gospels — including many of Jesus' best-known
parables. The book concludes with the Passion, resurrection, and
ascension, explicitly setting up Luke's second volume, Acts.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: Jesus is the compassionate Savior of all people, especially the
marginalized. Purpose: to provide an orderly, historically grounded
account confirming the reliability of the Christian message for
Theophilus and, by extension, the wider church. Application: readers
are called to recognize God's concern for the poor, women, outsiders,
and sinners, and to respond to Jesus with the same repentance and joy
Luke's characters model.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 15 (the parables of the lost sheep, lost coin, and prodigal
son); chapter 19 (Zacchaeus and the triumphal entry); chapter 24 (the
resurrection and Emmaus road appearance).

### Key Passages
Luke 1:1-4 (Luke's stated historical purpose); Luke 4:18-19 (Jesus'
Nazareth manifesto, quoting Isaiah 61); Luke 19:10 ("the Son of Man came
to seek and to save the lost").

### Main Teaching
Luke teaches that Jesus came as Savior specifically for those excluded
by respectable society — the poor, women, tax collectors, and
Gentiles — and that his mission fulfills Israel's prophetic hope while
extending salvation universally.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Isaiah 61:1-2 (the passage Jesus reads and claims to fulfill in
Nazareth); Genesis 12:3 (the promise of blessing to all nations, which
Luke-Acts traces to fulfillment).

### Distinctive Features
Luke contains the most extensive infancy narrative of the four
Gospels, the largest amount of material unique to a single Gospel
(especially parables like the Good Samaritan and the Prodigal Son),
notable attention to women, the poor, and social outcasts, and forms
the first of a two-volume work continued in the book of Acts.

## JOHN

### Introduction and Outline
John, traditionally identified as the apostle "whom Jesus loved,"
explicitly states his purpose: that readers "may believe that Jesus is
the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in
his name" (20:31). Distinct in style and content from the other three
("Synoptic") Gospels, John is organized around seven selected
miraculous "signs" and extended theological discourse. Outline: prologue
(1:1-18), the Book of Signs (1:19-12), the Book of Glory / Upper Room
discourse and Passion (13-19), resurrection and epilogue (20-21).

### Structure and Major Divisions
John's Gospel divides naturally into the "Book of Signs" (public
ministry organized around seven miraculous signs, each paired with
theological discourse) and the "Book of Glory" (the private Upper Room
teaching to the disciples followed by the Passion and resurrection),
framed by a theologically dense prologue and an epilogue.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Prologue | 1:1-18 | The eternal Word made flesh |
| Book of Signs | 1:19-12 | Seven signs, public discourse, growing unbelief |
| Book of Glory | 13-17 | Upper Room discourse, Jesus' final teaching and prayer |
| Passion | 18-19 | Betrayal, trial, crucifixion |
| Resurrection and epilogue | 20-21 | Resurrection appearances, restoration of Peter |

### Summary of Content
John opens with a prologue identifying Jesus as the eternal Word who
was with God and was God, now become flesh. The public ministry section
is structured around seven selected miraculous signs (turning water to
wine, healing, feeding the five thousand, walking on water, and others),
each generating extended theological reflection and growing division
between belief and unbelief. The second half moves indoors to Jesus'
final, intimate teaching to his disciples before his arrest, followed
by the Passion narrative and resurrection appearances, with a final
epilogue restoring Peter after his denial.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: Jesus is the eternal, divine Son of God, and believing in him
brings eternal life. Purpose: explicitly evangelistic and
faith-confirming — to lead readers to believe (or continue believing)
that Jesus is the Christ. Application: readers are called to respond to
the evidence of the signs with genuine, ongoing belief, not mere
intellectual assent, and to receive the eternal life John says is
available now through that belief.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 1 (the prologue's high Christology); chapter 11 (the raising of
Lazarus); chapters 14-17 (the Upper Room discourse).

### Key Passages
John 1:1, 14 ("the Word was God... the Word became flesh"); John 3:16
("For God so loved the world..."); John 14:6 ("I am the way, the truth,
and the life"); John 20:30-31 (John's stated purpose for writing).

### Main Teaching
John teaches the full deity of Christ as the eternal Word incarnate,
and that eternal life is received not through ritual or ancestry but
through personal, continuing belief in Jesus as the Son of God.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Genesis 1:1 (the deliberate echo of "in the beginning" in John's
prologue); Exodus 3:14 (the divine name "I AM," which John's Gospel
repeatedly applies to Jesus' self-descriptions).

### Distinctive Features
John omits material central to the Synoptics (the Olympic parables,
the Sermon on the Mount, the transfiguration) while including
substantial material found nowhere else (the wedding at Cana, the
raising of Lazarus, the extended Upper Room discourse, the "I am"
statements), and is organized theologically around selected signs
rather than following a strict chronological or geographical outline.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

Presenting each Gospel's distinct portrait of Jesus (King in Matthew,
Servant in Mark, Savior of all people in Luke, divine Son in John)
equips missionaries to choose the Gospel emphasis that speaks most
directly into a given cultural context — for example, Luke's attention
to outsiders and the marginalized often resonates strongly in honor-shame
cultures, while John's direct declarations of Jesus' deity address
worldviews (Muslim, pluralist) that need Christ's full divinity stated
unambiguously.

## Discussion prompts for training

- Which Gospel's portrait of Jesus (King, Servant, Savior of the
  marginalized, or divine Son) would you lead with in your own
  ministry context, and why?
- How does Mark's emphasis on costly discipleship (8:34-38) speak
  differently to a persecuted church than to a comfortable one?
- Why might John's high, explicit Christology be especially important
  when engaging a worldview (such as Islam) that affirms Jesus as a
  prophet but denies his deity?
