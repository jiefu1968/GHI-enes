# New Testament Survey: The General Epistles

## HEBREWS

### Introduction and Outline
An anonymous but carefully argued sermon-letter demonstrating Christ's
superiority over every element of the old covenant system, written to
Jewish Christians tempted to abandon their faith and return to Judaism
under persecution pressure. Outline: Christ superior to angels and
Moses (1-4), Christ our superior high priest (5-10), living by faith
(11), practical exhortations to endurance (12-13).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The letter alternates between doctrinal exposition (demonstrating
Christ's supremacy) and urgent pastoral warning ("do not drift," "do not
harden your hearts," "do not fall away"), building toward the extended
call to persevering faith in chapter 11.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Christ superior to angels/Moses | 1-4 | Christ's supremacy, warning against unbelief |
| Christ our high priest | 5-10 | Melchizedek priesthood, the new covenant, one sacrifice |
| Faith and endurance | 11-13 | Heroes of faith, discipline, practical exhortation |

### Summary of Content
Hebrews opens by declaring the Son superior to the prophets, angels, and
even Moses, then presents Christ as the perfect high priest after the
order of Melchizedek, whose single sacrifice permanently accomplishes
what the repeated Levitical sacrifices could never achieve. This
argument for Christ's supremacy is punctuated by five urgent warning
passages cautioning readers against drifting from or abandoning their
confession. Chapter 11 catalogs Old Testament examples of persevering
faith, and the closing chapters urge endurance, mutual encouragement,
and holy living.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: Christ is superior to every element of the old covenant and
alone provides complete, final access to God. Purpose: to prevent
Jewish believers under pressure from abandoning Christ for a return to
the old covenant system. Application: readers are called to hold fast
their confession, draw near to God confidently through Christ's finished
work, and persevere in faith despite suffering.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 11 (the roll call of faith); chapter 9 (Christ's once-for-all
sacrifice).

### Key Passages
Hebrews 1:1-3 (the Son as the exact imprint of God's nature); Hebrews
4:14-16 (drawing near to the throne of grace); Hebrews 11:1 (the
definition of faith); Hebrews 12:1-2 ("run with endurance... looking to
Jesus").

### Main Teaching
Christ's priesthood and sacrifice are once-for-all and fully sufficient,
making any return to the old covenant system both unnecessary and a
tragic step backward from what believers already possess in him.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Genesis 14:18-20 and Psalm 110:4 (Melchizedek, the priest-king type
central to Hebrews' argument); Jeremiah 31:31-34 (the new covenant
promise Hebrews argues is fulfilled in Christ).

### Distinctive Features
Hebrews contains the New Testament's most developed argument from Old
Testament priesthood and sacrifice, the famous "roll call of faith"
(chapter 11), and five distinct, escalating warning passages unique in
their intensity among New Testament letters.

## JAMES

### Introduction and Outline
A intensely practical letter, likely one of the earliest New Testament
documents, written by James (the Lord's brother) to Jewish Christians
scattered among the nations, emphasizing that genuine faith inevitably
produces visible works. Outline: trials and temptation (1), faith and
works (2), the tongue and true wisdom (3), worldliness and
humility (4), patience and prayer (5).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The letter reads more like concentrated wisdom literature (echoing
Proverbs) than a systematic argument, moving rapidly between practical
topics unified by the theme of genuine, tested faith.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapter | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Trials | 1 | Testing produces endurance, hearing and doing the word |
| Faith and works | 2 | Impartiality, faith without works is dead |
| Speech and wisdom | 3 | Taming the tongue, wisdom from above |
| Worldliness | 4 | Friendship with the world, humility before God |
| Patience | 5 | Warning to the rich, patience, prayer for healing |

### Summary of Content
James instructs readers to consider trials as opportunities for
spiritual growth and warns against merely hearing God's word without
obeying it. He confronts favoritism toward the wealthy and insists that
genuine faith always produces visible works, using Abraham and Rahab as
examples. Warnings against an uncontrolled tongue and worldly ambition
lead into a call for humble submission to God, culminating in warnings
to oppressive rich landowners and a closing call to patient endurance
and effective prayer.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: genuine faith is demonstrated through practical obedience and
good works. Purpose: to correct a passive, merely intellectual
Christianity among scattered Jewish believers. Application: readers are
challenged to let their faith visibly shape their speech, treatment of
the poor, and daily conduct, not merely their professed beliefs.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 2 (faith and works); chapter 3 (the tongue and wisdom).

### Key Passages
James 1:22 ("be doers of the word, and not hearers only"); James 2:17
("faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead"); James 4:6
("God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble").

### Main Teaching
Authentic saving faith inevitably and necessarily produces observable
good works; a faith that produces no change in conduct is not genuine
faith at all.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Genesis 22 (Abraham's offering of Isaac, James's key example of
faith proven by works); Proverbs (the wisdom-literature style and
content James's letter closely echoes throughout).

### Distinctive Features
James is among the most practically direct New Testament books, closely
resembling Old Testament wisdom literature in style, and has
historically been read alongside Paul's letters on faith and works as
a complementary (not contradictory) emphasis on faith's necessary fruit.

## 1 PETER

### Introduction and Outline
Written by the apostle Peter to Christians scattered across Asia Minor
facing social hostility and persecution, encouraging them toward hope,
holy conduct, and submission modeled on Christ's own suffering. Outline:
the hope of salvation (1), holy living as God's people (2), suffering
for doing good (3-4), shepherding and standing firm (5).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The letter moves from the theological basis of Christian hope (living
hope through Christ's resurrection) to its practical outworking in
household, social, and church relationships, with suffering as a
recurring, unifying concern throughout.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Living hope | 1 | New birth, holiness, the enduring word |
| God's people | 2 | Living stones, submission to authority |
| Suffering well | 3-4 | Suffering for righteousness, following Christ's example |
| Final exhortation | 5 | Shepherding elders, humility, standing firm |

### Summary of Content
Peter grounds believers' identity in their new birth and living hope
through Christ's resurrection, calling them to holiness befitting their
status as God's chosen people and "living stones" being built into a
spiritual house. Much of the letter addresses how to endure unjust
suffering — submitting rightly within households and society, following
Christ's own pattern of suffering unjustly and entrusting himself to
God — and closes with instruction to elders on humble, willing
shepherding and a final call to firm faith amid the devil's
opposition.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: hope in Christ sustains holy living and patient endurance
through unjust suffering. Purpose: to encourage persecuted believers
scattered across the empire and instruct them in godly conduct amid
hostility. Application: readers learn to respond to unjust suffering
by following Christ's example rather than retaliation, finding their
true identity and hope secure regardless of present hardship.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 2 (the living stones and God's chosen people); chapter 3
(suffering for righteousness, following Christ's pattern).

### Key Passages
1 Peter 1:3 ("he has caused us to be born again to a living hope");
1 Peter 2:9 ("you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood"); 1 Peter
2:21-23 (Christ's example of suffering unjustly without retaliation);
1 Peter 5:6-7 (humbling oneself and casting anxiety on God).

### Main Teaching
Christian identity and hope, secured by Christ's resurrection, enable
believers to endure unjust suffering with patient, Christlike conduct
rather than retaliation or despair.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Isaiah 53 (the Suffering Servant, directly quoted regarding Christ's
example in chapter 2); Exodus 19:5-6 (the "royal priesthood" language
Peter applies to the church).

### Distinctive Features
1 Peter contains the New Testament's most sustained direct teaching on
suffering well as a Christian, and directly applies Isaiah 53's
Suffering Servant language to Christ as a model for believers' own
unjust suffering.

## 2 PETER

### Introduction and Outline
Peter's final letter, written shortly before his death, warning against
false teachers who denied Christ's return and lived licentiously,
while affirming the reliability of apostolic testimony and prophetic
Scripture. Outline: growing in Christian character (1), warning against
false teachers (2), the certainty of Christ's return (3).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The letter moves from a positive call to grow in godly character,
grounded in reliable apostolic eyewitness and prophetic Scripture, into
an extended warning against specific false teachers, closing with
assurance regarding the certainty (though seemingly delayed) of Christ's
return.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapter | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Growing in godliness | 1 | Christian virtues, reliability of Scripture |
| False teachers | 2 | Warning against greedy, licentious false teachers |
| Christ's return | 3 | The Day of the Lord, patient expectation |

### Summary of Content
Peter urges believers to supplement their faith with a chain of
Christian virtues, grounding confidence not in "cleverly devised myths"
but in eyewitness testimony (the Transfiguration) and the certainty of
prophetic Scripture. Chapter 2 harshly warns against false teachers
motivated by greed and immorality, comparing their coming judgment to
that of fallen angels and the flood generation. The closing chapter
addresses scoffers who doubted Christ's promised return, explaining that
God's apparent delay reflects patience, not failure, and that the Day
of the Lord will come suddenly.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: reliable apostolic and prophetic testimony grounds both godly
growth and confident hope in Christ's certain return. Purpose: to warn
against specific false teachers denying Christ's return and modeling
immorality. Application: readers are called to pursue active Christian
growth, discern and reject false teaching, and hold confidently to
Christ's promised return despite its apparent delay.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 1 (Christian growth and Scripture's reliability); chapter 3
(the Day of the Lord).

### Key Passages
2 Peter 1:20-21 (prophecy's origin in the Holy Spirit, not human will);
2 Peter 3:9 (the Lord's patience, not slowness, regarding his promise);
2 Peter 3:10 (the Day of the Lord will come like a thief).

### Main Teaching
Believers can trust the reliability of apostolic eyewitness testimony
and prophetic Scripture, growing in godly character while confidently
awaiting Christ's certain, if seemingly delayed, return.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Genesis 6-9 (the flood judgment, cited as a precedent for coming
judgment); Genesis 19 (Sodom and Gomorrah, similarly cited).

### Distinctive Features
2 Peter contains striking parallels with the book of Jude (particularly
chapter 2), one of the New Testament's clearest statements on the divine
origin of prophetic Scripture, and Peter's own anticipation of his
approaching death (1:14).

## 1 JOHN

### Introduction and Outline
Written by the apostle John to combat an early docetic-leaning false
teaching that denied Christ's full humanity and to reassure genuine
believers of their salvation, this letter repeatedly tests true faith
by three marks: doctrinal truth, righteous living, and love for fellow
believers. Outline: fellowship with God and walking in light (1-2),
children of God versus children of the devil (3), love and truth (4),
confidence and assurance (5).

### Structure and Major Divisions
Rather than a strict linear argument, the letter cycles repeatedly
through its three tests of genuine faith (right belief about Christ,
righteous conduct, and love for other believers), returning to each
theme multiple times with increasing depth.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Fellowship and light | 1-2 | Confession of sin, walking in the light, antichrists |
| Children of God | 3 | Righteousness and love as marks of true birth from God |
| Love and truth | 4 | God is love, testing the spirits |
| Assurance | 5 | Faith that overcomes the world, confidence in prayer |

### Summary of Content
John insists that genuine fellowship with God requires walking in the
light and honest confession of sin, while warning against those
denying Christ came in the flesh. True children of God are marked by
practiced righteousness and genuine love for other believers, in
contrast to those who claim spiritual knowledge while showing neither.
The letter's central theological claim, "God is love," grounds its
ethical demand that believers love one another as evidence of God's
presence in them, and closes with assurance that those who believe in
the Son of God may know with confidence that they have eternal life.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: genuine faith is demonstrated by right belief about Christ,
righteous living, and love for fellow believers. Purpose: to refute
false teaching denying Christ's full humanity and to give genuine
believers confident assurance of salvation. Application: readers can
test the genuineness of their own and others' faith by these three
marks, and may hold confident assurance of eternal life through belief
in the Son.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 4 (God is love, testing the spirits); chapter 5 (assurance of
eternal life).

### Key Passages
1 John 1:9 (confession and forgiveness); 1 John 4:8 ("God is love");
1 John 5:13 ("that you may know that you have eternal life").

### Main Teaching
Assurance of genuine salvation is confirmed by right belief about
Christ's incarnation, a life of practiced righteousness, and love for
fellow believers — not by isolated claims of spiritual experience alone.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Genesis 4 (Cain as a negative example of failed love toward a brother,
referenced in chapter 3).

### Distinctive Features
1 John contains the New Testament's most concentrated statement that
"God is love" and offers some of Scripture's clearest tests for
believers to examine the genuineness of their own faith and assurance
of salvation.

## 2 JOHN

### Introduction and Outline
A brief personal letter from "the elder" (traditionally John) to "the
elect lady" (likely a local church), warning against welcoming
traveling false teachers who denied Christ's incarnation. Outline:
walking in truth and love (1-6), warning against deceivers (7-11),
closing (12-13).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The short letter moves from an opening call to mutual love grounded in
truth to a specific, practical warning about hospitality toward false
teachers.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Verses | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Truth and love | 1-6 | Walking according to God's commandments |
| Warning | 7-11 | Refusing hospitality to deceivers |
| Closing | 12-13 | Personal greeting |

### Summary of Content
John commends the recipients for walking in truth and urges continued
mutual love, defined as obedience to God's commandments. He then warns
specifically against extending hospitality or welcome to traveling
teachers who deny that Jesus Christ came in the flesh, since doing so
would make the host complicit in spreading that false teaching.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: love for fellow believers must be paired with discernment
regarding doctrinal truth. Purpose: to warn a local congregation against
supporting itinerant false teachers. Application: readers learn that
genuine Christian hospitality has doctrinal limits, and that supporting
false teaching (even unintentionally, through hospitality) carries real
responsibility.

### Key Chapters
The entire brief letter functions as a single unit, centered on verses
7-11.

### Key Passages
2 John 6 ("this is love, that we walk according to his commandments");
2 John 10-11 (the warning against welcoming false teachers).

### Main Teaching
Christian love and hospitality must be exercised with doctrinal
discernment, since supporting false teachers, even through simple
hospitality, makes one complicit in their error.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Deuteronomy 13 (warning against false prophets, an Old Testament
precedent for guarding the community against deceptive teaching).

### Distinctive Features
2 John is one of the shortest books in the New Testament and offers a
rare, explicit New Testament instruction to withhold hospitality from
specific false teachers.

## 3 JOHN

### Introduction and Outline
A brief personal letter from "the elder" to Gaius, commending his
hospitality to traveling missionaries and contrasting him with
Diotrephes, a domineering local leader who refused to welcome them.
Outline: commendation of Gaius (1-8), condemnation of Diotrephes
(9-11), commendation of Demetrius and closing (12-14).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The letter's structure contrasts two models of leadership and
hospitality — Gaius's generous welcome of traveling ministers versus
Diotrephes's self-serving refusal — with a brief closing commendation
of a third figure, Demetrius.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Verses | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Gaius commended | 1-8 | Faithful hospitality to traveling workers |
| Diotrephes condemned | 9-11 | Refusal of hospitality, love of preeminence |
| Closing | 12-14 | Commendation of Demetrius |

### Summary of Content
John praises Gaius for his faithful support of traveling gospel
workers, noting this hospitality is itself a form of partnership in
their ministry. In sharp contrast, Diotrephes is condemned for loving
"to put himself first," refusing to acknowledge John's authority,
maliciously slandering him, and even expelling from the church those
who welcomed the traveling missionaries. The letter closes with brief
commendation of Demetrius and a personal note anticipating a visit.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: genuine hospitality supports gospel ministry, while self-serving
leadership opposes it. Purpose: to commend faithful support of itinerant
workers and address a specific leadership abuse. Application: readers
are encouraged to support gospel workers generously and warned against
Diotrephes-like leadership that prioritizes personal power over the
church's good.

### Key Chapters
The entire brief letter functions as a single unit, centered on the
Gaius/Diotrephes contrast in verses 5-11.

### Key Passages
3 John 8 ("we ought to support people like these, that we may be
fellow workers for the truth"); 3 John 9-10 (Diotrephes's abuse of
leadership).

### Main Teaching
Faithful hospitality toward gospel workers is genuine partnership in
ministry, while self-serving, power-seeking leadership actively harms
the church and opposes legitimate apostolic authority.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Numbers 16 (Korah's self-serving rebellion against established
authority, a conceptual parallel to Diotrephes).

### Distinctive Features
3 John is the New Testament's shortest book and its only letter
addressed to a named private individual concerning a specific internal
church leadership conflict rather than doctrine.

## JUDE

### Introduction and Outline
A short, urgent letter from Jude (likely the Lord's brother) written to
warn against false teachers who had infiltrated the church, twisting
grace into license for immorality. Outline: the occasion for writing
and the false teachers described (1-16), the call to persevere and
contend for the faith (17-25).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The letter's warning section draws heavily on a rapid sequence of Old
Testament and extrabiblical examples of judgment on rebellion and
immorality, before pivoting to direct pastoral instruction for
believers on how to respond.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Verses | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| The false teachers | 1-16 | Historical examples of judgment, description of the intruders |
| The response | 17-25 | Remembering apostolic warning, building up the faith, doxology |

### Summary of Content
Jude explains he had intended to write about shared salvation but felt
compelled instead to urge readers to "contend for the faith" against
false teachers who had secretly infiltrated the church, perverting
grace into a license for sensuality and denying Christ. He illustrates
coming judgment with a rapid series of examples — the wilderness
generation, fallen angels, Sodom and Gomorrah, Cain, Balaam, and
Korah — before instructing believers to remember apostolic warnings,
build themselves up in the faith, and show mercy to the wavering while
guarding against contamination. The letter closes with one of
Scripture's best-known doxologies.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: believers must actively contend for authentic faith against
those who distort grace into license for sin. Purpose: to warn a
church that false teachers had already infiltrated and to urge
vigorous defense of sound doctrine and conduct. Application: readers
are called to actively guard and defend authentic Christian faith
rather than passively assuming error will not reach them.

### Key Chapters
The entire short letter functions as a single unit, especially verses
3-4 and the closing doxology (24-25).

### Key Passages
Jude 3 ("contend for the faith that was once for all delivered to the
saints"); Jude 24-25 (the closing doxology, "to him who is able to keep
you from stumbling").

### Main Teaching
Believers must actively and vigorously defend authentic Christian
faith and conduct against false teachers who twist grace into an excuse
for immorality.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Numbers 16 (Korah's rebellion); Genesis 19 (Sodom and Gomorrah); Genesis
4 (Cain) — all cited directly as examples of judgment on rebellion.

### Distinctive Features
Jude closely parallels 2 Peter 2 in content and structure, references
non-canonical Jewish literature (such as material associated with
1 Enoch) more directly than any other New Testament book, and contains
one of Scripture's most quoted closing doxologies.

# New Testament Survey: Revelation

### Introduction and Outline
Written by the apostle John while exiled on Patmos, Revelation unveils
the risen, glorified Christ's ultimate victory over evil and his final
establishment of a new heaven and earth, addressed to seven churches
facing persecution and compromise. Outline: the vision of Christ and
messages to seven churches (1-3), the throne room and the scroll (4-5),
the seal, trumpet, and bowl judgments (6-16), the fall of Babylon and
Christ's return (17-19), the millennium, final judgment, and new
creation (20-22).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The book is often read according to John's own stated structure: "the
things you have seen" (the vision of Christ, ch. 1), "the things that
are" (messages to the seven churches, ch. 2-3), and "the things that are
to take place after this" (the remaining prophetic vision, ch. 4-22),
with judgment sequences (seals, trumpets, bowls) intensifying in a
pattern of escalating severity.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Vision of Christ | 1 | John's commission, the glorified Christ |
| Seven churches | 2-3 | Specific messages of commendation and correction |
| Throne room | 4-5 | Heavenly worship, the Lamb and the scroll |
| Judgments | 6-16 | Seal, trumpet, and bowl judgments |
| Babylon's fall / Christ's return | 17-19 | Judgment of the world system, the Second Coming |
| Consummation | 20-22 | Millennium, final judgment, new heaven and earth |

### Summary of Content
John receives a vision of the glorified Christ walking among seven
lampstands, followed by specific messages to seven churches, each
commended or corrected for its particular spiritual condition. A vision
of heaven's throne room introduces the Lamb who alone is worthy to open
a sealed scroll, initiating cycles of seal, trumpet, and bowl judgments
that escalate in severity against a rebellious world. Chapters 17-19
depict the fall of "Babylon" (the world's corrupt political-religious
system) and Christ's triumphant return as conquering King. The closing
chapters describe a period of Christ's reign, the final judgment of the
dead, and the descent of the new Jerusalem in a fully renewed creation
where God dwells directly with his people.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: Christ ultimately and decisively triumphs over evil, vindicating
his suffering church and establishing eternal new creation. Purpose: to
encourage persecuted and compromised churches with the certainty of
Christ's final victory and to call for faithful endurance and purity.
Application: readers facing suffering, compromise, or discouragement
are called to persevere faithfully, trusting the certainty of Christ's
ultimate victory and the promised renewal of all things.

### Key Chapters
Chapters 2-3 (messages to the seven churches); chapter 5 (the worthy
Lamb); chapters 21-22 (the new heaven and new earth).

### Key Passages
Revelation 1:8 ("I am the Alpha and the Omega"); Revelation 5:9-10 (the
song to the worthy Lamb); Revelation 21:3-4 (God dwelling with his
people, wiping away every tear); Revelation 22:20 ("Surely I am coming
soon. Amen. Come, Lord Jesus!").

### Main Teaching
Despite present suffering, compromise, and apparent evil triumph,
Christ's victory is certain and his people will be vindicated in a
fully renewed creation where evil, suffering, and death are finally and
permanently ended.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Daniel 7 (the beast imagery and heavenly throne scene Revelation
develops); Ezekiel 40-48 (the new temple/city vision paralleling
Revelation's new Jerusalem); Isaiah 65:17 (the promise of new heavens
and a new earth).

### Distinctive Features
Revelation is the New Testament's only fully apocalyptic book, draws
more heavily on Old Testament imagery (especially Daniel and Ezekiel)
than any other New Testament writing without directly quoting it, and
has generated more varied interpretive approaches (preterist, historicist,
futurist, idealist) than perhaps any other biblical book.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

Revelation's messages to the seven churches (2-3) offer a directly
transferable diagnostic tool for evaluating the spiritual health of
any local congregation across cultures — commendation and correction
patterns (lost love, tolerance of false teaching, lukewarmness,
compromise) recur in church-planting contexts everywhere. Hebrews'
argument for Christ's sufficiency over every religious system offers a
direct apologetic model for engaging Buddhist, Muslim, or folk-religious
contexts where additional mediators, rituals, or teachers are commonly
assumed necessary alongside (or instead of) Christ.

## Discussion prompts for training

- Which of the seven churches' specific commendations or corrections
  (Revelation 2-3) most closely mirrors a church-planting situation you
  have observed or served in?
- How would Hebrews' argument for Christ's absolute sufficiency as
  priest and sacrifice apply directly to a context where intermediary
  spiritual figures (saints, ancestors, other mediators) are commonly
  assumed necessary?
- James insists genuine faith produces visible works; how would you
  present this alongside Paul's teaching on grace (Romans, Galatians)
  without implying contradiction, especially to a new believer confused
  by the apparent tension?
