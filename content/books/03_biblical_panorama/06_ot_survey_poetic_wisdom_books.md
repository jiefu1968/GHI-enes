# Old Testament Survey: The Poetic and Wisdom Books

## JOB

### Introduction and Outline
Job wrestles with the problem of innocent suffering through the story
of a righteous man who loses everything and debates the cause of his
suffering with three (later four) friends before God himself finally
speaks. Outline: prologue (1-2), dialogues with Job's friends (3-31),
Elihu's speeches (32-37), God's speeches and Job's response (38-42).

### Structure and Major Divisions
Framed by a prose prologue and epilogue, the book's long poetic middle
section cycles through three rounds of dialogue between Job and his
friends, followed by young Elihu's speeches, before God's own answer
from the whirlwind resolves (without fully explaining) Job's questions.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Prologue | 1-2 | Job's prosperity, the heavenly wager, his losses |
| Dialogues | 3-31 | Three rounds of debate with Eliphaz, Bildad, Zophar |
| Elihu | 32-37 | A younger man's speeches on God's justice |
| God's answer | 38-42 | God speaks from the whirlwind; Job's restoration |

### Summary of Content
Job, a righteous and prosperous man, loses his children, wealth, and
health in rapid succession, prompting three friends to argue that his
suffering must indicate hidden sin, a position Job consistently
rejects while still struggling to understand God's purposes. Young
Elihu offers a further, somewhat different perspective emphasizing
God's use of suffering to instruct. God finally answers not by
explaining the reason for Job's suffering but by overwhelming him with
questions about creation's vastness and mystery, leading Job to humble
submission rather than resolved explanation; the book closes with Job's
restored fortunes.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: God's wisdom and sovereignty are trustworthy even when
suffering's specific purpose remains hidden from human understanding.
Purpose: to correct simplistic retribution theology (suffering always
equals personal sin) and model faithful trust amid inexplicable
suffering. Application: readers learn not to assume suffering always
indicates personal sin, and to trust God's wisdom even without full
explanation for their pain.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 1-2 (the prologue and Job's initial response); chapters 38-41
(God's speeches from the whirlwind).

### Key Passages
Job 1:21 ("the Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away; blessed be the
name of the Lord"); Job 13:15 ("though he slay me, I will hope in
him"); Job 42:5-6 (Job's humbled response to God).

### Main Teaching
God's wisdom and purposes remain trustworthy even when suffering has no
apparent explanation, and simplistic assumptions that all suffering
results from personal sin are mistaken.

### Selected Related Scriptures
James 5:11 (citing Job's endurance and the Lord's compassionate
outcome); Ezekiel 14:14 (Job cited alongside Noah and Daniel as a
model of righteousness).

### Distinctive Features
Job is among the oldest and most sustained poetic treatments of
suffering in world literature, uniquely opens with a scene in the
heavenly court explaining a mystery hidden from its human characters
throughout the entire narrative, and resolves through divine
confrontation rather than direct explanation.

## PSALMS

### Introduction and Outline
Psalms is Israel's inspired collection of prayers and songs, spanning
lament, praise, thanksgiving, wisdom, and royal/messianic themes, likely
compiled over centuries and organized into five "books" echoing the
Pentateuch's structure. Outline: Book I (1-41), Book II (42-72), Book
III (73-89), Book IV (90-106), Book V (107-150).

### Structure and Major Divisions
Beyond its five-book division, Psalms is often analyzed by genre:
laments (individual and communal), hymns of praise, thanksgiving
psalms, wisdom psalms, royal/messianic psalms, and imprecatory psalms,
each following recognizable poetic patterns.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Psalms | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Book I | 1-41 | Largely Davidic, personal lament and trust |
| Book II | 42-72 | Davidic and Korahite psalms, kingship themes |
| Book III | 73-89 | Asaph and Korahite psalms, national crisis |
| Book IV | 90-106 | God's eternal kingship, historical reflection |
| Book V | 107-150 | Praise, ascent psalms, concluding hallelujah psalms |

### Summary of Content
The Psalter gives voice to the full range of human experience before
God — anguished lament over enemies, sin, and suffering; exuberant
praise for creation and redemption; quiet trust amid danger; wisdom
reflection on the righteous and wicked; and royal psalms anticipating
an ideal Davidic king, several of which the New Testament applies
directly to Jesus as Messiah. The collection moves overall from
frequent lament in its earlier books toward increasing praise, closing
with an extended crescendo of hallelujah psalms.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: God is worthy of praise and trustworthy across every human
circumstance, from deepest lament to highest joy. Purpose: to provide
Israel's (and the church's) inspired vocabulary for approaching God in
prayer and worship across all of life's circumstances. Application:
readers are given permission and language for honest lament as well as
exuberant praise, learning that both belong legitimately in
relationship with God.

### Key Chapters
Psalm 1 (the two ways); Psalm 22 (messianic suffering); Psalm 23 (the
shepherd psalm); Psalm 51 (repentance); Psalm 150 (closing praise).

### Key Passages
Psalm 23:1 ("the Lord is my shepherd"); Psalm 51:10 ("create in me a
clean heart, O God"); Psalm 110:1 (quoted more than any other Old
Testament verse in the New Testament, applied to Christ's exaltation).

### Main Teaching
God is worthy of trust and praise across the full range of human
experience, and honest prayer — including lament, confession, and
praise — is the appropriate response to every circumstance of life.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Hebrews 1 (multiple psalms quoted as testimony to Christ's deity and
kingship); Acts 2:25-36 (Peter's use of Psalm 16 and 110 regarding
Christ's resurrection and exaltation).

### Distinctive Features
Psalms is the Bible's longest book and its primary hymnbook and prayer
book, contains more messianic prophecy applied directly to Christ in
the New Testament than perhaps any other Old Testament book, and
uniquely models raw, honest lament alongside exuberant praise.

## PROVERBS

### Introduction and Outline
Proverbs collects short, memorable sayings offering practical wisdom
for godly living, largely attributed to Solomon, framed by an extended
call to embrace wisdom over folly. Outline: wisdom's call (1-9),
Solomon's proverbs (10-24), Hezekiah's collection of Solomon's proverbs
(25-29), the words of Agur and King Lemuel (30-31).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The book moves from extended poetic discourses personifying wisdom and
folly as two contrasting figures (1-9) to dense collections of
short, standalone proverbial sayings (10-29), closing with distinct
shorter collections and the famous acrostic poem praising the excellent
wife (31).

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Wisdom's call | 1-9 | Extended appeals to choose wisdom over folly |
| Solomon's proverbs | 10-24 | Short, topical proverbial sayings |
| Hezekiah's collection | 25-29 | Further Solomonic proverbs, royally compiled |
| Closing collections | 30-31 | Agur's words, King Lemuel, the excellent wife |

### Summary of Content
The opening chapters personify wisdom as calling out publicly to the
simple, contrasting her with the seductive, destructive figure of
folly, and repeatedly ground true wisdom in "the fear of the Lord."
The bulk of the book collects short, standalone proverbs addressing
practical daily topics — speech, work, wealth, relationships,
integrity, discipline — arranged loosely by theme. The closing chapters
add further wisdom sayings and conclude with an acrostic poem praising
a wife of noble character.

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: the fear of the Lord is the foundation of true wisdom for
practical daily living. Purpose: to instruct in practical, godly
wisdom for navigating ordinary life skillfully and righteously.
Application: readers gain concrete, memorable guidance for speech,
work, relationships, and character, grounded in reverence for God
rather than mere pragmatic cleverness.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 1 (wisdom's call, the fear of the Lord); chapter 31 (the
excellent wife).

### Key Passages
Proverbs 1:7 ("the fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge");
Proverbs 3:5-6 ("trust in the Lord with all your heart... he will make
straight your paths"); Proverbs 22:6 ("train up a child in the way he
should go").

### Main Teaching
True wisdom for daily living begins with reverent fear of the Lord and
expresses itself in practical, disciplined, righteous conduct across
every ordinary area of life.

### Selected Related Scriptures
1 Kings 4:29-34 (Solomon's God-given wisdom, the traditional background
for his authorship); James 1:5 (asking God for wisdom, echoing
Proverbs' themes).

### Distinctive Features
Proverbs is the Bible's most concentrated collection of practical
wisdom sayings, distinctively personifies wisdom and folly as
contrasting female figures, and closes with a celebrated acrostic
poem describing an ideal wife of noble character.

## ECCLESIASTES

### Introduction and Outline
Ecclesiastes records "the Preacher's" honest, searching investigation
into life's meaning "under the sun," testing pleasure, wisdom, work,
and wealth before concluding that fearing God and keeping his
commandments is the whole duty of humanity. Outline: the search for
meaning (1-6), wisdom for living meaningfully (7-12).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The book's argument circles repeatedly through the refrain "vanity of
vanities" as the Preacher tests various pursuits (pleasure, wisdom,
labor, wealth) and finds each ultimately unsatisfying "under the sun"
(apart from God), before arriving at practical wisdom and a concluding
call to fear God.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| The search | 1-6 | Testing pleasure, wisdom, labor, wealth as ultimate meaning |
| Wisdom for living | 7-12 | Practical wisdom, aging, closing conclusion |

### Summary of Content
The Preacher surveys his exhaustive pursuit of pleasure, wisdom,
achievement, and wealth, repeatedly concluding that each is "vanity" —
fleeting and ultimately unable to provide lasting meaning when
considered strictly "under the sun," apart from reference to God and
eternity. Amid this searching honesty, practical wisdom for daily
living is still commended, along with enjoyment of ordinary life as a
gift from God. The book closes by urging readers, especially the young,
to remember their Creator and concludes that fearing God and keeping
his commandments is "the whole duty of man."

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: life's pursuits are ultimately meaningless "under the sun"
without reference to God; true meaning is found in fearing him.
Purpose: to expose the emptiness of purely secular pursuit of meaning
and redirect readers toward God-centered living. Application: readers
are freed from misplaced ultimate expectations of pleasure, achievement,
or wealth, and directed toward enjoying ordinary life as God's gift
while fearing him as life's true anchor.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 1 (the Preacher's opening investigation); chapter 12 (the
concluding call to remember the Creator).

### Key Passages
Ecclesiastes 1:2 ("vanity of vanities... all is vanity"); Ecclesiastes
3:1 ("for everything there is a season"); Ecclesiastes 12:13 ("fear God
and keep his commandments, for this is the whole duty of man").

### Main Teaching
Apart from God, every human pursuit of ultimate meaning proves empty;
true wisdom fears God and receives ordinary life gratefully as his
gift, rather than demanding it provide ultimate meaning on its own.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Genesis 3:17-19 (the futility/toil introduced at the Fall, echoed
throughout Ecclesiastes); 1 Corinthians 15:58 (Paul's contrasting
assurance that labor "in the Lord" is not in vain).

### Distinctive Features
Ecclesiastes is unusually philosophical and searching in tone for
biblical wisdom literature, repeats its "vanity" refrain more than
thirty times, and has often been used apologetically to demonstrate the
emptiness of a purely secular worldview from the inside.

## SONG OF SOLOMON

### Introduction and Outline
An extended love poem celebrating romantic and sexual love between a
bridegroom and his bride within marriage, traditionally attributed to
Solomon, read both for its plain celebration of marital love and,
historically, as an allegory of divine love. Outline: courtship and
anticipation (1-3), the wedding and consummation (4-5), maturing love
(6-8).

### Structure and Major Divisions
The poem unfolds through alternating voices (the bride, the
bridegroom, and a chorus of companions) tracing the couple's
relationship from courtship through wedding to a maturing, secure love
tested and strengthened over time.

### Book Chart Summary
| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Courtship | 1-3 | Mutual longing, anticipation of marriage |
| Wedding | 4-5 | Wedding night, praise of the bride and groom |
| Maturing love | 6-8 | Continued devotion, love's enduring strength |

### Summary of Content
The Song traces a romantic relationship through extended, richly
poetic mutual praise between bride and groom, celebrating physical
beauty and desire without embarrassment, moving through anticipation,
wedding-night intimacy, temporary separation and longing, and a mature,
settled love that the poem itself describes as "strong as death."

### Theme, Purpose, and Application Message
Theme: romantic and sexual love within marriage is good, beautiful, and
worth celebrating as God's gift. Purpose: to affirm the goodness of
marital love and intimacy within Scripture's larger witness.
Application: readers gain a positive, unembarrassed biblical vision of
marital intimacy, and many readers throughout history have also found
in its imagery a picture of God's passionate love for his people.

### Key Chapters
Chapter 4 (the groom's praise of the bride); chapter 8 (love's enduring
strength).

### Key Passages
Song of Solomon 2:16 ("my beloved is mine, and I am his"); Song of
Solomon 8:6-7 ("love is strong as death... many waters cannot quench
love").

### Main Teaching
Marital love, including its physical and romantic dimensions, is a
good and beautiful gift from God worth celebrating openly, not a
source of embarrassment or mere biological necessity.

### Selected Related Scriptures
Ephesians 5:31-32 (Paul's application of marital union typologically to
Christ and the church); Genesis 2:24 (the "one flesh" union the Song
celebrates poetically).

### Distinctive Features
Song of Solomon is the Bible's only book devoted entirely to romantic
love poetry, never explicitly mentions God by name, and has generated
a long history of both literal (celebrating marital love) and allegorical
(picturing divine love) interpretive traditions.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

Job's model of faithful lament without full explanation is especially
valuable for missionaries ministering amid suffering, disaster, or
persecution where simplistic "suffering equals sin" theology (common in
many folk-religious and karma-influenced worldviews) needs direct,
pastoral correction. The Psalms' full range of lament and praise
provides missionaries with a transferable devotional and liturgical
resource especially suited to oral cultures, since many psalms were
composed for corporate singing and recitation.

## Discussion prompts for training

- How would you use Job's example to gently correct a karma-influenced
  assumption (common in Hindu or Buddhist-influenced contexts) that
  suffering always indicates personal wrongdoing?
- Which genre of Psalm (lament, praise, wisdom, royal) is most absent
  from your own church's worship life, and what would recovering it add?
- How does Ecclesiastes' honest exposure of meaninglessness "under the
  sun" provide a bridge for engaging secular or nihilistic
  worldviews before presenting the gospel's answer?
