# Christian Witness to Hindus: A Lausanne Movement Perspective

## Overview

As part of the broader Lausanne Movement's ongoing effort to address
Christian witness to specific major world religions with theological
seriousness and missiological care, this framework addresses witness
to Hindus specifically, treating the encounter between Christian faith
and Hindu tradition as requiring sustained, informed engagement rather
than either superficial dismissal or uncritical accommodation.

## The Scale and Significance of the Task

Hinduism represents one of the world's largest religious traditions,
concentrated especially in India but with substantial and growing
global diaspora communities, making thoughtful, sustained Christian
witness to Hindus a matter of major strategic significance for global
mission rather than a peripheral concern. The historical relationship
between Christianity and Hinduism in India carries complicated
colonial-era baggage that continues to shape how the gospel is
received and perceived, requiring contemporary witness to reckon
honestly with this history rather than proceeding as though the
historical context were neutral.

## Theological Engagement with Hindu Thought

Serious Christian witness to Hindus requires theological engagement
with core Hindu concepts on their own terms — karma, reincarnation,
the concept of Brahman and the ultimate unity of reality, and the
range of paths (bhakti devotion, jnana knowledge, karma yoga action)
Hindu tradition offers toward liberation — identifying genuine points
of contact as well as places where biblical revelation offers a
substantively different, and better, answer to the same underlying
human questions and longings these Hindu concepts address.

## Contextualization and Its Limits

Following broader missiological consensus on contextualization, this
framework affirms that the gospel must be presented in genuinely
Indian and Hindu cultural forms rather than requiring wholesale
adoption of Western cultural Christianity, while maintaining that
certain core theological convictions (Christ's unique deity and
atoning work, the nature of salvation as grace received by faith) are
not negotiable cultural variables but essential, non-negotiable
gospel content. This requires careful, ongoing discernment about which
specific elements of Hindu cultural and religious practice can be
redeemed and repurposed for Christian worship and identity, and which
must be set aside as incompatible with the gospel's essential content.

## The Believing Community and Social Cost

Conversion from Hinduism to Christian faith frequently carries
significant social cost — family rejection, community ostracism, and
in some contexts legal or social hostility — meaning effective witness
must anticipate and prepare new believers for these real costs rather
than presenting conversion as a simple, low-cost individual decision.
Building genuine, supportive Christian community becomes especially
important for converts who may face significant loss of their prior
social network as a direct consequence of their new faith.

## A Call to Prayer and Sustained Engagement

Consistent with the broader Lausanne Movement's emphasis on
prayerful, strategic global mission engagement, this framework calls
the global church to sustained prayer specifically for Hindu peoples
and communities, alongside serious, informed strategic engagement
rather than sporadic or poorly prepared evangelistic effort.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

This framework's insistence on holding contextualization and
theological non-negotiables together — genuinely Indian cultural
expression of an unchanging gospel core — offers missionaries a
balanced model resisting both the historic failure of imposing
Western cultural Christianity and the opposite failure of diluting
essential gospel content for the sake of cultural accommodation.
Preparing new believers realistically for the social cost of
conversion, and building supportive community around them, is treated
as essential rather than optional pastoral care.

## Discussion prompts for training

- How would you distinguish specific Hindu cultural or religious forms
  that could be redeemed for Christian use from those that must be set
  aside as incompatible with the gospel?
- How would you help a Hindu inquirer realistically anticipate and
  prepare for the social cost of conversion, without either
  minimizing it or discouraging them unnecessarily?
- Where do you see colonial-era history still shaping how the gospel
  is perceived in Hindu communities you serve or know, and how would
  you address that history honestly?
