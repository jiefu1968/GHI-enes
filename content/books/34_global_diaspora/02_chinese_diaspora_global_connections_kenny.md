# The Chinese Diaspora: Homeland Connection and the Global Chinatown

## Overview

This file continues the synthesis of Kevin Kenny's *Diaspora: A Very
Short Introduction*, focusing specifically on his treatment of the
Chinese diaspora's distinctive relationship to its homeland — a case
study with direct relevance to this curriculum's own Chinese
missionary audience — and comparing it briefly with the Indian
diaspora's parallel but distinct pattern of homeland engagement.

## A Global Network Built on Recognition, Not Prior Contact

Kenny observes a striking feature of the contemporary Chinese
diaspora: in major cities around the world, Chinese people who have
had no prior interaction with one another and come from entirely
different national contexts nonetheless find themselves in an
immediately recognizable cultural setting — what Kenny calls "the new
global Chinatown." This recognizability itself is a marker of
successful diasporic connectivity in Kenny's framework: shared
cultural markers, language, and social practice allow strangers from
across a genuinely global network to recognize each other as
belonging to a common diaspora, independent of direct prior contact
or even shared national origin within China's own regional diversity.

## The State's Active Cultivation of Diaspora Ties

A distinctive feature of the Chinese case, compared to many other
diasporas, is how actively the homeland state itself works to
cultivate and mobilize diaspora connection rather than simply allowing
it to develop informally. The Overseas Chinese Affairs Office (OCAO),
an administrative branch of China's State Council with a branch in
every Chinese province and municipality except Tibet, exists
specifically to protect the interests of overseas Chinese, support
Chinese-language schools and media abroad, and actively promote
economic, scientific, and cultural exchange between China and its
diaspora. The scale of the resulting economic connectivity is
substantial: by some estimates, nearly three-quarters of direct
foreign investment in China now originates from overseas Chinese
sources — a concrete illustration of Kenny's "connectivity" dimension
operating at a genuinely macroeconomic scale.

This state engagement operates within real legal constraints: the
People's Republic of China does not recognize dual nationality, so
individuals of Chinese descent living abroad may pursue Chinese
citizenship only if they hold no citizenship in their host country.
Despite this formal boundary, Kenny notes that the ever-expanding
practical links between China and its overseas population are
producing "a new, global sense of Chineseness that transcends national
boundaries and citizenship status" — an identity increasingly
sustained through cultural and economic connection rather than through
formal legal citizenship alone. Many overseas Chinese, having received
their elementary and secondary education within China before pursuing
tertiary study abroad, retain substantial loyalty to the Chinese state
and Communist Party; their own foreign-born children, in turn, often
attend OCAO-run summer camps specifically designed to connect them
with their Chinese heritage before they return to their permanent
lives overseas — a deliberate, multi-generational strategy for
sustaining diaspora connectivity that might otherwise erode after the
first generation.

## A Brief Comparison: The Indian Diaspora

Kenny notes that the Indian government pursues broadly comparable
diaspora-engagement strategies, though across a differently
distributed global population: long-established and substantial
Indian communities exist in Nepal, Malaysia, Myanmar, Singapore, South
Africa, Mauritius, Trinidad and Tobago, and Fiji, with more recent, large-scale
migration to the United Kingdom (since World War II), to the United
States, Canada, and Australia (since the 1960s), and more recently to
the labor-hungry, oil-rich countries of the Persian Gulf — a
destination, notably, where migrant workers (disproportionately
Muslim) are typically denied citizenship and diaspora settlement tends
to remain comparatively short-term and precarious, a meaningfully
different diaspora pattern than the multi-generational settlement more
characteristic of the Chinese case in North America, Europe, and
Southeast Asia.

## Missiological Reflection

For Christian workers among Chinese diaspora communities specifically,
Kenny's analysis carries a direct practical implication: Chinese
diaspora identity today is being actively, deliberately sustained
across generations through cultural, educational, and economic
mechanisms, not simply left to fade through natural assimilation
pressure as earlier generations of migration scholarship sometimes
assumed would happen to any diaspora over time. A ministry strategy
that assumes second- or third-generation overseas Chinese will have
lost meaningful connection to Chinese identity and homeland
orientation risks badly misjudging the actual, sustained strength of
that connectivity — with real consequences for how gospel
communication, discipleship, and eventual "mission through diaspora"
strategies (explored in this module's other files) should be designed
for congregations whose members remain genuinely, actively connected
to a Chinese identity that spans borders, not merely a fading memory
of one.
