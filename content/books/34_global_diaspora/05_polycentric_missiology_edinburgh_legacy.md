# Polycentric Missiology: From Everywhere to Everywhere

## Overview

This file synthesizes Allen Yeh's *Polycentric Missiology:
Twenty-First-Century Mission from Everywhere to Everywhere*, a history
and theological analysis of four major 2010 centenary conferences
commemorating the landmark 1910 Edinburgh World Missionary Conference
— held in Tokyo, Edinburgh, Cape Town, and Boston. Yeh's central
argument is that the very existence of four distinct, geographically
scattered successor conferences (rather than one obvious inheritor)
is itself the clearest possible demonstration of how thoroughly global
mission has become polycentric since 1910 — a direct, structural
complement to this module's treatment of diaspora, since polycentric
mission and diaspora mission both describe the same underlying shift
away from a single Western missionary center sending to a passive
"rest" of the world.

## From Unilateral to Polycentric and Polydirectional

Yeh's thesis directly addresses a natural question: which of the four
2010 centenary conferences is the "true" successor to Edinburgh 1910?
His answer is that all four are needed together, and that this very
multiplicity is the point. A century ago, mission proceeded largely
unilaterally and unidirectionally — summarized in the once-common
phrase "from the West to the rest." Today, in light of the broader
phenomenon of world Christianity, mission has become genuinely
*polycentric* and *polydirectional*: from everyone, everywhere, to
everywhere. No single conference, and no single continent, does
mission "best" in some uniquely authoritative sense; each
contributes a genuinely valuable, non-redundant piece of a larger,
necessarily plural picture.

Yeh is transparent about his own positioning in writing this history:
of Asian heritage (his parents first-generation immigrants from Taiwan
and China), born in Guam, educated in Scotland and England, with a
research specialization in Latin America, and having visited over
sixty countries across six continents — yet, by his own account, still
formed by a thoroughly American upbringing (Southern California) and
Western academic training. He treats this as a genuine limitation on
any claim to fully represent the perspectives of everyone involved in
these four conferences, modeling the same kind of epistemic humility
this whole module's treatment of diaspora and polycentrism calls for.

## The Legacy — and the Limits — of Edinburgh 1910

Edinburgh 1910 carries the popular nickname "The Birthplace of the
Modern Ecumenical Movement," a title historian Brian Stanley (cited
extensively by Yeh) argues rests on three distinct grounds: a
retrospective connection to William Carey's earlier (1810) proposal
for an ecumenical missions conference; a forward-looking connection
to the founding of the World Council of Churches in 1948, which traces
its own lineage directly back to Edinburgh; and the conference
organizers' and participants' own considerable sense, at the time, of
their gathering's historical significance. Yeh's history goes on to
examine where Edinburgh 1910 fell short by later standards — most
notably its near-total silence on the nascent Pentecostal movement,
whose founding revivals (in Wales in 1904, India in 1905, Chile in
1906, and Korea in 1907) occurred right around the same period as
Edinburgh but had no institutional connection to the conference's
organizing missionary societies, and were consequently ignored by it
even as Pentecostalism went on to become one of the most significant
forms of global Christianity encountered a century later.

## Pentecostalism as an Early Instance of Polycentric Revival

Yeh treats the geographically scattered, mutually unconnected origins
of early twentieth-century Pentecostal revival — Wales, India, Chile,
and Korea, none derivative of North American mission activity — as an
early, almost prophetic instance of the polycentric pattern his book's
broader thesis identifies as characteristic of contemporary world
Christianity. He notes Pentecostalism's genuinely ecumenical
character in practice (encompassing both Catholic and Protestant
expressions, transcending denominational lines) and its geographic
diversity today (thriving in settings as different as China, Brazil,
Nigeria, and Canada), theologically grounding this global,
decentralized spread in the Spirit's own character as believers'
present Immanuel, in contrast to the Father and Son enthroned in
heaven — a pneumatological argument for why a genuinely global,
non-centralized form of Christian revival might be theologically
fitting rather than merely a historical accident.

## An Honest Accounting of the 2010 Conferences' Own Shortcomings

Yeh's history is not simply celebratory. He specifically flags the
2010 conferences' comparatively weak inclusion of youth voices as an
area where the centenary gatherings actually fell short of Edinburgh
1910's own legacy — despite explicit target representation goals (50
percent women, 30 percent youth) set for the Cape Town 2010
conference, youth participation reportedly reached only about 3
percent, with young delegates present mainly in steward roles rather
than as full participants with voice, even though it was youthful
energy and vision that had originally animated much of the Edinburgh
1910 movement itself. Yeh treats this discrepancy as a genuine,
unresolved shortfall rather than glossing over it in an otherwise
celebratory account of the polycentric mission movement's progress.

## Missiological Reflection

Yeh's polycentric thesis and this module's treatment of diaspora
missiology describe the same underlying historical shift from two
complementary angles: diaspora missiology (covered in this module's
other files) traces how migrating peoples themselves have become
agents of mission "to, through, and beyond" their diaspora
communities, while polycentric missiology traces the same
decentering at the level of global mission conferences, institutions,
and theological leadership — with no single Western center any longer
positioned to speak for or organize world Christianity's mission
activity uncontested. Read together, both sources argue that
twenty-first-century mission demands a genuinely multipolar posture:
Chinese, Korean, Latin American, African, and other Majority World
churches are not merely recipients of a mission history authored
elsewhere, but active co-authors of what comes next — provided, as
Yeh's honest accounting of the 2010 conferences' own shortcomings
reminds us, that genuine representation (including of the young)
keeps pace with the rhetoric of polycentric inclusion.
