# The Concept of Diaspora: Origins, Meaning, and Three Key Dimensions

## Overview

This file synthesizes the historical and conceptual foundations of
"diaspora" as an analytical term, drawing on Kevin Kenny's *Diaspora:
A Very Short Introduction* (Oxford University Press). It traces the
word's origin in Jewish history, the etymology that gave the term its
distinctive theological coloring, and Kenny's mature synthesis of
diaspora around three interrelated dimensions — movement,
connectivity, and return — along with his sober warnings about how
easily the concept can be used carelessly.

## Where the Word Comes From

The word "diaspora" derives from the Greek verb *diaspeirein*
("to scatter" or "disperse") and its related noun *diasporá*. These
terms entered wide circulation through the Septuagint, the Greek
translation of the Hebrew Scriptures produced by Jewish scholars in
Alexandria around 250 BCE. Within the Septuagint, *diaspeirein*
appears more than forty times and *diasporá* about a dozen times,
consistently describing a condition of spiritual anguish and
dissolution accompanying the dispersal of the Jewish people —
translating the Hebrew term for a scattering understood as divine
judgment, not merely a neutral description of migration. This
theological weight — dispersal as consequence, exile as spiritual
condition — remains embedded in the term even as it has since been
extended to describe many other migrant populations with very
different histories and religious framings.

## The Jewish Diaspora: A Founding Narrative

Most accounts of the Jewish diaspora conventionally begin with the
Babylonian captivity of 586 BCE, when Nebuchadnezzar II conquered
Judah, destroyed the Jerusalem Temple, and carried the Jewish elite
into exile in Babylon. But the biblical narrative itself locates the
pattern of migration, suffering, and eventual return much earlier —
in Abraham's original journey from Babylonia to Canaan, the subsequent
migration to Egypt under Joseph's protection, and Moses's later
deliverance of the Israelites back toward the promised land. Kenny
observes that this cycle of migration, suffering, and return is so
foundational to Jewish self-understanding that the history the Jewish
people have told about themselves, from the very beginning, has been
substantially structured around the idea of diaspora — regardless of
questions about the Bible's historical accuracy as such. Later
historical ruptures compounded this pattern: the Roman destruction of
Jerusalem in 70 CE (commemorated on the Arch of Titus, still standing
in Rome) and the definitive Roman suppression of a further Jewish
revolt in 135 CE, after which the Jewish people lacked an independent
state until the founding of modern Israel in 1948.

## Three Interrelated Dimensions: Movement, Connectivity, Return

Kenny's most useful contribution for missiological purposes is his
attempt to rescue "diaspora" from conceptual overuse by anchoring it
in three interrelated dimensions that, taken together, distinguish a
genuinely diasporic population from migration in general:

**Movement** — the actual historical dispersal of a population from
an original homeland, whether through conquest and exile (as with the
Jewish and Armenian cases), through slavery (the Atlantic slave trade
that scattered African peoples across the Americas), through famine
and economic pressure (the Irish diaspora following the Great
Famine), or through labor migration and family-network-driven
resettlement (much of the modern Chinese and Indian diasporas).

**Connectivity** — the ongoing political, economic, and cultural
connections diaspora populations maintain both with a homeland (real
or imagined) and with fellow diasporans scattered in other locations
around the world. This dimension explains why diaspora communities so
often function as genuine transnational networks rather than simply
as assimilated minorities within their host societies — connectivity,
not mere common ancestry, is what makes a diaspora a diaspora in an
ongoing, living sense.

**Return** — the idea, whether pursued as literal physical relocation
or held more often as a persistent cultural and emotional orientation,
of eventual return to a homeland. Kenny stresses that this dimension
operates metaphorically as much as literally: many diaspora
populations sustain a powerful "myth of return" across generations
without any realistic expectation, or even desire, of actually
relocating — the orientation toward homeland itself, not the physical
act of returning, is what matters for diaspora identity.

## A Caution Against Overuse

Kenny is emphatic that diaspora, precisely because the word "has a
nice ring to it," risks becoming a fashionable label applied so
broadly that it loses analytical value — reducing complex, varied
migration histories to a single, flattened narrative type. He warns
against letting a single dramatic historical episode (the Babylonian
captivity, the Atlantic slave trade, the Irish famine, the Armenian
genocide) stand in as a shorthand substitute for a population's
entire, more varied migration history, and against uncritically
extending analogies of exile, racism, or genocide to migrations that
do not actually share those features. He also flags a subtler
distortion: diaspora discourse can sometimes take the views of a
small, vocal elite as representative of an entire dispersed
population, producing something closer to a top-down national
narrative than an accurate account of ordinary migrants' actual, more
varied experience.

## Missiological Reflection

Kenny's three-dimensional framework gives Christian workers serving
diaspora populations — a task central to this whole module — a
disciplined way to ask whether a specific migrant community is
genuinely diasporic in a meaningful sense (sustained homeland
connectivity, a live orientation toward eventual return, whether
literal or symbolic) or whether "diaspora" is being applied to them
more loosely, as a fashionable label without much explanatory power.
His caution against letting an elite's narrative stand in for an
entire population's experience is equally relevant to mission
practice: understanding a specific diaspora congregation requires
attending to the actual diversity of experience within it, not
assuming every member relates to homeland, return, and identity in
exactly the same way.
