# The Chinese Church in the USA: An Empirical Case Study in Diaspora Mission

## Overview

This file synthesizes *Mission through Diaspora: The Case of the
Chinese Church in the USA*, a dissertation-level empirical study
combining survey research with missiological analysis of how Chinese
churches in the United States actually organize and prioritize their
mission activity. Unlike the more theoretical frameworks covered
elsewhere in this module, this source offers real, quantitative
survey data (drawing on 273 responding churches) about what Chinese
diaspora congregations in America actually do, not merely what
missiological theory suggests they might or should do.

## The "Chinese First" Principle

The study confirms, through its own survey data, a pattern earlier
identified by researcher Fenggang Yang in *Chinese Christians in
America*: a widely shared "Chinese first" principle of ministry
priority among Chinese churches in the United States. Given that
mainland Chinese and diaspora Chinese together constitute
approximately 1.4 billion people — roughly one-fifth of the world's
population — and that China itself remains among the world's largest
concentrations of people without confessed Christian faith, many
overseas Chinese Christians hold a strong sense of responsibility to
reach their own people first. The survey data bears this out
concretely: when asked about prioritized ministry concerns, 73 percent
of responding churches ranked "missions to Chinese" as their single
top priority.

This pattern distinguishes Chinese congregations notably from two
comparison groups examined in the same study: US megachurches and US
Korean churches, both of which rank "missions to the unreached" as
their own top priority, with "missions to Chinese" (or an equivalent
ethnic-specific priority) appearing only as a secondary concern for
Korean congregations. By contrast, only 28 percent of Chinese churches
in the study placed "missions to the unreached" as their own top
priority (with 33.6 percent ranking it second) — a striking difference
the study attributes to Chinese congregations' unusually strong sense
of responsibility for their own kinsmen's salvation. Interestingly,
despite this difference in top priority, Chinese, Korean, and
megachurch respondents converged on the same second and third
priorities (reaching the unreached and church planting) and the same
fourth priority (theological education) — suggesting a shared broader
missiological outlook beneath the distinctive ethnic-specific
emphasis.

## Three Progressive Stages of Chinese-American Mission Practice

Drawing on missions strategist Zephaniah Yu's fourfold typology
("Local-Local," "Local-Global," "Global-Local," and "Global-Global"),
the study's own survey data reveals three empirically identifiable
stages through which US Chinese churches actually progress in their
mission engagement, each mapped directly onto the "to/through/beyond"
diaspora missiology framework covered in this module's companion
file:

**Stage 1: Local-Local (mission through diaspora).** Churches at this
stage focus outreach specifically on ethnic Chinese people within
their own local community — new immigrants, international students,
and workers — without yet organizing their own overseas short-term
mission trips. About 28 percent of surveyed churches fit this profile.
These churches tend to be smaller and, notably, are significantly less
likely to support long-term missionaries overseas at all (a
statistically significant correlation in the study's data), suggesting
this stage functions as something like a foundational or entry-level
phase of diaspora mission engagement rather than its mature endpoint.

**Stage 2: Global-Local (mission through diaspora).** Churches at
this stage organize short-term mission trips specifically to ethnic
Chinese populations — in mainland China, Hong Kong, Taiwan, or Chinese
communities in other countries — while continuing their local
ministry to Chinese people in their own community. This is the
majority pattern among churches that send short-term mission teams at
all: about 69 percent of churches with active short-term mission
programs in the study's reference year primarily ministered to ethnic
Chinese on those trips.

**Stage 3: Global-Global (mission by/beyond diaspora).** Churches at
this most advanced stage organize short-term mission trips reaching
not only their own ethnic kinsmen but people of other ethnicities in
other countries entirely — about 22 percent of short-term-mission-sending
churches in the study ministered to both Chinese and
non-Chinese populations on their trips, with 9 percent ministering
primarily to non-Chinese populations. These churches are
significantly more likely to support a larger number of long-term
missionaries overseas, and their long-term missionaries are
significantly less likely to be focused specifically on Chinese
ministry — instead prioritizing causes like evangelizing the Muslim
world, reaching the unreached generally, and addressing poverty. The
data suggests these Global-Global churches represent a genuine
missiological maturation beyond ethnic-specific mission, achieved
through — rather than in spite of — the earlier ethnic-specific stages.

## Missiological Reflection

This empirical progression offers an important corrective to any
simplistic missiological argument that ethnic-specific ("Chinese
first") mission priority is inherently a limitation to be overcome as
quickly as possible. The study's own data suggests the opposite
trajectory in practice: churches most committed to reaching their own
diaspora community in the earlier stages are not thereby permanently
confined to that focus, but appear, over time and through the
practical experience of doing short-term missions among their own
people first, to develop the capacity and confidence to engage
increasingly cross-cultural, "Global-Global" mission engagement — an
empirical vindication, in this specific case, of exactly the kind of
"mission through and beyond diaspora" progression Wan, Tira, and Payne's
theoretical framework anticipates, now documented with real survey
data rather than argued only in the abstract.
