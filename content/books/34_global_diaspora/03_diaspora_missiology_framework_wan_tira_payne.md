# Diaspora Missiology: A Framework for Mission To, Through, and Beyond

## Overview

This file synthesizes the theological and missiological framework
developed in *Doing Diaspora Missiology Toward "Diaspora Mission
Church,"* which draws extensively on the pioneering work of Enoch Wan
and Sadiri Joy Tira (closely associated with the Lausanne Movement's
engagement with global migration) and on J. D. Payne's influential
formulation of mission "to, through, and beyond" the diasporas. It
also surveys two complementary theological voices — the Bijbel in
Meervoud/BIAMS network in Europe and Daniel Groody's Center for Latino
Spirituality and Culture (CLSC) — that have each contributed distinct
emphases to the wider diaspora missiology conversation.

## Why Diaspora Missiology Emerged When It Did

The book situates the emergence of diaspora missiology as a
missiological discipline within the demographic realities of
contemporary globalization. Citing data from "Faith on the Move: The
Religious Affiliation of International Migrants," it notes that nearly
half of the world's international migrants (49 percent) are
Christians — meaning global migration is not simply a challenge
mission must respond to from the outside, but is itself already, in
large part, a movement of Christian people across borders. Jehu
Hanciles, a missiologist at Emory University's Candler School of
Theology, is quoted making an even stronger historical claim: every
so-called "world religion" achieved its global reach through
migration and dispersion, "none more so than Christianity" — situating
contemporary diaspora mission not as a novel phenomenon but as
Christianity's oldest and most characteristic growth pattern,
newly visible again under conditions of accelerated globalization.
Since 2004, the Lausanne Movement, in partnership with Wan and Tira
specifically, has worked systematically to develop diaspora missiology
as a distinct field of missiological reflection and practice.

## Mission To, Through, and Beyond the Diasporas

J. D. Payne's widely cited formulation — mission "to," "through," and
"beyond" the diasporas — supplies the book's organizing missiological
logic for thinking about diaspora populations not merely as a mission
field to be reached, but as active participants in God's mission in
at least three distinct, complementary directions:

**Mission TO diaspora** describes the straightforward task of
evangelizing and discipling diaspora populations themselves within
their new host countries — treating migrants and diaspora communities
as a genuine, often underserved mission field in their own right,
frequently living within easy reach of existing churches that have
not yet engaged them.

**Mission THROUGH diaspora** describes diaspora believers themselves
functioning as missionaries within their host society — reaching
fellow diaspora members of their own ethnic community, and potentially
the wider host population, precisely because their bicultural
position gives them relational and linguistic access many outside
missionaries lack.

**Mission BEYOND diaspora** describes diaspora believers carrying the
gospel onward — back to their country of origin, or forward to third
countries — leveraging the same transnational connectivity (in Kenny's
sense, covered in a companion file) that sustains diaspora identity
in the first place as a channel for gospel transmission across
borders that a purely locally rooted church could not as easily cross.

## The Church and Mission Cannot Be Separated: "Diaspora Mission Church"

The book's central constructive proposal, building on Lesslie
Newbigin's insistence that mission is properly ecclesial (a function
of the church's own nature, not a specialized activity delegated to a
subset of especially motivated members), is the concept of a
"Diaspora Mission Church" — a congregation that builds diaspora
awareness and diaspora mission into the whole fabric of its ordinary
church life, on the theological conviction that diaspora believers
are, by their very transnational position, already positioned as
missionaries whether or not they have been formally commissioned as
such. On this view, church and mission cannot be treated as separable
categories for a diaspora congregation — the diaspora church's
ordinary existence already constitutes a missionary reality.

## Complementary Voices: BIAMS and CLSC

Two further theological streams enrich the book's framework. The
European BIAMS network frames diaspora mission around the theme of
"embrace of the marginalized" — connecting the *missio Dei* directly
to the church's calling to stand in solidarity with, rather than
merely minister toward, diaspora populations who are often socially
and economically marginal within their host societies. Separately,
Daniel Groody's Center for Latino Spirituality and Culture (drawing on
a 2008 edited volume, *A Promised Land, A Perilous Journey*) proposes
four theological frameworks for engaging immigrants specifically:
recognizing every immigrant's full *imago Dei* status requiring active
restoration of dignity too often denied them; pursuing reconciliation
within the "one body" of Christ, with forgiveness extended across
immigration-related social divisions; grounding immigrant ministry in
the *missio Dei*, Christ's own redemptive work applied without
discrimination; and holding a *visio Dei*, a vision of God's kingdom
realized without the exclusionary borders that so often structure
migrants' actual lived experience.

## Missiological Reflection

The "to/through/beyond" framework corrects a common but limiting
assumption — that diaspora populations are simply a mission field to
be reached and nothing more — by insisting that diaspora believers are
simultaneously mission *field*, mission *force*, and mission *bridge*
all at once, often within the very same congregation. The
complementary emphases of BIAMS (marginalization and solidarity) and
CLSC (imago Dei, reconciliation, missio Dei, and visio Dei) further
insist that diaspora missiology cannot be reduced to strategic
efficiency alone (diaspora networks as a clever channel for gospel
distribution) without also reckoning honestly with the real social
marginalization and dignity-denial many diaspora populations
experience in their host societies — a caution directly relevant to
how the "Diaspora Mission Church" concept should actually be lived
out, not just theorized.
