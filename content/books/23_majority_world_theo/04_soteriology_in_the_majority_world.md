# Soteriology in the Majority World

## Overview

This file synthesizes Part Four of *Majority World Theology: Christian
Doctrine in Global Context* (ed. Gene L. Green, Stephen T. Pardue, and
K. K. Yeo), on the doctrine of salvation. The section opens with an
overview of eight distinct Western soteriological traditions before
turning to African, Latin American, Indigenous Canadian, and Korean
reflections that consistently press the same underlying question:
does salvation address only the individual soul's standing before God,
or does it also address bodily, social, political, and cosmic
brokenness?

## Western Soteriologies and Their Chief Gap

Daniel J. Treier surveys eight Western soteriological traditions —
Orthodox, Catholic, Lutheran, Calvinist, Anabaptist, Arminian,
Wesleyan/Holiness, and Pentecostal — before offering both internal and
external critique. His central diagnosis, drawing on liberationist and
Majority World perspectives, is that traditional Western dogmatic
accounts of salvation focus too exclusively on the personal blessings
of participation in the new covenant, leaving the sociopolitical and
cosmic dimensions of salvation as the chief lacuna in an otherwise
rich theological inheritance. Treier's own constructive proposal is
that "new creation" — the theme the Old Testament prophets consistently
wrap around their expectation of a new covenant — can supply what is
missing, without requiring that sociopolitical and cosmic concerns be
severed from the new covenant's genuinely personal elements.

## Africa: Telling Our Stories of Salvation Amid Real Suffering

Emily J. Choge Kerama opens with the stark juxtaposition at the heart
of her chapter: Africa remains ravaged by Ebola, HIV/AIDS, malaria,
hunger, and extreme poverty, yet it is precisely in Africa that the
center of gravity of global gospel growth has shifted. She presses
hard questions few soteriological treatments ask directly: is
salvation, in the African context, understood as genuinely addressing
these existential and material crises, or does it function instead as
an escape hatch from them? Writing as a second-generation African
Christian whose own family's faith traces back to the earliest
missionaries who landed on the Kenyan coast in 1844, Kerama argues for
a holistic understanding of salvation in the African context capable
of "grappling with" the very real powers and principalities — spiritual
and material — that continue to afflict African communities, rather
than offering salvation as pure spiritual escape from them.

## Botswana: Whose Liberation Does Luke 4:18-19 Announce?

Rosinah Mmannana Gabaitse examines how Luke 4:18-19 — Jesus'
Nazareth manifesto, foundational to Pentecostal self-understanding in
Botswana — has consistently been read in a heavily "spiritualized"
way: spiritual blindness, spiritual poverty, spiritual oppression and
release, with little or no attention to the sociopolitical and
physical dimensions of blindness, poverty, and oppression the same
text names. Gabaitse's specific concern is how this spiritualizing
interpretive habit functions to minimize, ignore, or explain away the
concrete marginalization of women, who in her account experience a
"dual patriarchy" from both indigenous cultural structures and the
Pentecostal church itself — meaning women are effectively denied the
holistic, this-worldly liberation Jesus' own manifesto announces,
even within congregations that recite that same manifesto as central
to their identity.

## Latin America: The Materiality of Salvation

Milton Acosta examines prayers of individual lament in the Psalms
(where petitioners frequently describe themselves as poor and
oppressed, appealing to God for deliverance from concrete social
evils, e.g. Ps 35:10) alongside New Testament material and the
documents of Latin American liberation theology and the Latin
American Theological Fellowship (Fraternidad Teológica
Latinoamericana), arguing that the continuity of *material* salvation
from Old to New Testament is considerably stronger than a soteriology
overly dominated by Paul's theology of suffering has tended to
recognize. Jules A. Martínez-Olivieri's companion chapter, drawing its
title from Eduardo Galeano's 1971 *Open Veins of Latin America* (a
searing account of the economic exploitation wrought by the region's
power relations with Europe and the United States), similarly
construes salvation as a divinely enacted, historically situated
liberation from both personal and collective expressions of sin,
restoring communion with both God and neighbor — while noting real
differences between how Catholic and Protestant Latin American
theologians have worked this concept out.

## Indigenous Canada: Repentance as Embracing True Identity, Not Erasing It

Ray Aldred confronts a painful historical inversion directly: under
Canadian colonial policy, "salvation" for Indigenous peoples was
explicitly defined as *becoming Western* — conversion meant repenting
of one's indigenous identity itself, putting it off in favor of
enfranchisement into settler Canadian society. Aldred's contextual
Cree theology reverses this colonial logic entirely: repentance is
reinterpreted as turning to Christ precisely *by* embracing a
God-given indigenous identity as a true human being, understood as the
Creator's fulfillment of what traditional Cree spirituality had always
longed for, rather than its erasure. Aldred extends this
reinterpretation beyond Indigenous readers themselves, arguing it is
equally necessary for non-Indigenous North Americans, who need their
own act of repentance — honestly reckoning with this colonial history —
as a step on the shared journey toward reconciliation.

## Korea: Salvation as Reconciliation Across a Divided Peninsula

Sung Wook Chung notes that reconciliation — as opposed to conversion,
regeneration, justification, and the other categories that have
historically dominated Western Protestant soteriology since the
Reformation — received comparatively little attention from classical
Lutheran and Reformed dogmatics. Chung develops reconciliation as the
central soteriological thread specifically in light of the ongoing
division between North and South Korea, arguing that salvation
properly includes not only vertical reconciliation between God and
humanity but horizontal reconciliation between human beings and
peoples — drawing on trinitarian theology to ground a vision of mutual
forgiveness, embrace, and love capable of addressing one of the
world's most intractable political divisions from within Christian
soteriology itself, rather than treating reconciliation between the
two Koreas as a purely secular political question outside soteriology's
proper scope.

## Missiological Reflection

Across every context surveyed in Part Four — African disease and
poverty, Botswana's patriarchal double bind, Latin American economic
exploitation, Canadian colonial identity-erasure, and the divided
Korean peninsula — the same pattern recurs: a soteriology inherited
from the West that has tended to spiritualize salvation into a
personal, otherworldly transaction is being pressed by Majority World
theologians to recover salvation's material, social, and political
dimensions, without thereby abandoning salvation's personal and
spiritual core. The consistent claim is not that Western soteriology
is simply wrong, but that it is *incomplete* — and that Majority World
contexts, precisely because they cannot afford to treat suffering as
merely spiritual, are positioned to help the whole church recover a
fuller biblical account of what salvation actually addresses.
