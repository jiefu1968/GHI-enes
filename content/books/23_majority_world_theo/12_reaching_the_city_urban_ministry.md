# Reaching the City: The Strategic Case for Urban Ministry

## Overview

This file synthesizes selected chapters of *Reaching the City:
Reflections on Urban Mission for the Twenty-First Century* (ed. Gary
Fujino, Timothy R. Sisk, and Tereso C. Casiño, William Carey
Publishing), a multi-author volume addressing why and how the global
church should prioritize urban ministry, given the massive and
accelerating worldwide shift of population into cities — a shift with
direct relevance for the growth of Christianity across the Majority
World, since the fastest-growing cities are increasingly concentrated
there.

## The Evangelical Retreat from the City

Alan McMahan opens by naming an uncomfortable history: white
evangelicals over the last half-century have largely viewed the city
with suspicion, distrust, and criticism — vilified as a "cesspool of
violence, moral decay, and poverty" — leading post-World War Two white
evangelicals to flee to the suburbs on newly built highways.
Theology, in McMahan's account, largely complied with this
demographic shift rather than critiquing it: seminaries and Bible
colleges followed the same flight to cheaper land and more culturally
compatible audiences, and missionaries and pastors were recruited
from suburban churches and sent to rural and suburban contexts abroad,
working with more culturally familiar and often more immediately
receptive peoples. McMahan's diagnosis is that this pattern cost the
evangelical church a genuinely strategic advantage in the worldwide
expansion of the church and its mission.

## Ten Strategic Realities of the City

McMahan structures his positive case around ten "strategic realities"
that should reshape how the evangelical church thinks about urban
ministry:

1. **Cities are where the people are** — 2008 marked the first point
in human history when more than half the world's population lived in
urban areas, a trend only accelerating.
2. **High density creates opportunity** — the sheer concentration of
people in cities multiplies potential gospel contacts per unit of
missionary effort compared to sparser rural contexts.
3. **The city brings freedom** — urban anonymity and mobility can
free individuals from rigid rural social and religious constraints,
sometimes making them more open to considering the gospel.
4. **Cities are filled with receptive people** — contrary to the
common assumption that cities are uniquely resistant to the gospel,
displaced and disoriented urban newcomers are often unusually open.
5. **The nations are moving to the city** — global urbanization means
unreached peoples who were once scattered and distant are increasingly
concentrated and locally accessible.
6. **The city is going to the world** — ideas, media, and cultural
influence generated in cities spread outward globally, giving urban
ministry outsized cultural leverage.
7. **The city as the engine of culture** — cities generate the
cultural products and trends that shape entire societies, making them
strategic points of theological and cultural engagement.
8. **The city at the crossroads** — cities function as transportation
and communication hubs connecting otherwise disparate regions and
peoples.
9. **The city as a place for meeting human need** — urban
concentrations of poverty, displacement, and social need create
natural openings for holistic Christian ministry.
10. **The city as the place for reinvention** — people often move to
cities specifically to remake their lives and identities, a
psychological and social openness to change that can extend to
spiritual life as well.

## Toward an Evangelical Theology of the City

Stephen Strauss argues that ministry disconnected from solid
theological grounding will inevitably wither or collapse into
short-term expediency — a special danger, in his view, for the
difficult, resource-intensive task of urban ministry specifically.
Citing missiologist C. Henk Koetsier's diagnosis that European
churches have failed to cope with urban ministry because of a
"theological withdrawal" — a lack of critical, creative theological
reflection on what is actually happening in cities — Strauss turns to
sociologist and theologian Jacques Ellul's extensive writing on
modernity and the city as a still-relevant resource for developing a
robust contemporary theology of urban life and ministry, arguing that
Ellul's decades-old analysis of "the technical society" remains highly
significant for urban mission in the current century.

## Specific Urban Ministry Contexts

The volume includes chapters addressing specific dimensions of urban
ministry with direct relevance to Majority World contexts: the
missiological implications of "new majority world" urbanization (Gary
Fujino and John Cheong), a historical survey of the Christian church's
own past models of urban mission (Edward L. Smither), reaching
immigrant and diaspora communities within Western cities (Janice A.
McLean on immigrant congregations, Birgit Herppich on Ghanaian
Pentecostal ministry in urban America), a call for more and better
urban missiological research (J. D. Payne, whose *Pressure Points* is
covered elsewhere in this module), ministering to transitional urban
neighborhoods (Kathryn Lewis Mowry), and reaching Muslims specifically
through the local urban church (Mark Hausfeld) — extending this
module's broader engagement with contextual mission strategy into the
specifically urban settings where growing numbers of the world's
Muslim, migrant, and Majority World populations now actually live.

## Missiological Reflection

*Reaching the City*'s core argument dovetails directly with this
module's broader concern: if the church's center of gravity has
genuinely shifted toward the Majority World, and if the Majority
World's own population is rapidly concentrating in enormous cities,
then Majority World theology cannot remain a purely rural or
village-oriented project — it must reckon seriously with urban life as
the actual, lived context of a growing share of the global church. The
volume's ten strategic realities, read alongside its historical and
contemporary case studies, argue that urban ministry is not a niche
specialty within missiology but an increasingly central strategic
priority for reaching the nations at all.
