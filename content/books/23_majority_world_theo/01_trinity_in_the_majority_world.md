# The Trinity in the Majority World

## Overview

This file synthesizes Part One of *Majority World Theology: Christian
Doctrine in Global Context* (ed. Gene L. Green, Stephen T. Pardue, and
K. K. Yeo; IVP Academic / Langham), an anthology bringing together
theologians from Africa, Asia, Latin America, and Indigenous North
America to reflect on classical Christian doctrine from their own
contexts. Part One addresses the doctrine of God and the Trinity,
introduced by K. K. Yeo as offering "kaleidoscopic views of God" that
enrich rather than fragment the historic Christian confession.

## Africa: Historically Rooted, Presently Neglected

Samuel Waje Kunhiyop argues that neither God nor the concept of the
Trinity are foreign imports to Africa — belief in a Supreme Being
predates the arrival of both Christianity and Islam across the
continent, and Africa itself played a formative historical role in
trinitarian theology: the North African theologian Tertullian is
credited with coining the very word "Trinity," and the Ethiopian
Orthodox Church traces its trinitarian belief and practice back to
the fourth century. Kunhiyop's central argument, however, is a
critical one: despite this deep historical rootedness, classical
trinitarian belief is largely disregarded in practice by contemporary
African Christianity, a problem he considers especially urgent given
the millions of untaught new believers streaming into fast-growing
Pentecostal movements. He rejects the pastoral shortcut of telling
believers "don't expect to understand it, just believe it" as only
half true, and calls the African church back to deliberate trinitarian
catechesis, warning (citing Harold O. J. Brown) that without a
coherent doctrine of the Trinity, the New Testament's witness to
God's activity in Christ and the Spirit inevitably collapses into
either modalism or a form of tritheism.

## North American Indigenous Perspectives: Shalom Community

Randy S. Woodley proposes that the fourth-century church's
preoccupation with divine ontology — worked out under conditions of
Roman imperial power that later fed forms of Christian imperialism —
left the West unable to hold the mystery of the Trinity in relational
rather than metaphysical terms. He suggests that Indigenous North
American understandings of a "divine shalom community" may recover
something closer to how the earliest Jewish followers of Jesus
actually experienced the move from strict monotheism to a trinitarian
confession: not primarily a claim about divine "substance" but a
picture of the Creator existing eternally in shalom relationality — a
communal ontology that Woodley argues can lead contemporary
Christians beyond debates centered on ontological categories toward a
richer understanding of their own call to communal existence under
God.

## Asian Reformulations: Testing Indigenous Resources Against Scripture

Natee Tanchanpongs surveys four Asian theologians' attempts to
reformulate trinitarian doctrine using indigenous philosophical and
religious resources — Raimundo Panikkar and Brahmabandhab Upadhyaya
(drawing on Indian thought), Jung Young Lee (Korean), and Nozomu
Miyahira (Japanese) — evaluating each proposal for biblical fidelity
using what he calls a "context-to-text" model. This model insists
that engaging one's cultural context and affirming the primacy of
Scripture can coexist only if elements drawn from context remain
genuinely open to correction by the text, rather than the text being
silently reshaped to fit pre-existing cultural categories — a
methodological caution relevant well beyond the four case studies
examined, since the same question (does context inform the text, or
quietly override it?) recurs throughout Majority World theological
reflection.

## Motherliness of God: Reading Paul in a Maternal Society

Atsuhiro Asano, writing from Japan, observes that creedal language
beginning "We believe in God the Father," when preached without
qualification into a "maternal society" — one where people
instinctively expect to relate to others, including the divine, the
way a mother relates to her children — can unintentionally communicate
that Christianity is exclusively patriarchal, hindering the gospel's
reception. Rather than treating this as merely a communication
problem to route around, Asano reads Paul's letter to the
Galatians — written, ironically, by an author frequently accused of
being a chauvinist — attentively for maternal imagery already present
in Paul's own vision for nurturing the Christian community, arguing
that the God of Christian Scripture is not exclusively patriarchal
even on close textual grounds, independent of the interpreter's
cultural starting point.

## How to Understand a Biblical God in Chinese

Zi Wang examines the historically fraught "term question" debate
among Catholic and Protestant missionaries in China: which Chinese
term — *Shen* or *Shang-ti*, among others — most adequately renders
the biblical God's name within a linguistic and cultural context
shaped by Confucianism? Wang argues that this translation question is
not a merely technical linguistic puzzle but the core theological
issue in relating Christian faith to Chinese culture, and proposes a
crosscultural biblical hermeneutics built around Hans-Georg Gadamer's
concept of a "fusion of horizons" — engaging Paul's argument in Romans
1:16-17 in genuine dialogue with Confucian understandings of the
biblical God, rather than assuming either that Chinese categories can
simply absorb the biblical God unchanged, or that translation must
flatten Chinese categories entirely to preserve biblical fidelity.

## The Trinity as Gospel

Antonio González argues that the divinity of Jesus should not be
understood as standing in tension with Jewish monotheism, but as its
messianic fulfillment: Jewish monotheism was never simply a
metaphysical claim about God's uniqueness in the abstract, but an
affirmation of God's exclusive rule that relativized every other
claimant to power, including kings. Read against this background,
Jesus' own proclamation of the kingdom of God, and the church's very
early inclusion of the resurrected and exalted Jesus within the
identity of the one God — occurring, notably, within a still-Jewish
context rather than only after later contact with Hellenistic
philosophy — becomes intelligible as an outworking of, rather than a
departure from, Israel's monotheistic confession.

## Missiological Reflection

Read together, these essays model a consistent pattern: each author
takes with full seriousness both the normative content of classical
trinitarian doctrine (worked out at Nicaea and Chalcedon) and the
specific cultural resources, wounds, and blind spots of their own
context — using the latter neither to dissolve the former into
relativism nor to defer uncritically to Western conceptual categories
as the only legitimate vehicle for expressing it. The recurring
methodological question — how does context genuinely inform without
silently overriding the text? — anticipates the same tension explored
throughout the rest of this anthology's treatment of Christology,
pneumatology, soteriology, ecclesiology, and eschatology.
