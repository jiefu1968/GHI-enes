# Mangoes or Bananas? The Quest for an Authentic Asian Christian Theology

## Overview

This file synthesizes Hwa Yung's *Mangoes or Bananas? The Quest for an
Authentic Asian Christian Theology* (Regnum Studies in Mission), a
critical survey of Protestant Asian theological writing evaluated
against explicit criteria for what makes a theology of mission
adequate. The book's title supplies its own controlling metaphor,
developed fully in the closing postscript: much post-World War Two
Asian theology, Hwa Yung argues, has ended up looking more like a
banana than a mango.

## Dissatisfaction with Western Theology

Hwa Yung opens by naming, without minimizing, real reasons for
growing dissatisfaction with imported Western theology across Africa,
Asia, and Latin America — a dissatisfaction rooted in two related
concerns: Western theologies are products of specific Western
histories and realities that do not map neatly onto Majority World
experience, and Christians throughout what Hwa Yung calls the
"Two-Thirds World" increasingly seek a clearer sense of self-identity
that a wholesale importation of Western theological categories cannot
supply. This dissatisfaction, he argues, is not primarily driven by
theological liberalism or a desire to relativize truth, but by the
entirely reasonable observation that theology done well should engage
the actual history, culture, and lived experience of the people
doing it.

## Criteria for an Adequate Theology of Mission

Hwa Yung develops explicit criteria against which he then evaluates a
wide range of Asian Protestant theological writing (deliberately
excluding most Roman Catholic material except for the historically
pivotal seventeenth-century Jesuit missions to China and India). His
selection of representative Asian theologians and theological
movements for study follows three considerations held in careful
balance: the theologian or movement must genuinely address the
concerns of the peoples and church of Asia; must speak *from* the
perspective of the Asian church rather than merely *about* it; and
should have some level of recognized standing within the international
Christian community — though Hwa Yung is explicit that this third,
more externally validated criterion must never be allowed to override
the first two, since doing so would effectively let First World
theologians and gatekeepers act as "kingmakers" deciding which Asian
voices count as legitimately representative.

## Power Encounter and Hiebert's Excluded Middle in Asian Christianity

A recurring theme throughout Hwa Yung's study is the crucial
importance of missiologist Paul Hiebert's "excluded middle" concept
for understanding Asian worldviews — the observation that a two-tier
Western cosmology (natural science below, abstract theology above)
leaves no functional category for the middle tier of everyday
spiritual power and causation that shapes most Asian people's actual
lived religious experience. Hwa Yung documents that many of Asia's
liveliest Protestant churches exhibit vigorous "charismatic forms" of
Christianity — and is careful to note that these are not simply
imports from the Western Pentecostal-charismatic movement (which
traces back to the Holiness Movement and the Azusa Street Revival),
but frequently have independent indigenous roots traceable to earlier
Asian Christian leaders such as Sadhu Sundar Singh and John Sung, and
to various Asian revival movements only loosely connected to Western
charismatic renewal.

His critique, however, is pointed: the *theological interpretation*
Asian Christians use to understand and articulate this work of the
Spirit has too often been borrowed wholesale from Western Pentecostal
and charismatic sources — theology that frequently lacks careful
exegetical grounding and sound historical-theological perspective,
having emerged from fundamentalist circles where rigorous scholarship
was not always prized. Hwa Yung's constructive challenge to Asian
Christians is that, having already learned experientially to
appropriate the Spirit's power in addressing exactly the "excluded
middle" realm that Western theology has often struggled even to
recognize, it is time for Asian believers to formulate their own
biblically rigorous, historically informed theology of these
dynamics — rather than continuing to borrow uncritically from
Western frameworks that were never well-suited to the Asian
worldview in the first place.

## The Postscript: Mangoes and Bananas

Hwa Yung closes with the extended parable that gives the book its
title. Among fruits common in tropical Asia, the banana is of
uncertain geographic origin, while the mango is an authentically Asian
fruit. Ripe bananas are yellow outside but off-white inside when
peeled; ripe mangoes, by contrast, are typically golden on both the
outside and the inside. Hwa Yung's parable: most of the post-World
War Two examples of Asian theology he examined look more like bananas
than mangoes — "yellow" (recognizably Asian) on the outside, but
"white" (essentially Western in content and method) on the inside.
Asians may enjoy bananas, he grants, but there is no doubt the sweet,
succulent flesh of a genuinely Asian mango is prized more highly when
available — and if a choice must be made, the mango is preferable.

Hwa Yung is careful that this critique does not slide into rejecting
everything Western wholesale — noting the "inner contradiction" of any
Asian Christian asserting that everything bound up with the historical
process that brought them the gospel (including its Western vessel)
was simply and entirely bad. His own constructive vision, drawing on
Andrew Walls's paired "pilgrim principle" (the call to leave behind
what is incompatible with the gospel) and "indigenizing principle"
(the gospel's genuine capacity to be at home in every culture),
requires Asian Christians to recover confidence *both* in the gospel
itself *and* in their own culture and history simultaneously — since
without both together, Christianity, in his assessment, will never
become fully incarnated in Asian soil. His hoped-for fruit of more
theological "mangoes" would, he argues, give Asian Christianity a
clearer sense of self-identity and help heal divisions within Asian
Christianity that have often been imposed from outside rather than
arising organically from within.

## Missiological Reflection

Hwa Yung's mango-and-banana metaphor offers this curriculum's own
project — original theological synthesis drawn from majority-world and
non-Western sources rather than simple repetition of Western
categories — a useful diagnostic question to ask of its own work: does
a given piece of contextual theology genuinely think in indigenous
categories all the way through, or does it merely dress imported
Western categories in local cultural color? Hwa Yung's insistence that
authentic contextualization requires confidence in *both* the gospel's
full authority *and* one's own culture and history, held together
rather than traded off against each other, offers a helpful balance
against two opposite failure modes: theology that abandons doctrinal
substance in the name of cultural relevance, and theology that merely
translates Western content into local vocabulary without genuine
indigenous theological reasoning underneath.
