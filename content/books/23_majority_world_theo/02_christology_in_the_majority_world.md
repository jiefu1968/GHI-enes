# Christology in the Majority World

## Overview

This file synthesizes Part Two of *Majority World Theology: Christian
Doctrine in Global Context* (ed. Gene L. Green, Stephen T. Pardue, and
K. K. Yeo), which surveys how the person and work of Jesus Christ are
being reflected upon across the global church — from Kevin Vanhoozer's
framing of Western Christological conversation, through African,
Asian, Israeli-Palestinian, and Latin American perspectives, to the
volume's own closing question of whether contemporary global
Christology moves "beyond Chalcedon."

## Framing Christology as Discipleship (Kevin Vanhoozer)

Vanhoozer opens Part Two by defining Christology not as an abstract
academic exercise but as the doctrine of "living to follow Jesus
Christ" — faith seeking understanding of its central confession, "I
believe in the Lord Jesus Christ." Doing Christology means knowing
both who Jesus Christ is "yesterday and today and forever" (Heb 13:8)
and how to follow this same Jesus today, in different contexts —
discerning the *same* Christ in the midst of the *different* contexts
his followers inhabit. Drawing on historian Andrew Walls, Vanhoozer
frames the entire history of Christian mission as a history of
successive translations of the gospel into new languages, thought
forms, and cultural practices — the church, in transmitting the
gospel, simply imitating God's own prior missionary movement in
sending the Son and the Spirit.

## African Christology: Passing Two Tests

Victor I. Ezigbo proposes that any African Christian Christology must
pass two tests simultaneously: a test of "Africanness" (genuinely
engaging the experience, history, cultures, and religious aspirations
of African Christians) and a test of "Christian-ness" (remaining
faithful to the Christ presented in Scripture and articulated at the
ecumenical councils of Nicaea, Constantinople, and Chalcedon). Ezigbo
is candid that both tests are contested from within — African
Christian theologians themselves disagree about how much weight
African history and traditional religious aspiration should carry in
constructing Christology, just as Christian theologians generally
disagree about what counts as a faithful reading of the conciliar
formulations. A Christology failing either test, in his view, forfeits
the right to the name "African Christian Christology."

Andrew M. Mbuvi's chapter on 1 Peter offers a concrete case study in
this vein: he reads the letter's dense cultic language of sacrifice,
temple, and priesthood — beginning with believers described as
"sprinkled" with Christ's blood (1 Pet 1:2) — in dialogue with African
traditions of animal (and, in some communities, historically human)
sacrifice for purification, thanksgiving, protection from malevolent
spirits, and birth rites, asking whether beginning gospel presentation
in sub-Saharan Africa (as most Western missionaries historically did)
with categories drawn from Western legal or philosophical frameworks
missed a more immediately intelligible cultic and sacrificial entry
point already present in 1 Peter itself.

## Asian Christologies: The Struggle to Hear a Global Church

Timoteo D. Gener frames his survey of Asian Christologies around
William Dyrness's blunt diagnosis of the state of global theological
conversation — "I'm Not Hearing You" — arguing that despite an
increasingly globalized world, genuine listening between different
parts of the body of Christ remains rare. Gener insists that
intercultural hermeneutics is not an optional accessory to biblical
interpretation but a corrective necessity, since interpreters are
routinely unaware of how their own cultural assumptions limit their
reading of the gospel — a concern shared by the influential Willowbank
Report on Gospel and Culture (1978), which called Christians to
discern authentic contextualization from "shallow alternatives."

## Palestinian Eyes: Reading John's Gospel Amid Contested Identity

Yohanna Katanacho examines how deeply readings of John's Gospel are
shaped by social, political, and religious location within
Israel-Palestine specifically. He surveys sharply divergent readings:
Messianic Jews who read Jesus as the quintessential Jew shaped by
Jewish culture; other Israeli Jews troubled by John's portrayal of
"unbelieving" Jews as spiritually blind (8:44); some Israeli Jews who
avoid engaging Jesus altogether, referring to him only by a Hebrew
acronym meaning "may his name and memory be obliterated"; and
Palestinian Christians and even some Palestinian Muslims who read
Jesus' shared geopolitical location with contemporary Palestinians as
making him not only a historical Palestinian but an identification
figure embodying Palestinian hopes. Katanacho's point is not to
adjudicate between these readings but to make explicit that no
reading of John — including a Western academic one — occurs from a
neutral, unlocated vantage point.

## Latin American Christology: ¿Quién Vive? ¡Cristo!

Jules A. Martínez-Olivieri's chapter (title translated roughly as
"Who Lives? Christ!") explores Latin American Christologies in
conversation with liberation theology's insistence on holding together
"both heavenly savior and earthly liberator" — a formulation directly
engaged in the volume's concluding synthesis essay, which reads this
insistence as one more instance of the underlying ontological question
running through the whole Part: what it means for Christ to be fully
God and fully human, and how that ontology relates to the concrete,
earthly conditions (African indigenous spirituality, Latin American
liberation from oppression, Palestinian dispossession) each contextual
Christology takes as its starting point.

## Beyond Chalcedon? A Concluding Synthesis

K. K. Yeo's closing chapter directly engages Vanhoozer's methodological
proposal that "what is normative in Chalcedon is not the particular
concepts but the underlying judgments, not a particular metaphysical
scheme but the underlying biblical ontology." Yeo agrees that a
particular fifth-century metaphysical vocabulary should not be treated
as eternally binding in itself, but pushes back that the "underlying
judgments" Chalcedon sought to protect — the full deity and full
humanity of Christ in a single person — remain inescapably tied to
some ontological claim about Christ's own reality, since it is
precisely this reality (two natures in one person) that undergirds
the triune God's creation of the world. Yeo notes that this same
underlying issue resurfaces in every contextual Christology surveyed
in Part Two: African Christology's engagement with indigenous
spirituality, Latin American liberation theology's dual affirmation of
heavenly savior and earthly liberator, and the historical episode of
violence against the Coptic church (raised by Ezigbo) all wrestle,
each in their own idiom, with the same question of how Christ's
two-natured reality relates to concrete historical and cultural life —
suggesting that a plurality of culturally embedded Christologies need
not mean abandoning Chalcedon's underlying judgment, even where its
precise conceptual vocabulary is renegotiated.

## Missiological Reflection

Part Two models what a genuinely catholic Christology can look like:
neither a single culturally specific formulation exported worldwide as
the only legitimate form, nor a collection of merely local
Christologies with no shared reference point, but a sustained
conversation in which African, Asian, Palestinian, and Latin American
readings each press on the same underlying question — who is Jesus
Christ, in his full deity and full humanity — from their own concrete
historical vantage point, in a way that can genuinely enrich, rather
than dissolve, the church's shared confession.
