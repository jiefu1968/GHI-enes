# Ecclesiology in the Majority World

## Overview

This file synthesizes Part Five of *Majority World Theology: Christian
Doctrine in Global Context* (ed. Gene L. Green, Stephen T. Pardue, and
K. K. Yeo), on the doctrine of the church, drawing on Latin American,
Asian, and African case studies. Editor Gene L. Green's introduction
frames the section's central question: how does the church's identity
as one, holy, catholic, and apostolic (per the Nicene Creed) take
concrete shape in radically different social, political, and
religious environments — and what happens when that shape is forged
under conditions the Western tradition never had to face?

## Latin America: Church, Power, and an Alternative Citizenship

Ruth Padilla DeBorst traces the church's shifting relationship to
political power across Latin American history, opening with a vivid
scene: an ordination service in a small Baptist congregation outside
Buenos Aires, held in a country worn down by hyperinflation and
military dictatorship, where questioning the government's repressive
policies could get a citizen forcibly "disappeared." Against this
backdrop, Padilla DeBorst reads ecclesiology through what she calls
the "double movement" of the church as the people of the triune
Community-of-Love: living out God's reign by following Jesus in the
power and gifting of the Spirit, while simultaneously never making
itself fully at home in the political and economic arrangements of
the world. Her central thesis is that the church's holy and apostolic
calling itself constitutes it as "a school of citizenship" — forming
believers in a different, more resilient form of belonging than the
citizenship offered (and sometimes violently enforced) by the state.

## Asia: Two Free Churches and the Absence of Formal Ecclesiology

Wonsuk Ma observes that ecclesiology within Asian evangelical,
Pentecostal, and charismatic circles has been strikingly
under-articulated as formal doctrine, despite the explosive growth of
specific Asian churches. He examines two contrasting "free church"
case studies — the Word of Life movement in China and the Yoido Full
Gospel Church in Korea — noting that the Chinese case has developed
with essentially no ties to churches outside the country, while the
Korean case has developed its own distinctive self-understanding of
what the church is. Ma's broader argument is that in both cases, the
theological shape of ecclesiology has been driven far more by
individual founders and immediate social context than by any
deliberate engagement with the historic tradition of ecclesiological
reflection — a pattern he treats as urgent precisely because these two
communities represent some of the fastest-growing segments of
contemporary Asian Christianity, meaning their functional, largely
undertheorized ecclesiologies are shaping enormous numbers of
believers with little connection to the wider church's accumulated
reflection on what the church actually is.

## Africa: Apprentices on a Mission

Stephanie A. Lowery surveys the striking variety of ecclesiological
models found across the African continent — some patterned after
Israel, others after angelic hierarchies, and other indigenous
frameworks — noting that many of these models productively challenge
the wider world church to root itself more closely in Scripture, even
as some raise real tensions with the Nicene affirmation of the church
as one and catholic. Lowery is candid that churches with roots in
historic North Atlantic mission agencies carry a particular
responsibility here: the Nicene Creed's description of the church as
one, holy, catholic, and apostolic functions, in her reading, as a
pointed reminder to these churches specifically not to treat their own
Western-inherited forms as the unquestioned standard against which
indigenous African ecclesiological developments are measured. Her
constructive proposal links ecclesiology directly to the *missio Dei*
(the mission of God) as the organizing principle capable of holding
together African ecclesial diversity and Nicene catholicity without
collapsing either into the other.

## Missiological Reflection

A shared concern runs through all three contexts: the church's actual,
lived shape in Latin America, Asia, and Africa has frequently developed
under conditions (political repression, isolation from the wider
church, indigenous religious frameworks with no Western precedent)
that classical Western ecclesiology, worked out under very different
historical circumstances, did not anticipate and cannot always
adequately address. Rather than treating this as a problem to be
corrected by importing more Western ecclesiological categories, all
three authors treat it as an invitation: for the Latin American
church, to understand its holiness and apostolicity as forming
citizens of a different order than the state; for the Asian church, to
begin the overdue work of formal theological reflection on
ecclesiologies that have so far developed almost entirely by
function and founder-personality rather than intentional doctrine; and
for the African church, to hold together vigorous scriptural
rootedness, genuine indigenous form, and Nicene catholicity through a
shared missional purpose rather than uniformity of structure.
