# Theology in the Context of World Christianity: A Doctrine-by-Doctrine Tour

## Overview

This file synthesizes Timothy C. Tennent's *Theology in the Context of
World Christianity: How the Global Church Is Influencing the Way We
Think About and Discuss Theology* (Zondervan). Tennent structures the
book around a striking historical parallel and nine case studies, each
pairing a classical Christian doctrine with a specific non-Western
context where that doctrine is being reworked under real theological
pressure — Muslim-background believers reconsidering the doctrine of
God, Hindu-background Jesuits reconsidering revelation, and so on
through Christology, soteriology, pneumatology, ecclesiology, and
eschatology.

## The Emergence of a Global Theological Discourse

Tennent opens with Martin Luther's ninety-five theses — dismissed
initially by Pope Leo X as the noise of a "drunken German" and a mere
"squabble among monks," before the world realized Christianity and
Europe itself were undergoing a change that would alter the entire
course of Western civilization. Tennent's argument is that a
comparably profound, if less immediately obvious, shift is underway
today: for the first time in centuries, the church's theological
center of gravity is moving outside the West, and Western theologians
who dismiss this shift as peripheral risk repeating Leo X's mistake of
underestimating a genuinely epochal transition already in progress.

## Theology Proper: Is the Father of Jesus the God of Muhammad?

Tennent's second chapter opens with a well-known Hindu parable: a
theistic census-taker who spends a lifetime cataloguing every deity
worshiped across India (arriving at a traditional total of 330
million), only to conclude after seven more years of counting that
there is, in the end, "one God worshipped in India" — every particular
deity ultimately understood as a name for the same One Divine Reality.
Tennent uses this parable to contrast Eastern theological reflection
(a "semi-permeable membrane" between particular gods and the One) with
the West's more systematic, sharply delineated doctrine of God, before
turning to his real subject: how Arab Muslim-background followers of
Jesus themselves navigate the continuity and discontinuity between
their former faith in Allah and their new Christian faith — pressing
the genuinely difficult question of whether the Father of Jesus simply
*is* the God proclaimed by Muhammad, partially so, or something quite
different.

## Revelation: Can the Upanishads Prepare Someone for the Gospel?

Chapter three moves the reader into a theological conversation
dominated by Indian Jesuits reflecting on how Hindu-background
followers of Jesus regard the sacred texts of their pre-Christian
past. Tennent frames the central question sharply: can God use Hindu
scripture such as the Upanishads to genuinely prepare someone to
receive the Christian gospel's revelation, or does treating Hindu
texts as any kind of preparatory revelation compromise the uniqueness
of biblical revelation? This chapter surveys how other major world
religions understand inspiration and revelation more broadly, and how
that understanding has shaped Christian theological discourse
specifically within the Indian context.

## Anthropology: Guilt and Shame in the Far East

Chapter four turns to theological anthropology — the doctrine of what
it means to be human, and what has gone wrong — arguing that shame-
based cultures common across the Far East illuminate a dimension of
human alienation from God that guilt-focused Western anthropology has
tended to underweight. Tennent's claim is not that guilt is the wrong
category but that it is an incomplete one: full biblical anthropology
needs to reckon with both guilt (a violation of moral law) and shame
(a rupture of relational honor and belonging) to capture the Bible's
own full scope of human alienation from God.

## Christology: Christ as Healer and Ancestor in Africa

Tennent opens chapter five with a demographic observation now widely
repeated in missiological literature: the typical "face" of
Christianity in the twenty-first century is more likely to be
encountered in Lagos than in London, prompting predictions (citing
Andrew Walls and Philip Jenkins) that African Christianity may become
the representative Christianity of the century, and that Western
Christians may live to see the day "a white Christian" sounds as
mildly surprising as "a Swedish Buddhist." Tennent contrasts an
increasingly secularized, nominal Western church with a vibrant,
supernaturalist, largely orthodox Southern Christianity, before
diving into decades of African theological conversation about how to
communicate the doctrine of Christ using categories immediately
intelligible in African settings — specifically Christ as healer and
as ancestor — and asking how these distinctively African Christologies
relate to the historic Christological confession hammered out at
Chalcedon.

## Soteriology: Salvation by Grace Through Faith from a Pure Land Buddhist Background

Chapter six turns to Chinese and Japanese Christians converted out of
Pure Land Buddhism, a major expression of Mahayana Buddhism distinct
for possessing its own strong doctrine of salvation by grace through
faith (in Amida Buddha's saving vow) prior to any contact with
Christianity. Tennent treats this convergence as theologically
significant precisely because it means Christians from this
background bring their own prior, sophisticated grace-and-faith
framework into conversation with the Reformation's own central
doctrine of *sola fide* — a genuinely two-way theological conversation
rather than a one-directional teaching relationship.

## Pneumatology: Latin American Pentecostalism's Challenge to Western Neglect

Chapter seven addresses the dramatic fifty-year rise of Pentecostal
Christianity across Latin America and the renewed interest in
pneumatology this growth has generated, alongside the insistence of
many Latin American theologians that the person of the Holy Spirit
received insufficient attention in classical Western systematic
theology. Tennent poses the question directly: has Western theology
in fact neglected the Spirit, and if so, what specifically can Latin
American Pentecostal reflection contribute to strengthening the
wider church's pneumatology?

## Ecclesiology: Followers of Jesus in Islamic Mosques

Chapter eight returns to the Islamic world, this time addressing
ecclesiology through the controversial and rapidly growing phenomenon
of "churchless Christianity" — Muslims who profess genuine faith in
Jesus Christ but, out of fear of reprisal or because "Christian
church" carries strong connotations of Western colonialism and
foreignness in their setting, choose not to identify as Christians or
join a visible church, sometimes continuing to attend the mosque.
Tennent draws on Herbert Hoefer's research documenting such believers
in India to press a genuinely difficult ecclesiological question with
no easy resolution: can Muslim-background believers follow Jesus
Christ authentically while remaining within the social and religious
structures of the mosque and retaining a Muslim communal identity, or
does New Testament ecclesiology require some form of visible,
identifiable break?

## Eschatology: Jonathan Edwards and the Chinese Back to Jerusalem Movement

Tennent's final chapter opens with a cartoon of an eschatology teacher
who tells a questioning student, "No questions, please, I find they
disrupt the flow of my answers" — a wry acknowledgment of how
contested and fissured eschatological debate has always been within
the church (millennial timing, the nature and timing of the rapture,
whether "the last days" names a specific pre-return period or the
entire church age, and more). Tennent's substantive contribution pairs
Jonathan Edwards's eighteenth-century postmillennial eschatology with
the contemporary Chinese "Back to Jerusalem" movement — Chinese
Christians who understand their own missionary calling in explicitly
eschatological terms, framing the gospel's westward journey back
through Central Asia and the Middle East to Jerusalem as itself a sign
of the approaching consummation of history.

## Missiological Reflection

Tennent's consistent method — pairing a classical doctrine with a
specific, real theological conversation happening in a non-Western
context — makes a broader argument that runs beneath all nine
chapters: global theological discourse is not a matter of Western
theology being merely "applied" or "translated" into new settings
after the fact, but of genuinely new theological questions and
insights emerging from within these settings that press back on, and
can enrich, historic Christian doctrine as inherited from the West.
Just as few theologians in 1517 anticipated how thoroughly Luther's
protest would reshape Western Christianity, Tennent's implicit warning
is that Western theologians who treat these nine conversations as
peripheral curiosities risk missing a comparably significant shift
already well underway.
