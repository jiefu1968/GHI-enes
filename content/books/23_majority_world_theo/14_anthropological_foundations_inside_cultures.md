# Anthropological Foundations for Theological Contextualization

## Overview

This file synthesizes relevant material from *Inside Cultures*, an
introductory cultural anthropology textbook, focusing on the concepts
most directly useful for the theological and missiological
contextualization work that runs throughout this module: what
"culture" actually means, how anthropologists have historically
theorized the relationship between individuals and their social
group, and why these theoretical tools matter for anyone attempting
to do theology faithfully across cultural lines.

## Defining Culture

*Inside Cultures* opens with a definition meant to cut through the
term's loose, everyday usage: culture is "learned, shared human
behaviour and ideas, which can and do change with time." This
compact definition carries three claims doing real theological and
missiological work. First, culture is *learned*, not innate or
biologically fixed — meaning no culture, including one's own, should
be treated as simply "natural" or theologically neutral by default,
a point directly relevant to every contextualization debate surveyed
elsewhere in this module (from the "term question" in Chinese
Bible translation to African Christology's engagement with ancestor
categories). Second, culture is *shared* — it is a social, not merely
individual, phenomenon, meaning theological contextualization is
inescapably a communal task, not simply a matter of individual
missionary creativity. Third, and perhaps most important for
missiological application, culture *changes over time* — meaning no
contextualized theology, however well done, should be treated as a
final, unchangeable achievement; cultures (and the theologies
expressed within them) remain living, dynamic, and subject to further
development.

## Durkheim: Collective Conscience and Two Types of Social Solidarity

The textbook surveys Émile Durkheim's foundational distinction between
*mechanical solidarity* (characteristic of smaller, simpler societies,
where individuals function as near-duplicates of one another, sharing
essentially the same occupations, beliefs, and "collective
conscience") and *organic solidarity* (characteristic of larger,
more complex societies, where people complement rather than duplicate
each other, working together the way different organs serve a single
body). Durkheim illustrated the collective conscience with the
example of a small-town sex scandal, where community members
instinctively converge to discuss and morally process the event
together, producing a "public temper" that belongs to everyone
collectively while belonging to no single individual in particular.

This distinction has direct relevance for missiological strategy:
church-planting and discipleship approaches well suited to
tight-knit, mechanically solidary communities (where a shared
"collective conscience" means the whole community tends to move
together toward or away from a new belief) function quite differently
than approaches suited to organically solidary, highly differentiated
urban or pluralistic societies (where individuals may hold quite
different beliefs while still functioning interdependently) — a
distinction with obvious bearing on the different people-movement and
individual-conversion dynamics documented elsewhere in this
curriculum's treatment of Majority World church growth.

## Structural-Functionalism and Fieldwork Method

The textbook traces how Durkheim's concepts were extended through
fieldwork by later anthropologists — notably Radcliffe-Brown's study
of the Andaman Islanders and, later, Australian Aboriginal kinship
systems, applying Durkheim's organic/mechanical solidarity framework
to specific small-scale societies through direct, sustained
observation. This modeling of theory tested against sustained
firsthand fieldwork — rather than theory developed purely from the
armchair — offers a methodological lesson directly applicable to
theological contextualization: adequate contextual theology, like
adequate cultural anthropology, requires sustained direct engagement
with an actual community's lived practice, not merely secondhand
description or theoretical inference from a distance.

## Missiological Reflection

The anthropological concepts surveyed here function throughout this
module as unstated background assumptions behind explicitly
theological arguments — Paul Hiebert's "excluded middle," repeatedly
invoked across this module's other sources (Mangoes or Bananas,
Majority World Theology's pneumatology section, and elsewhere), is
itself a piece of anthropological theorizing about worldview applied
to theological method. Understanding that culture is learned, shared,
and dynamic rather than natural, individual, and fixed helps explain
why Majority World theologians across this entire module consistently
resist two opposite errors: treating their own indigenous culture as
theologically neutral raw material simply awaiting a Western
theological overlay, and treating Western theological categories as
themselves culturally neutral simply because they arrived first or
carry greater institutional prestige. Both errors rest on a failure to
recognize that *every* theology, Western or otherwise, is inescapably
expressed through some particular, learned, historically situated
culture — the anthropological insight underlying this entire module's
central argument that culturally embedded theological reflection from
Africa, Asia, Latin America, and beyond is not a deviation from proper
theology, but an inescapable feature of how theology has always
actually been done, everywhere, including in the West.
