# Eschatology in the Majority World

## Overview

This file synthesizes Part Six of *Majority World Theology: Christian
Doctrine in Global Context* (ed. Gene L. Green, Stephen T. Pardue, and
K. K. Yeo), on eschatology — the doctrine of last things. The section
moves from a foundational essay on eschatology and apocalyptic
literature through Latin American, Asian, and Taiwanese case studies,
each asking how hope in Christ's return and coming kingdom should
shape the church's posture toward suffering, injustice, and political
life in the present.

## Eschatology and Apocalyptic: A Necessary Pairing

D. Stephen Long opens Part Six by examining a common modern complaint
against apocalyptic literature: that it is otherworldly, fosters
extreme or violent politics, or fuels a political Zionism that
destabilizes the international order — leading some theologians to
try to separate "eschatology" (respectable doctrine about the ultimate
future) from "apocalyptic" (embarrassing, literalistic end-times
speculation). Long argues this division is finally unsustainable:
without apocalyptic literature, there simply would be no eschatology
at all, since the major themes eschatology addresses (the restoration
of Israel, the coming reign of God, the renewal of creation, the
union of heaven and earth, final judgment, and history's ultimate
completion) are themselves apocalyptic in origin and are embedded
directly in the Nicene Creed, recited weekly by Christians worldwide.
The real interpretive task, in Long's framing, is not whether to use
apocalyptic literature but how to interpret it well for constructive
eschatology and, ultimately, political theology.

## Latin America: From Dispensationalism to Theology of Hope

Alberto F. Roldán traces a significant historical shift in Latin
American eschatological thought — from classic dispensationalism
(with its detailed end-times charts and expectation of an imminent
rapture) toward Jürgen Moltmann's theology of hope, tracking this
development through the literature of Church and Society in Latin
America and the Latin American Theological Fraternity. Roldán
illustrates the contrast concretely by comparing classic evangelical
hymnody's eschatological imagery with the newer songs emerging from
contemporary Latin American evangelical worship — a shift in what
believers actually sing revealing a deeper shift in how they
understand what they are hoping for.

Nelson R. Morales Fredes extends this analysis by examining how four
distinct Latin American theological streams — traditional Catholicism,
liberation theology, the Latin American Theological Fraternity, and
dispensationalism — each read Mark 1:14-15 (Jesus' proclamation that
the kingdom of God is at hand) differently, with sharply different
implications for the relationship between God's kingdom and the
church's this-worldly political engagement. Morales Fredes writes out
of personal memory of the tumultuous 1970s and 1980s, when some
believers anticipated an imminent millennial return that could be
"accelerated" through evangelism, while others, confronting pervasive
poverty and systemic injustice, insisted the kingdom of God had to be
established concretely in the present to address oppression directly
— two eschatological postures with radically different practical
consequences for how Christians spend their energy.

## Asia: Cruciform Eschatological Reign

Aldrin Peñamora argues that eschatology plays an outsized role in
shaping how Asian Christians engage — or fail to engage — public and
sociopolitical life, with the historical and cultural intertwining of
*sola Scriptura* commitments with specific national histories (his
examples are Korea and China) often producing an eschatology that
withdraws from public engagement altogether. Citing Moltmann's
claim that Christianity is, "from first to last," eschatology and
hope — inherently forward-looking, revolutionizing, and transforming
of the present — Peñamora argues for recovering Jesus' own *cruciform*
eschatology: an eschatology that, following the pattern of the cross,
directly confronted the oppressions of its own day in light of God's
dawning reign, rather than deferring all confrontation with injustice
to a future, otherworldly kingdom. He frames this recovery as urgent
precisely because poverty, exclusion, and oppression remain such
constant and often deadly companions for so many people across Asia.

## Taiwan: Eschatological Restoration of Israel and the Ta-Tung Ideal

Shirley S. Ho examines a striking recent phenomenon: renewed interest
among Chinese and Taiwanese Christians in the eschatological
restoration of Israel, brought into sharp public focus when the 2017
recognition of Jerusalem as Israel's capital and the transfer of the
US embassy provoked polarized reactions worldwide. Ho documents that
Taiwanese Christian responses ranged from quiet neutrality to open or
clandestine support, with forceful public resistance to the policy
almost entirely absent among Taiwanese Christians — an unusual
pattern worth explaining. Working especially from Isaiah 2:1-5, Ho
constructs an eschatology for the Taiwanese context around the
indigenous Chinese concept of *Ta-Tung* ("Great Unity" or "Great
Harmony," a classical Confucian ideal of universal peace and
commonwealth), arguing this concept offers Taiwanese Christians
resources for a robust eschatology relevant well beyond the specific
question of Israel — a timely task, Ho notes, given the resurgence of
nationalism shaping both ecclesiastical and national politics
globally, not only in East Asia.

## Missiological Reflection

Across Latin America, Asia, and Taiwan, the recurring question is not
merely "what will happen at the end of history" but "how should hope
in that ending shape action now" — whether that means Latin American
believers choosing between accelerating an imminent millennium through
evangelism or confronting present systemic injustice directly; Asian
Christians recovering a cruciform eschatology willing to confront
public oppression rather than defer all reckoning to the age to come;
or Taiwanese Christians drawing on an indigenous vision of universal
harmony to navigate contested questions of national identity and
international politics. Long's opening insistence that eschatology
and apocalyptic cannot be separated proves true across every case
study: each context's eschatology is inescapably bound up with how
believers read their own concrete political and social moment in
light of God's promised future.
