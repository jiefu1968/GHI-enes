# Pressure Points: The Global Church Amid Growth, Pluralism, and Migration

## Overview

This file synthesizes selected chapters of J. D. Payne's *Pressure
Points*, which identifies twelve critical issues facing the church in
the first half of the twenty-first century, framed around the
metaphor of pressure — a force that can either break something apart
or, applied rightly, produce something as valuable as a diamond. This
file focuses on the chapters most directly relevant to Majority World
theology and missiology: the shift of Christianity's center of gravity
toward the Majority World, the West's own transformation into a
mission field, religious pluralism, and international migration.

## Unreached People Groups: The Bread of Life Not Yet Available

Payne opens with the missiological concept of "people groups" rather
than nation-states as the proper unit for measuring gospel access —
noting that a single country can contain hundreds of distinct
linguistic, ethnic, and social groups, some reached with the gospel
and some entirely unengaged. He surveys ongoing debates within
missiology over how "unreached" and "unengaged" should be defined and
measured (drawing on data sources like the World Christian Database
and the Joshua Project), and specifically why missiologists count
evangelical presence rather than nominal Christian presence as the
relevant threshold — since a country with a long Christian history can
still contain unreached peoples if evangelical gospel witness has
never taken root among them. He closes by noting that many previously
distant unreached peoples are now literally "strangers next door" in
Western cities, due to global migration patterns — a theme picked up
at length in a later chapter.

## The West as a Mission Field

Payne traces a historical arc from William Carey (conventionally
marking the start of the Modern Protestant Missionary Movement in
1792) to Lesslie Newbigin, whose decades of missionary experience in
India gave him unusual clarity, on returning to the West, to see his
own home context as a genuine "post-Christian mission field" rather
than a settled Christian society merely sending missionaries
elsewhere. Payne documents the scale of Western secularization with
hard data — the rise of religiously unaffiliated "nones," entire US
states and metro areas falling under 5 percent evangelical, and
comparable patterns elsewhere in the historically Christian West — and
argues that missiology itself needs to shift registers accordingly:
early-stage "apostolic missiology," suited to pioneering gospel
access where it does not yet exist, is not the same discipline as the
"pastoral missiology" appropriate to nurturing and maturing an
already-established, growing church. Payne's practical concern is that
Western church leaders often default to pastoral methods appropriate
to a Christianized society that, in much of the contemporary West, no
longer actually exists.

## Growth of the Majority World Church

Payne surveys the dramatic numerical growth of Christianity outside
the West — noting, using mission-sending statistics, that several
Majority World countries now send out more missionaries than
traditional Western sending nations — and cautions against what he
calls "forgetting about the mustard seed": Western observers can
easily lose sight of how many Majority World churches began from
almost nothing within living memory. Payne's practical missiological
proposal is a shift from a paradigm of Western "abandonment" (handing
off ministry entirely to national churches and withdrawing) to one of
genuine, ongoing partnership, arguing that Western churches have as
much to learn from their Majority World brothers and sisters as they
have historically assumed they had to teach them.

## Pluralism and the Plurality of Faiths

Payne distinguishes two related but distinct pressures: *pluralism*,
an internal philosophical and theological pressure (the increasingly
common assumption that no single religious claim can be exclusively
true), and the *plurality of faiths*, an external, demographic
pressure (the simple fact of growing religious diversity in
previously more homogeneous Western societies). He surveys global
religious demographic data, with particular attention to the growth
of Islam — including projected Muslim population growth by country
and region through 2030, and estimates of the small but real number
of Muslim-background believers in Christ worldwide — alongside the
rapid rise of religiously unaffiliated "nones." Payne's central
argument is that the pressure of religious plurality makes serious,
theologically informed contextualization not an optional missiological
refinement but an urgent practical necessity for any church that
wants to communicate the gospel intelligibly across these growing
lines of religious difference.

## International Migration: The Divine Maestro

Drawing on Acts 17:26 ("[God] made from one man every nation of
mankind to live on all the face of the earth… having determined
their appointed times, and the boundaries of their habitation"), Payne
frames the entire sweep of human migration history as, in some sense,
conducted by a "Divine Maestro" — arguing that Christians should read
contemporary mass migration (economic migrants, refugees, international
students, and others) not merely as a humanitarian or political
challenge but as a providentially significant missiological
opportunity, since unprecedented numbers of people from unreached or
unengaged people groups are, for the first time, living within easy
reach of existing Western churches, without those churches needing to
send anyone across an ocean to encounter them.

## Missiological Reflection

Payne's twelve "pressure points" (only some of which are covered in
this file; others address globalization, poverty, urban growth,
children and youth, health care, oral learners, and sexual
immorality) collectively make the case that the pressures facing the
contemporary global church are deeply interconnected — the growth of
the Majority World church, the West's own transformation into a
mission field, rising religious pluralism, and mass international
migration are not separate issues to be addressed in isolation but a
single, converging missiological moment. Payne's closing image — a
flywheel that gains momentum precisely because pressure is applied to
it, rather than being destroyed by that pressure — captures his
overall argument: rightly discerned and prayerfully engaged, none of
these pressures need push the church toward irrelevance; each can, in
fact, become an occasion for renewed kingdom advance.
