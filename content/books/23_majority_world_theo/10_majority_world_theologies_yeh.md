# Majority World Theologies: An Arboretum of Local Theologizing

## Overview

This file synthesizes the introduction and framing chapters of Allan
Yeh's edited volume *Majority World Theologies: Theologizing from
Africa, Asia, Latin America, and the Ends of the Earth*, produced
under the auspices of the Evangelical Missiological Society. The
volume offers side-by-side case studies of local theologizing from
around the globe, deliberately including regions (the Middle East,
Indigenous North America) beyond the conventional "big three"
non-Western continents.

## The Missiologist's Impossible Task

Yeh opens by naming the structural bind every missiologist faces:
expected to be an interdisciplinary expert on the entire world —
spanning theology, history, anthropology, linguistics, and
strategy — while realistically no single scholar, however
credentialed and field-experienced, can achieve this. Rather than
resolving this tension, Yeh's volume embraces it by explicit design,
offering not a single totalizing theory of majority world theology but
a curated set of specific "snapshots."

## The Arboretum Metaphor

Yeh's governing image for the volume is an arboretum rather than a
forest: instead of overwhelming the visitor with a single, undifferentiated
mass of trees, an arboretum plants a California redwood, a cedar of
Lebanon, a Japanese bonsai, an African baobab, an Australian
eucalyptus, and an Amazonian cacao tree side by side, allowing close
inspection and comparison while making the differences and diversity
immediately visible. Yeh is explicit about the danger built into this
model — that a single representative example from a continent can
easily be mistaken for a stereotype of that continent's theology as a
whole — but insists that, held with appropriate caution, the model
lets readers grasp both the underlying unity (each specimen is
recognizably a tree, just as each contribution is recognizably
Christian theology) and the genuine diversity of the specimens
(no two trees, and no two contextual theologies, are identical).

Yeh's own illustration of the interpretive danger to avoid is
memorable: a tiger telling a lion "you are not a cat because you do
not have orange fur and black stripes" is structurally identical to a
Western Christian telling a non-Western Christian "you are not
Christian because you do not define Jesus using words like
*hypostatic union*, *imputed righteousness*, *eternally begotten*,
*homoousios*, and *filioque*" — mistaking one particular, historically
contingent form of theological expression for the necessary and only
legitimate form Christian doctrine can take.

## An Evangelical Epistemology for Majority World Theology

Yeh is explicit that the volume operates from a self-consciously
evangelical epistemology, growing out of the Evangelical Missiological
Society, and defends this choice on the grounds that shared
evangelical commitments — the authority of Scripture, the centrality
of the cross, and the impetus toward mission and conversion — function
as a more genuine common denominator across the global church than
appeals to tradition, reason, ecclesial office, or contextual
situation alone. At the same time, Yeh frames this as "a different,
more robust, type of evangelicalism" — one that incorporates the
classic Bebbington quadrilateral (conversionism, activism, biblicism,
crucicentrism) while also adding cultural perspective as a secondary,
supporting factor rather than treating it as something that "taints"
theology, as some within more traditionalist wings of evangelicalism
have been prone to assume. For Yeh, the primacy of Scripture and the
Trinity as the two load-bearing foundations of Christian doctrine does
not require devaluing everything else that different cultures bring to
theological reflection — culture and doctrinal fidelity are not, in
his framing, competitors.

## Regional Case Studies as Pedagogy, Not Just Content

The volume's African case study chapter is distinctive in that it
doubles as a description of an actual theological education course —
run in collaboration with African institutions and churches (AIU,
Nairobi Chapel, Mavuno Church) and structured around site visits from
cosmopolitan city centers to informal settlements — designed not
merely to transmit information about African theology but to immerse
students experientially in a new context of Christianity, integrating
theological reflection with hands-on ministry praxis as a foundational
skill for theological formation itself. This pedagogical framing
underscores a point implicit throughout the volume: majority world
theology is not simply a body of content to be studied from a
distance, but a way of doing theology that requires direct, embodied
engagement with a particular context to be learned at all.

## Beyond the Big Three: The Middle East and Indigenous North America

Yeh's volume deliberately extends beyond Africa, Asia, and Latin
America — the three regions most commonly invoked in discussions of
majority world or Two-Thirds World Christianity — to include local
theologizing from the Middle East and from Indigenous North America
specifically. The rationale is straightforward: as the center of
gravity of world Christianity has shifted, younger churches across
the world are reaching theological maturity and gaining the capacity
to theologize for themselves rather than simply reproducing what they
were taught by Western missionaries — and this capacity for
self-theologizing is not limited to the three most commonly discussed
regions, but extends wherever a genuinely local church has matured
enough to reflect on the timeless truths of the transcendent God in
its own language, culture, and context.

## Missiological Reflection

Yeh's arboretum metaphor and his explicit warning against
mistaking any single contextual specimen for the whole of a
continent's theology are directly relevant to how this curriculum
itself should be used: African, Asian, Latin American, Middle Eastern,
and Indigenous theological voices synthesized throughout this module
should be read as specific, situated specimens — richly informative
about their own particular context — rather than as fully
representative of "African theology" or "Asian theology" as
monolithic, singular things. The volume's broader argument, that
genuine theological maturity in the Majority World means the capacity
to theologize independently rather than merely to receive and
reproduce theology handed down from the West, is the same maturity
this entire module aims to document and take seriously.
