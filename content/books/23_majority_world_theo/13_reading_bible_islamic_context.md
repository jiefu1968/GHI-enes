# Reading the Bible in Islamic Context: Comparative Hermeneutics as Theological Method

## Overview

This file synthesizes *Reading the Bible in Islamic Context: Qur'anic
Conversations* (ed. Ida Glaser, Shirin Shafaie, and others,
Routledge), an academic volume emerging from a 2015 Oxford conference
on "biblical interpretation in Islamic context." Unlike apologetic
literature aimed at winning arguments with Muslims, this volume treats
the Qur'an's relationship to the Bible as a genuine, open hermeneutical
question — bringing together Christian, Muslim, and secular scholars
to read specific biblical and Qur'anic texts side by side and observe
what interpretive patterns emerge.

## The Project's Method: "Studiously and Stubbornly Particular" Experiments

Ida Glaser's introductory chapter explains the project's governing
methodological commitment, drawn from comparative theologian Francis
X. Clooney: reading a Christian text in the context of another
faith's texts should proceed through "studiously and stubbornly
particular" experiments rather than starting from a general
methodology imposed on the material in advance. In practice, this
means the volume's editorial team did not ask contributors to apply a
single predetermined comparative method to the Bible and Qur'an;
instead, contributors tried out different ways of reading specific
paired texts in their own contexts, with patterns allowed to emerge
inductively from observing the whole range of resulting experiments
rather than being dictated from the outset.

Glaser also poses a foundational conceptual question the volume
returns to repeatedly: what does "Islamic context" even mean, given
the enormous internal variety of Muslim belief and practice worldwide?
She suggests that speaking of a single "Islamic context" may be less
accurate than speaking of a variety of "Islams" — but identifies one
feature nearly universal across that variety: the central importance
of the Qur'an itself, and specifically the Qur'an's own complex
relationship to the Bible, as the truly distinctive challenge and
opportunity facing any biblical interpreter working in a Muslim-majority
or Muslim-influenced setting. An important corollary Glaser draws out:
the hermeneutical task is not only how to read the Bible in this
context, but also how to read the Qur'an — since serious comparative
work requires taking the Qur'an's own internal logic and interpretive
tradition seriously on its own terms, not merely mining it for
material useful to a predetermined Christian argument.

## Three Organizing Tasks

The volume organizes its seventeen chapters (following Glaser's
introduction) into three broad tasks, which structure the book's three
main parts:

**Intertextual conversations** (Part I) pair specific biblical and
Qur'anic narratives for close comparative reading — Abraham as read
through Christian-Muslim conversation in Turkey (George Bristow), the
Joseph narratives read across both traditions (Shirin Shafaie), the
"sin" of David examined in light of Islamic thought (Ali Makhlabi and
Larry Ciccarelli), the story of David, Bathsheba, and the parable of
the ewe lamb read alongside its Qur'anic counterpart in Sura 38
(Carol M. Walker), and the golden calf narrative as it appears,
intertwined, across both scriptural traditions (Mohammad Ghandehari
and Mohsen Feyzbakhsh).

**Questions about texts** (Part II) turns to more strictly text-critical
and reception-historical questions: the use of *Isra'iliyyat*
(Jewish- and Christian-derived material) within Muslim scholarship as
a kind of "fourth source" behind Qur'anic exegesis (Wan Mohd Fazrul
Azdi Wan Razali, Ahmad Yunus Mohd Noor, and Jaffary Awang), the
constraints scriptural polemics have historically placed on Muslim
exegetes such as Hamiduddin Farahi in his treatment of the Akedah
(Nazirudin Mohd Nasir), the "culture shock" many readers experience
encountering the Bible for the first time from within an Islamic
cultural frame (Daniel J. Crowther), and the Islamic reception history
of the Bible more broadly (Martin O'Kane and Talha Bhamji).

**Analogical explorations** (Part III) takes up thematic and
structural comparisons less tied to a single shared narrative: the
place of ritual purity within faith (Dwight Swanson), the biblical
Ruth read alongside the qur'anic Queen of Sheba as parallel scriptural
narratives of a foreigner's assent to true faith (Georgina L. Jardim),
Paul's argument about idolatry in Romans 1:18-32 read alongside the
Qur'an's own theology of divine "signs" (Michael Lodahl), the shared
use of indirection as a rhetorical strategy across biblical and
qur'anic discourse, with implications for Bible translation in
Islamic contexts (Andy Warren-Rothlin), and John's Gospel considered
specifically as a potential structural bridge for Muslim-Christian
mutual understanding (Daniel A. Madigan, SJ).

## A Muslim Scholar's Concluding Reflection

The volume closes with a chapter by Shabbir Akhtar, a Muslim scholar
affiliated with the Centre for Muslim-Christian Studies at Oxford,
outlining three distinct methods available to a Muslim reader
approaching the Bible — a deliberate editorial choice to close the
volume not with a Christian summary of what has been learned about
reading the Qur'an, but with a Muslim scholar's own reflection on how
a Muslim reader might, in turn, approach the Bible on comparably
serious terms. This structural choice embodies the volume's larger
commitment to genuine two-way conversation rather than one-directional
Christian analysis of Islamic material.

## Missiological Reflection

This volume models a mode of biblical and qur'anic engagement distinct
from — though not necessarily in tension with — the more directly
evangelistic and apologetic material found elsewhere in this
curriculum's treatment of Christian-Muslim engagement (see Module 15).
Where apologetic literature typically argues *for* a particular
Christian reading of contested Qur'anic material, this volume's
contributors more often simply place biblical and qur'anic texts
alongside each other and observe, with scholarly patience, what
emerges — a method valuable precisely because it takes the Qur'an's
own internal coherence and interpretive tradition seriously rather
than treating it only as a target for refutation. For Christian
workers in Muslim-majority or Muslim-influenced contexts, the specific
paired readings this volume develops (Abraham, Joseph, David, the
golden calf, Ruth and the Queen of Sheba) offer concrete, textually
grounded starting points for exactly the kind of respectful,
substantive scriptural conversation this curriculum's other modules
on Muslim evangelism repeatedly recommend.
