# Pneumatology in the Majority World

## Overview

This file synthesizes Part Three of *Majority World Theology:
Christian Doctrine in Global Context* (ed. Gene L. Green, Stephen T.
Pardue, and K. K. Yeo), addressing the doctrine of the Holy Spirit
across Indian, Chinese, African, Latin American, and Indigenous
American Christian thought. The editors note that pneumatology has
long been considered the "shy" or relatively neglected member of the
Trinity in classical Western systematic theology — a neglect the
Majority World church, marked by explosive Pentecostal and charismatic
growth, is actively correcting.

## Amos Yong: A Renaissance in Pneumatology

Amos Yong frames the whole section by tracing the diversity of
pneumatological thinking across the Eastern Orthodox tradition, a
century of Majority World theology, and modern Pentecostal-charismatic
movements, arguing that this global pluralism can enrich — rather than
threaten — contemporary reflection on the Nicene Creed's third
article ("I believe in the Holy Spirit"), provided that enrichment
remains disciplined by historic Christian commitments rather than
detached from them.

## India: Does Advaitic Hinduism Undermine the Spirit's Personhood?

Ivan Satyavrata offers a pointed critical argument: the pervasive
influence of *advaitic* (non-dualist) Hinduism on Indian Christian
thought has, in his assessment, significantly undermined — and in some
formulations effectively denied — the personhood of the Holy Spirit,
reducing the Spirit to an impersonal force or principle more
compatible with non-dualist metaphysics than with the New Testament's
personal, relational Spirit. Satyavrata points instead to the more
personalist *bhakti* (devotional) strand of Hinduism as offering
considerably more promising resources for constructive, biblically
faithful contextualization of pneumatology in the Indian setting —
illustrating that "Hindu context" is not monolithic, and that which
strand of a host religious tradition a theologian engages can produce
sharply different theological outcomes.

## China: Pauline Pneumatology and the Chinese Rites Controversy

Wei Hua examines Pauline pneumatology in dialogue with the historic
"Chinese Rites" controversy — the centuries-long dispute within
Roman Catholic missionary strategy over whether traditional Chinese
ancestor veneration and Confucian ritual practice could be tolerated
as compatible cultural forms or had to be rejected as incompatible
with Christian worship. Reading this history through a pneumatological
lens allows Hua to reframe the rites controversy not merely as a
historical missiological dispute but as an ongoing test case for how
the Spirit's work relates to inherited cultural ritual — a question
still very much alive for Chinese Christians navigating ancestor
veneration today.

## Africa: From Function to Person

David Tonghou Ngong traces three broad phases of African Christian
reflection on the Spirit — early Christianity in Roman North Africa,
Egypt, Nubia, and Ethiopia; precolonial African Christianity dominated
by Roman Catholicism in regions like present-day Nigeria, Angola, and
the Congo; and colonial/postcolonial Christianity — arguing that
contemporary African theology of the Spirit has emphasized what the
Spirit *does* (empowering healing, deliverance, prophecy, and other
practical functions) far more than reflecting on who the Spirit *is*
within the life of the Trinity itself. Ngong's constructive proposal
is that this heavily functional understanding needs recalibrating: the
Spirit's *place* within trinitarian life deserves the same sustained
theological attention African Christianity has already given the
Spirit's practical operations.

## The Americas: Indigenous Pneumatologies Before and After 1492

Oscar García-Johnson poses a provocative historical-theological
question: what should Christians make of God's apparent encounters
with the cultures and religious traditions of the Americas *before*
the European conquest — encounters that occurred, by definition,
outside any Western or "occidentalized" socioreligious framework?
Drawing on Robert Johnston's work on "God's wider presence,"
García-Johnson argues for uncovering pneumatological continuity
between pre-Columbian ancestral religious traditions and
post-Columbian indigenous Christian communities, deliberately working
to "disarticulate" over-Westernized theological categories in order
to open space for a genuinely contextual pneumatology rooted in the
Americas' own indigenous history rather than imported wholesale from
European or North American sources.

## Latin America: The Spirit as Power for Life and Hope

C. René Padilla observes that until fairly recently, evangelical
systematic theologians gave the Holy Spirit little sustained
attention — leaving an "implicit theology" present mainly within
Pentecostalism, itself heavily shaped by North American
fundamentalism. Padilla traces a significant shift over recent
decades as new voices (predominantly, though not exclusively,
Pentecostal) began exploring pneumatology in real theological depth.
The distinctive focus of Latin American pneumatology, in Padilla's
account, centers the Holy Spirit as the source of power for practical
Christian life and mission *and* as the source of hope specifically in
contexts of poverty and oppression — a focus he traces back at least
to the 1984 conference of the International Fellowship of Mission
Theologians in Tlayacapan, Mexico, where thirty-seven Majority World
theologians identified "life in the Spirit" as a subject central to
the church's mission worldwide.

## Missiological Reflection

Read together, these essays show pneumatology functioning as a genuine
site of theological growth rather than merely a settled inheritance
being applied locally. Each context brings a different challenge to
the doctrine of the Spirit — a competing metaphysic that can hollow
out the Spirit's personhood (India), an inherited ritual controversy
that never fully resolved how the Spirit relates to ancestral practice
(China), a functional emphasis in need of deeper trinitarian grounding
(Africa), a search for pre-Christian continuity honestly reckoned with
rather than erased (the Americas), and a socioeconomic context that
demands the Spirit be understood as source of both practical power and
enduring hope amid poverty (Latin America). None of these
contributions treats pneumatology as a finished doctrine merely
awaiting local application; each treats their own context as
genuinely capable of deepening the whole church's understanding of
who the Spirit is.
