# Globalizing Theology: Method, Hermeneutics, and the Local-Global Tension

## Overview

This file synthesizes *Globalizing Theology: Belief and Practice in an
Era of World Christianity* (ed. Craig Ott and Harold A. Netland, Baker
Academic), a volume of fourteen essays written in honor of missiologist
and anthropologist Paul G. Hiebert, organized around the challenge of
doing theology faithfully when the gospel is inescapably communicated
through particular human languages, cultures, and historical contexts
— and when Christianity's center of gravity has shifted decisively
toward the non-Western world.

## Part One: World Christianity and Theological Reflection

Tite Tiénou opens the volume with a direct challenge to the Western
theological academy: the provincialism of Western theology can only
be overcome once Western institutions intentionally address the
systemic assumptions — what he calls "the hegemony postulate" — that
quietly govern which theological voices count as normative academic
theology and which are treated as merely local or contextual by
comparison. Darrell Whiteman follows with an anthropological
argument for why theology needs multiple disciplines working
together, tracing how anthropology and theology have been brought
into fruitful dialogue throughout Paul Hiebert's own body of work.
Historian Andrew Walls closes the opening section by reminding readers
that boundary-crossing has characterized Christianity from its very
inception — world Christianity today is simply the latest phase of a
process as old as the faith itself — and calls for a study of Christian
history less provincially confined to the Western story alone.

## Part Two: Methodological Issues for Globalizing Theology

Kevin Vanhoozer proposes understanding theology as "theodrama" — the
words and acts of the Triune God — governed by two disciplining
principles held together: the "canonical principle" (theology must
remain properly rooted in Scripture) and the "catholic principle" (the
church's worldwide ethnic and social diversity is "the condition for
an enriched and enlarged theological understanding," not a threat to
right doctrine). His often-quoted formulation is that faithful global
theologizing means Christians "speaking and doing the same truth
differently" across cultures — a phrase that captures the volume's
central balancing act between universal truth and local particularity.

David Strong and Cynthia Strong turn to the Jerusalem Council (Acts
15) as a biblical precedent for exactly this balancing act, reading it
as a model "hermeneutical community" from which contemporary
principles for relating local concerns to universal gospel truth can
be derived. Steve Strauss extends the question of normativity into
church history directly, using a case study of Christology within the
Ethiopian Coptic Church — and its historic rejection of the Chalcedonian
formula — to press the genuinely difficult question of whether all
Christians, in every culture and era, must adopt the specific
conceptual language of Nicaea and Chalcedon, or whether the church's
universal confession can survive being expressed in different
conceptual frameworks.

Charles Van Engen introduces the concept of "glocalization" (developed
by sociologist Roland Robertson) to describe the necessary,
simultaneous involvement of local congregations in God's mission both
locally and globally — churches understanding themselves and their
ministry as inherently "glocal" rather than choosing between local
faithfulness and global vision. Robert Priest closes Part Two by
arguing that theology requires not only a proper doctrine of God but
an *accurate* understanding of actual human selves — drawing on his
own ethnographic fieldwork among the Aguaruna people of Peru to model
what he calls "experience-near" theologizing: unpacking biblical
themes (his example is sin) using concepts genuinely close to how a
given people actually experience and articulate their own lived
reality, rather than concepts borrowed wholesale from Western
theological vocabulary and simply translated.

## Part Three: Implications of Globalizing Theology

M. Daniel Carroll R., an Old Testament scholar who has lived much of
his life in Guatemala, argues that globalization's economic dimension
specifically requires what he calls "a hermeneutics of responsibility"
— insisting that the "hegemonic capitalism" shaping so much of
contemporary global life must itself be processed theologically, even
as Majority World and Western theologies alike must be examined for
their own potential ideological captivity to the economic systems
they inhabit. Vinoth Ramachandra explores how national and religious
identities (Western, Eastern, British, Indian, Hindu, Christian,
Muslim) were themselves forged through the historical confluence of
many factors across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, noting
soberly that Christian witness in India was historically compromised
by real and perceived linkages to British colonialism — and warning
that Christianity today risks being perceived, in a new but structurally
similar way, as a partner of American political, economic, and
cultural dominance, especially as this plays out in the Middle East.
Eloise Hiebert Meneses picks up directly where Ramachandra leaves off,
issuing what she frames as a prophetic challenge to American
Christians specifically — living, in her metaphor, in "the Rome of our
time" — to resist Christian nationalism by consciously choosing Jesus
over Caesar.

James E. Plueddemann examines the genuinely new missiological reality
that missionaries today increasingly come "from everywhere to
everywhere" rather than flowing in a single historic direction from
West to the rest, with direct implications for how mission agencies
and theological education institutions must restructure themselves.
Lois McKinney Douglas draws on a half-century of her own experience in
theological education across multiple cultures to reflect on the
opportunities and challenges this shift poses for global theological
training specifically. Paul Hiebert himself — in whose honor the whole
volume was written — closes the substantive essays by proposing that
missionaries in the twenty-first century's more fluid, boundary-crossing
world can serve a distinctive function as *mediators*: "inbetweeners"
standing between different worlds, building bridges of understanding
and negotiating ministry partnerships, including specifically
mediating between those doing primarily local theology and those more
concerned with theology's universal, cross-cultural dimension. Editor
Craig Ott closes the volume by summarizing its major themes and
identifying what remains undone in the ongoing task of globalizing
theology.

## Missiological Reflection

*Globalizing Theology* functions as a sustained argument that the
tension between local particularity and universal truth in theology is
not a problem to be resolved once and set aside, but a permanent,
productive feature of theology as such — since the gospel has never
been communicated except through some particular human language and
context, from the very first Christian generation onward. The volume's
recurring image of theology "speaking the same truth differently," its
biblical grounding in the Jerusalem Council's own handling of exactly
this tension, and its closing vision of missionaries and theological
educators as bridge-building mediators together offer this
curriculum's broader project — synthesizing majority-world theological
reflection across many modules — a clear methodological rationale:
the point is neither to relativize Christian doctrine into
incoherence nor to flatten global theological diversity back into a
single Western template, but to let genuinely diverse, locally rooted
theological voices deepen the whole church's shared grasp of a truth
that remains, in its substance, one.
