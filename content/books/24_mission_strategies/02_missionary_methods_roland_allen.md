# Missionary Methods: St. Paul's or Ours? (based on Roland Allen's 1912 classic)

## Overview

Written in 1912, this classic missiology text remains one of the most
influential critiques of Western missionary method ever produced. Allen's
core argument is that the apostle Paul planted genuinely indigenous,
self-governing, self-supporting, and self-propagating churches with
astonishing speed across the Roman world, while the missionary methods
of Allen's own era (heavily dependent on foreign funding, foreign
clergy, and prolonged foreign oversight) produced churches that
remained dependent indefinitely. The book systematically compares
Paul's actual practice, drawn from Acts and the epistles, against
early twentieth-century mission methodology, and finds the contrast
stark enough to warrant a fundamental rethink.

## Part I: Paul's Strategic Method

Allen observes that Paul concentrated his church-planting efforts in
strategic urban centers — provincial capitals and major trade
crossroads — trusting that the gospel would spread outward from these
hubs through the new believers' own social and trade networks, rather
than Paul personally visiting every town himself. Paul's converts were
drawn from a genuine cross-section of society, not exclusively the
poor or exclusively the elite, and Paul made no attempt to first
"civilize" or educate converts to a particular cultural standard of
respectability before receiving them into the church, trusting instead
that the moral and social transformation of new believers would emerge
from the Spirit's work and the church's own internal life rather than
requiring extensive prior tutelage from the missionary.

## Part II: Miracles, Finance, and Paul's Message

Allen argues that Paul did not rely on a continuous, personally
performed program of miracles to authenticate his ministry the way
some contemporary mission strategies assumed necessary, and that
miraculous gifts, where present, operated within the local church body
itself rather than being a foreign missionary's exclusive possession.
On finance, Allen is especially pointed: Paul refused to create
long-term financial dependency, generally supporting himself through
his trade rather than drawing an ongoing salary from the churches he
planted, and did not leave behind paid local clergy dependent on
continued foreign subsidy. Paul's actual preaching content, Allen
argues, was simpler and more centrally Christ-focused than the often
elaborate doctrinal and cultural instruction Western missions assumed
new converts needed before baptism.

## Part III: Teaching and Training Leaders

Paul's teaching method, in Allen's reading, trusted new believers with
substantial responsibility very quickly — appointing local elders from
among recent converts within a matter of months rather than requiring
years of foreign-supervised training before indigenous leadership was
considered safe. Allen contrasts this with his era's typical mission
practice of retaining foreign missionaries in positions of authority
over local congregations for decades, training candidates for ministry
through prolonged, often foreign-language-based education before
entrusting them with real leadership responsibility.

## Part IV: Authority, Discipline, and Unity

Paul, according to Allen, exercised a light apostolic hand once a
church was planted — intervening decisively on core doctrinal and
moral issues (as in his letters to Corinth and Galatia) while otherwise
leaving day-to-day governance, discipline, and decision-making to the
local church itself rather than retaining ongoing personal control.
Unity among the diverse, often culturally distant churches Paul planted
was maintained through shared doctrine, mutual correspondence, and
relational connection rather than through centralized foreign
administrative control.

## Part V: Principles and Contemporary Application

Allen closes by distilling operating principles from Paul's example —
trust indigenous believers with real responsibility early, avoid
creating long-term financial dependency, keep the initial gospel
message centrally Christ-focused rather than overloaded with
supplementary cultural or doctrinal requirements, and resist the
temptation to retain permanent foreign control over young churches —
and applies these principles as a direct challenge to the mission
methods of his own day, which he saw as systematically preventing the
same speed and durability of church multiplication Paul achieved.

## Enduring Influence

This book's central concept — indigenous churches that are
self-governing, self-supporting, and self-propagating (often called the
"Three-Self" principle, developed further by later missiologists such
as Henry Venn and Rufus Anderson before Allen, and popularized further
after him) — remains foundational to contemporary church-planting
strategy, cited across a wide range of later missiological work
addressing dependency, indigenous leadership, and rapid multiplication.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

Allen's critique remains directly applicable to contemporary patterns
of missionary dependency: foreign-funded pastors' salaries, foreign-run
seminaries as the only accepted path to local leadership, and
prolonged foreign oversight of young churches all reproduce the exact
dynamic Allen identified as un-Pauline over a century ago. His example
of appointing local elders quickly, trusting the Spirit's work in new
believers rather than requiring extensive foreign supervision first,
offers a direct historical precedent for contemporary movements
emphasizing rapid indigenous leadership development (such as T4T and
Discovery Bible Study approaches) over slower, foreign-dependent
models.

## Discussion prompts for training

- Where in your own ministry context do you see foreign financial or
  leadership dependency that Allen's reading of Paul's method would
  challenge directly?
- How quickly did Paul entrust new believers with real leadership
  responsibility, and what specifically made that level of trust
  possible in his method?
- What would it look like, practically, to plant a church in a way
  that is genuinely self-governing, self-supporting, and
  self-propagating from its very first months, rather than gradually
  transitioning toward independence over many years?
