# Beyond Literate Western Models: Contextualizing Theological Education in Oral Contexts

## Overview

This framework, drawn from a multi-author consultation on theological
education and orality, challenges the near-universal export of a
literate, Western academic model of theological training to contexts
where the majority of learners are primarily oral rather than
literate. Its central claim is that Western theological education's
emphasis on progressively mastering written material through
individual study has produced real strengths but has also
systematically underserved — and sometimes actively excluded — the
majority of the world's Christians, who learn best through story,
process, and communal experience rather than written text.

## Two Contrasting Educational Orientations

Western theological education has typically emphasized intellectual
formation, covering extensive written content and progressively
mastering difficult subjects, often measured by degree completion — an
approach that has tended to produce a trained clergy elite while
underserving ordinary believers' priesthood. Majority World education,
by contrast, more often emphasizes whole-person formation, depth over
breadth, process over product, and — since a majority of the world's
population are oral rather than literate learners (roughly 60 percent
oral to 40 percent literate globally, the reverse of typical Western
populations) — moves from story to practice to guideline to principle,
essentially the reverse sequence of the typical Western approach, which
moves from principle to guideline to practice to story.

## The Rise of Non-Formal, Story-Based Training

The biblical storytelling movement has demonstrated significant
effectiveness in non-Western contexts, spawning non-formal training
approaches across a wide range of global contexts. This has pushed even
traditionally formal, accredited institutions to take oral, story-based
approaches seriously — incorporating andragogical (adult-learning)
principles suited to oral learners into curriculum design and, in some
cases, degree-granting structures, rather than treating oral training
as merely a lesser, informal alternative to "real" theological
education.

## Affirming Oral Learners and Leaders

A recurring theme is the need to actively affirm and empower oral
preference learners and leaders rather than treating literacy as a
prerequisite for legitimate spiritual authority or theological
competence. Effective leaders have emerged and been trained through
oral means throughout church history and across the contemporary
Majority World Church; treating written theological education as the
only legitimate pathway to leadership risks excluding gifted, godly
leaders whose learning style simply differs from the Western academic
norm, and can inadvertently communicate that oral cultures' own ways of
knowing are theologically second-class.

## Institutional Case Studies in Applying Orality

Multiple institutions illustrate practical application of these
principles: theological schools have restructured curricula and
training approaches specifically around oral Bible storytelling,
integrating it with systematic theology and biblical studies rather
than treating storytelling as a simplified add-on for those who cannot
handle "real" theological content. These case studies address concrete
practical questions — how a seminary should prepare students headed
into the field to train local storytellers effectively, how oral
biblical narrative form and content relate to systematic theological
categories, and how institutions can measure and support the impact of
orality-based training programs on the ground.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

Missionaries training indigenous leaders in oral-preference cultures
face a direct practical choice: import a Western, literate-model
curriculum requiring extensive reading and writing (which will
systematically exclude gifted oral leaders and slow multiplication), or
build training around story, process, and communal learning suited to
how their trainees actually learn best. Given that a majority of the
global population, and a large share of unreached peoples specifically,
are oral-preference learners, orality-based theological training is not
a simplified compromise but often the more theologically and
practically appropriate primary strategy — not merely a supplement to
literate training for those who "can't handle" written material.

## Discussion prompts for training

- How would restructuring your own discipleship or leadership training
  to move from story to practice to guideline to principle (rather
  than the reverse) change what you currently teach first?
- What would it look like in your context to actively affirm an
  oral-preference leader's theological competence as equal to, not
  lesser than, a literate leader's?
- Which of your current training materials assume literacy as a
  prerequisite, and how could they be adapted for oral transmission
  without losing theological substance?
