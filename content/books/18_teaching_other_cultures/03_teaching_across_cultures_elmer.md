# Teaching Across Cultures: Contextualizing Education for Global Mission (based on Duane Elmer's framework)

## Overview

This framework addresses a genuinely global reality: teachers now
travel in every direction — a Guatemalan teaching in India, a Korean in
Zambia, an American in China — usually qualified only by academic
mastery of their subject matter in their home country, with little
attention paid to whether they understand the culture they are now
teaching into. The central argument is that effective cross-cultural
teaching requires three competencies together: mastering the content
to be taught, genuinely appreciating the host learners' cultural
values and context, and — the hardest and most often neglected skill —
actively building connections between that content and the learners'
actual context, rather than assuming content transfer alone produces
transformed lives.

## Theological Assumptions About Education

Christian education, properly understood, is far broader than formal
schooling — it includes any teaching that builds on God's truth and
fosters a learner's development into all God intends them to become.
Because "all truth is God's truth wherever it is found," academic
subjects as different as trigonometry, athletic rules, or biblical
themes all carry potential to nourish integrated human development,
provided teaching connects that content to the learner's actual life.
Three pitfalls threaten teaching that claims to be Christian: ignoring
God's truth altogether, disregarding the learner's whole-person
development in favor of content coverage alone, and failing to connect
truth with life — teaching that transfers information without ever
producing transformed living. Human development itself is explicitly
modeled as holistic in Scripture — Jesus grew "in wisdom and in
stature and in favor with God and all the people" (Luke 2:52), and
Samuel similarly grew "in stature and in favor with the Lord and with
people" even in a spiritually dark era — meaning mental, physical,
spiritual, and social development belong together, not in separate,
unconnected tracks.

## Cultural Assumptions Shaping How Teaching Is Received

Beyond theological grounding, teachers must recognize that their own
assumptions about how learning happens, how authority functions in a
classroom, and how knowledge should be evaluated are themselves
cultural, not universal or neutral. A teaching method that communicates
respect, engagement, and competence in one culture may communicate the
opposite in another — a Western preference for open debate and student
questioning, for instance, may register in some contexts as
disrespectful challenge to a teacher's authority rather than engaged
learning, while a highly directive lecture style celebrated as
authoritative in one culture may register as disengaged or
condescending in another that expects more relational, dialogical
teaching. Cross-cultural teachers must therefore identify their own
default assumptions before they can recognize where those assumptions
clash with, rather than automatically translate into, a host culture's
expectations.

## Metaphors and Models for Cross-Cultural Teaching

Multiple practitioner case studies illustrate concrete models teachers
have developed for specific cross-cultural teaching challenges: a
"rail fence" model (drawing on a simple, sturdy, locally adaptable
image) offers a framework for building teaching that is flexible
enough to be adapted to different cultural contexts while remaining
structurally sound; other case studies address specific populations
with unique cross-cultural teaching needs, including third-culture
kids (children raised across multiple cultures) who require
intentional support to thrive amid their own complex, blended cultural
identity rather than being taught as though they belonged neatly to
either their passport culture or their host culture alone.

## Turning Mirrors into Windows

A recurring image across the framework describes the goal of good
teaching as turning "mirrors into windows" — moving learners beyond
merely reflecting back their teacher's own culture and assumptions
(a mirror) toward genuinely seeing into other people's experience,
worldview, and context (a window). This requires teachers to
continually examine and adjust their own cultural "colored glasses"
rather than assuming their instinctive teaching style is culturally
neutral, treating cross-cultural competence as an ongoing learning
journey for the teacher, not a credential mastered once and then
applied unreflectively everywhere.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

Missionaries frequently arrive on the field highly qualified in
content — biblical languages, theology, ministry skill — but
under-prepared in the second and third competencies this framework
identifies as equally essential: genuine appreciation of the host
learners' cultural context, and the skill of actively connecting
content to that context rather than assuming it will transfer
automatically. Training programs preparing missionaries for
cross-cultural teaching roles (Bible teaching, leadership training,
theological education) should deliberately build all three
competencies together, since content mastery alone — however
theologically sound — routinely fails to produce transformed learners
without the other two.

## Discussion prompts for training

- Of the three competencies (content mastery, cultural appreciation,
  connecting content to context), which is currently weakest in your
  own cross-cultural teaching preparation?
- Can you identify a specific teaching method you consider "neutral"
  that might actually communicate very differently in your host
  culture than it does in your own?
- How would you help a third-culture kid or a learner straddling
  multiple cultural identities feel like a full participant, rather
  than an outsider, in a teaching setting designed for one culture
  alone?
