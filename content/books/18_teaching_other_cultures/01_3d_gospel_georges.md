# The 3D Gospel: Ministering Across Guilt, Shame, and Fear Cultures (based on Jayson Georges's framework)

## Overview

This framework argues that the gospel is fully three-dimensional, but
most Western gospel presentations flatten it into a single dimension —
guilt and forgiveness — leaving genuine believers from other cultural
backgrounds without a biblical framework for real struggles their own
culture is most attuned to: shame and fear. The core claim is not that
guilt-based gospel presentation is wrong, but that it is incomplete on
its own, and that Scripture itself speaks fully to all three moral
emotions.

## Three Types of Culture

Missiologists commonly distinguish three cultural orientations toward
wrongdoing, each built around a different core moral emotion. Guilt-
innocence cultures, common in the individualistic West, understand
wrongdoing primarily as breaking a law, addressed through justice or
forgiveness that settles a legal account. Shame-honor cultures, common
across much of the collectivist East and Majority World, understand
wrongdoing primarily as failing group expectations, addressed through
restoring honor and standing within the community rather than merely
settling an individual legal debt. Fear-power cultures, common in
animistic and tribal contexts, understand wrongdoing and danger
primarily through vulnerability to spiritual harm, addressed by
securing power or protection against hostile spiritual forces. These
orientations function like a shared cultural personality (sometimes
termed a "groupality"), shaping a whole community's intuitive sense of
right, wrong, danger, and rescue well before any individual
personality difference comes into play.

## Why One Dimension Isn't Enough

A gospel presentation built exclusively around guilt and legal
forgiveness can leave a shame-oriented or fear-oriented believer
genuinely converted yet still privately unaddressed at the level of
their culture's deepest anxieties — a new believer from a shame-honor
culture may fully accept that her sins are forgiven yet still organize
major life decisions around avoiding shame rather than around any
robust theology of honor restored in Christ; a believer from a fear-
power background may trust Christ for eternal salvation yet still seek
out traditional spiritual protection against everyday misfortune,
because no one ever showed her what Scripture says about Christ's power
over spiritual forces. The claim here is not that guilt theology is
mistaken, but that Scripture's own witness is richer than any single
lens captures, and presenting only one dimension leaves real,
Scripturally addressable needs untouched.

## The Three-Dimensional Gospel in Scripture

Careful attention to the biblical text shows all three dimensions
present throughout, often in the same passage or book. Guilt is
addressed through justification — believers are declared not guilty
and legally forgiven through Christ's atoning death. Shame is
addressed through honor — believers are adopted into God's family,
clothed in Christ's righteousness, and given a new, honorable identity
that replaces disgrace with acceptance and belonging. Fear is addressed
through power — Christ's death and resurrection triumph over hostile
spiritual powers, and believers are given authority and protection
against the very spiritual forces a fear-power worldview takes most
seriously. Different atonement models developed across church history
map onto these same three dimensions: penal substitution emphasizes
the guilt dimension, satisfaction/honor-restoration language emphasizes
the shame dimension, and ransom/Christus Victor language emphasizes the
fear-power dimension — suggesting these are not competing theories so
much as complementary facets of one atonement addressing every
dimension of the human predicament.

## Maintaining Balance

The framework cautions against simply replacing a one-dimensional guilt
gospel with a one-dimensional shame or fear gospel tailored to a
specific audience. The goal is a genuinely three-dimensional
presentation — like a diamond with multiple facets rather than a flat
surface — that can be presented with appropriate emphasis for a given
audience's primary cultural orientation, without neglecting the other
two dimensions Scripture equally affirms. A gospel message tailored
entirely to shame-honor categories, for instance, risks leaving a
guilt-sensitive listener within the same audience without clear
teaching on forgiveness of specific sins.

## Application to cross-cultural and diaspora ministry

For missionaries serving Chinese, Central Asian, African, or other
majority-world populations where shame-honor and fear-power dynamics
are often more culturally active than individual guilt, this framework
supplies specific biblical vocabulary (adoption, honor, authority over
spiritual powers) that can be preached and taught with the same
biblical seriousness as forgiveness of guilt — rather than treating
shame and fear as merely psychological or cultural add-ons to a
"real" gospel of guilt and forgiveness alone.

## Discussion prompts for training

- Which of the three dimensions (guilt, shame, fear) is most
  culturally active in your ministry context, and which is most
  absent from how you currently present the gospel there?
- How would you explain Christ's victory over spiritual powers
  (the fear-power dimension) to someone whose worldview already takes
  spiritual danger and protection very seriously?
- Why might presenting only one dimension of the gospel — even a
  biblically accurate one — leave real, addressable needs untouched in
  a new believer's life?
