# Member Care for Chinese Missionaries: A Holistic Framework (based on Wang, Chang & Chang's framework)

## Overview

This framework is distinctive among member-care literature for
addressing the Chinese missionary movement specifically, rather than
applying a generalized Western member-care model. Its central
conviction is that missionaries are humans first — meaning care for
their psychological, relational, physical, and family well-being is not
a distraction from mission but a precondition for sustainable, healthy
mission, tied explicitly to both the Great Commandment and the Great
Commission. The framework is organized as a holistic, developmental
model: care needs differ across a missionary's life stage and season
on the field, from pre-field screening through active service to
reentry.

## Foundations: What Member Care Is, and Why It Matters

Member care is defined broadly as the ongoing support structure —
personal, organizational, spiritual — that sustains a missionary
through the full arc of their service. A major practical driver is
missionary attrition: workers leave the field not primarily because of
doctrinal failure but because of identifiable personal factors
(unaddressed emotional or relational strain, poor cross-cultural
adjustment, family stress) and organizational factors (inadequate
support structures, unclear expectations, absence of pastoral
oversight). Naming these factors explicitly gives sending churches and
agencies a diagnostic checklist rather than treating attrition as an
unpredictable, unavoidable cost of mission work. Effective member care
requires close collaboration between sending churches and mission
agencies — each contributing pastoral care, community, and practical
support in ways neither can fully provide alone — with shared
responsibility rather than assuming "the agency handles that" or
"the church handles that."

## Building a Culture of Proactive, Peer-to-Peer Care

Rather than treating member care as a reactive service only engaged
after a crisis, this framework advocates proactive, peer-to-peer care —
regular "care-and-share" groups among missionaries themselves, alongside
leadership-initiated preventive structures. This requires cultivating a
culture where vulnerability is safe: confidentiality, unconditional
positive regard, and honest acknowledgment of shame or struggle are
treated as prerequisites for a team where people will actually disclose
difficulty before it becomes a crisis, rather than performing spiritual
wellness while privately deteriorating.

## Screening, Spiritual Health, and Cross-Cultural Competence

Before deployment, careful psychological screening and evaluation are
treated as a matter of stewardship, not distrust — protecting both the
missionary and the mission from placing emotionally unprepared workers
in high-stress environments. Ongoing spiritual health is addressed
through sustainable, "unforced" rhythms of spiritual practice, rather
than rigid or externally imposed devotional performance, alongside
deliberate cross-cultural adaptation, active cross-cultural competence
development, and sustained language learning as core, ongoing tasks —
not preliminary hurdles to clear once and move past.

## Sustainable Rhythms: Health, Rest, and Resilience

Healthy lifestyle habits, sustainable rhythms of work and rest, and
resilience are treated as essential missionary infrastructure rather
than personal indulgence. A missionary who treats rest as optional or
who normalizes chronic busyness is, by this framework, undermining
their own long-term sustainability regardless of how theologically
sound their motivations are — resilience is presented as a
"must-have," not a bonus trait some missionaries happen to possess.

## Marriage, Family, and Team Dynamics

Significant attention goes to marriage and family life on the field,
including practical strategies for addressing marital strain under
cross-cultural stress, parenting in a cross-cultural context, and
specific support needs of single missionaries (who face distinct
relational and practical challenges from married colleagues on
multicultural teams). Multicultural team dynamics are addressed
directly, since team conflict is a major, underestimated driver of
attrition — as significant, in this framework's view, as external
persecution or hardship.

## Missionary Kids (MKs)

A substantial section addresses the specific needs of missionary
children — helping MKs thrive amid repeated relocation and identity
formation between cultures, and equipping parents specifically for the
task of raising children as MKs, treating this as a distinct competency
parents need rather than an automatic extension of ordinary parenting.

## Debriefing, Trauma, and Reentry

The framework devotes substantial attention to structured debriefing —
regular wellness check-ins, transition debriefing when moving between
assignments, trauma-specific debriefing after acute critical incidents,
and reentry debriefing when returning to the home culture, which is
treated as its own distinct adjustment challenge rather than simply
"going home." Ongoing support during home assignment (furlough) and
during the reentry period itself is addressed as needing deliberate
structure, and aftercare for those who return early or permanently
("early returnees") is treated as a distinct ministry of care in its
own right, resisting the tendency to treat an early return as simply a
failure requiring no further pastoral attention.

## Application to training and to the mentor feature specifically

This framework maps closely onto structured mentor-care systems: its
emphasis on proactive check-ins, psychological screening, and
distinguishing personal from organizational risk factors for attrition
provides a direct theological and practical rationale for pastoral-care
escalation processes (flagging distress, tracking wellness over time)
built into a mission-training program, rather than treating such
features as merely administrative.

## Discussion prompts for training

- What personal and organizational attrition risk factors named here
  are currently present, unaddressed, on your own team?
- What would it take to build a genuine "care-and-share" culture on
  your team, where vulnerability is actually safe rather than only
  officially encouraged?
- How is your team currently handling reentry and aftercare for anyone
  who has returned home, whether on schedule or early?
