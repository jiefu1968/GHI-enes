# Misreading Scripture with Individualist Eyes: Kinship, Patronage, and Honor-Shame (based on Richards & James's framework)

## Overview

This is a companion framework to Misreading Scripture with Western Eyes,
narrowing in specifically on collectivism versus individualism as the
single most consequential blind spot for Western Bible readers. Its
central claim is that the biblical world operated as a "we" culture —
identity, decisions, and obligations were fundamentally shaped by
group belonging — while modern Western readers instinctively read the
Bible as a "me" culture, assuming an autonomous individual self as the
natural unit of decision, morality, and salvation. Because this
assumption operates beneath conscious awareness, it distorts reading in
ways more pervasive than any single mistranslated word.

## Part One: The Social Structures of the Biblical World

Three structures are described as foundational to how people in the
biblical world related to one another. Kinship treats family
membership as a collective, group-defining reality rather than simply
private relationship — "being" and "staying" in a family carried
obligations and identity far beyond a modern nuclear-family sense of
relatedness, which reframes texts about adoption, inheritance, and even
divorce as fundamentally about group belonging, not just individual
relationship status. Patronage describes an economic and social system
of unequal but reciprocal relationships — a patron providing resources
or protection to a client who owes loyalty and honor in return — with
this framework arguing that patronage vocabulary (gift, grace, favor)
saturates biblical language about God's relationship to his people in
ways a modern reader focused only on individual transaction easily
misses. Brokerage adds a third role: an intermediary who connects a
person to a patron they could not otherwise access — a role this
framework argues clarifies how mediating figures function throughout
Scripture, illuminating why intercession and mediation carry the
specific social weight they do in the biblical world.

## Part Two: The Social Tools of Honor and Shame

Honor and shame are treated as the primary moral currency of the
biblical world, functioning the way individual guilt or conscience
often functions for modern Western readers. Honor could be ascribed
(inherited through birth, family, or group membership) or achieved
(gained through action), and it operated as a genuinely social
possession — evaluated by the community, not simply an individual's
private sense of self-worth. Honor contests — public exchanges in which
status was tested, defended, or won — are identified as a recurring
narrative pattern worth recognizing across both Old and New Testament
stories. Shame, correspondingly, is described as a constructive social
tool rather than merely a painful emotion to avoid — a mechanism that
reinforced community values by marking behavior as unacceptable, with
"shaming done right" distinguished from "shaming done wrong" as a
biblical distinction worth taking seriously rather than assuming shame
is inherently unhealthy. Boundaries — the social markers distinguishing
"us" from "them" — are treated as necessary tools for maintaining group
identity and values, both defining a group's distinctives and requiring
active guarding against erosion.

## Part Three: Redeeming Collectivist Categories Theologically

Rather than treating collectivist categories as merely descriptive
background, the constructive move here is theological: these categories
are redeemed and reworked, not simply reported, by the biblical
narrative itself. Kinship and boundaries are redefined around God as
Father and believers as siblings — a genuinely new family whose
boundaries are drawn not by ethnicity or bloodline but by faith,
illustrated through the prodigal son's framework of being "saved into
we" rather than saved as an isolated individual. Patronage and
brokerage are reframed with God himself as the ultimate patron, and
Jesus functioning as the broker who secures access to that patronage —
while believers are cast as brokers extending that access to others,
never as patrons in their own right, since only God occupies that role.
The closing argument insists that collectivism itself is thoroughly
biblical and should not be treated as a cultural problem to be
overcome on the way to Christian maturity — but that whatever is
retained from patronage, honor, and kinship structures must be
reoriented and redeemed by love, rather than left to operate on its own
unredeemed terms (where patronage becomes manipulative obligation, or
honor becomes status-seeking status pursued for its own sake).

## Application to cross-cultural and diaspora ministry

Because collectivism, kinship obligation, patron-client dynamics, and
honor-shame evaluation remain far more culturally active in much of the
majority world — including many Chinese cultural contexts — than in the
modern West, this framework gives missionaries working in those
contexts a vocabulary that is often closer to their host culture's lived
experience than to their own. Rather than needing to translate
biblical concepts into an unfamiliar collectivist framework, missionaries
from individualist backgrounds may find that their host culture already
intuitively grasps categories (family honor, patron obligation, group
boundaries) that they themselves need to relearn from Scripture in
order to teach accurately.

## Discussion prompts for training

- How does describing salvation as being "saved into we" rather than
  simply "saved as an individual" change how you would explain the
  gospel to someone from a strongly family-oriented culture?
- Where in your ministry context do you see patron-client dynamics
  already at work culturally, and how might that vocabulary help
  explain grace more clearly than an individualist framing would?
- What is the difference, in this framework's view, between shaming
  done right and shaming done wrong — and how would you apply that
  distinction pastorally in your own context?
