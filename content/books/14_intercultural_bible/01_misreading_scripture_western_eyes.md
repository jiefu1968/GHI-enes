# Misreading Scripture with Western Eyes: Cultural Blind Spots in Bible Reading (based on Richards & O'Brien's framework)

## Overview

This framework's central claim is uncomfortable but important: every
reader approaches Scripture wearing invisible cultural glasses, and
Western readers in particular tend to assume their own cultural
assumptions are simply "how things are" rather than one specific
cultural lens among many. Because the biblical authors and their
original audiences lived in cultures far closer, in many respects, to
much of the majority world today than to modern Western culture, this
framework argues that many Western readers systematically misread
Scripture in identifiable, patterned ways — and that readers from
collectivist, honor-shame cultures often have less distance to cross to
understand the original context than Western readers do. For
cross-cultural training, this reframes an assumption often held
backwards: it is not simply that missionaries need to translate the
gospel for "different" cultures; Western missionaries specifically need
to recognize how their own culture has already colored their reading of
the text.

## Part One: Above the Surface — Customs

Three everyday areas of custom are examined as places where cultural
assumptions quietly distort reading: attitudes toward sexuality, money,
and food. In each case, the ancient Mediterranean world operated with
different assumptions than the modern West — communal rather than
purely individual decision-making around marriage and sexuality,
different assumptions about generosity and reciprocity around money,
and different social meanings attached to shared meals and dietary
practice. Race and ethnicity are addressed next: the biblical world had
its own categories of ethnic and social difference (slave and free,
Jew and Gentile, regional dialects and accents) that don't map neatly
onto modern racial categories, and importing modern racial assumptions
onto ancient text risks either flattening real ancient distinctions or
inventing distinctions the text doesn't actually make. Language itself
is treated as a further blind spot: the assumption that one's own
language transparently and sufficiently captures reality, that ideas
translate with full equivalency between languages, and that plain,
unambiguous statement is inherently superior to more indirect or
poetic expression — assumptions that are not universal and that shape,
often invisibly, how a reader expects Scripture to communicate.

## Part Two: Just Below the Surface — Individualism, Honor-Shame, and Time

A second layer of blind spots operates below conscious custom, at the
level of basic social orientation. Individualism versus collectivism is
treated as a primary example: much Western reading assumes the
individual, autonomous self is the natural unit of decision and
identity, whereas the biblical world (and much of the contemporary
majority world) operates with a far more communal, family- and
group-oriented sense of self — which changes how texts about sin,
salvation, and calling are naturally read. Honor and shame are treated
as a moral vocabulary largely foreign to a modern Western reader
primed to think in terms of guilt and innocence, yet pervasive
throughout both Old and New Testaments — meaning readers who don't
recognize honor-shame dynamics will miss or flatten a great deal of
what a text is actually doing. Time is treated similarly: the West's
precise, clock-and-calendar orientation toward time contrasts with a
more event-oriented, relationally-paced sense of time in Scripture,
reflected in the distinction between chronos (measured, sequential
time) and kairos (a fitting or significant moment) — a distinction easy
to miss for a reader who assumes time is always primarily about
measurement.

## Part Three: Deep Below the Surface — Rules, Virtue, and the Self

The deepest layer of blind spots concerns how right and wrong, and the
self, are fundamentally conceived. Western ethical reasoning tends to
be rule-based — right and wrong reduced to abstract, universal
principles — whereas the biblical world often reasons in terms of
relationships and their obligations, meaning a rule-oriented reading can
miss the deeply relational logic underneath many biblical commands.
Similarly, which virtues and vices a culture prioritizes, supplements,
or simply fails to notice varies significantly — a modern reader may
readily recognize sins of personal indulgence while remaining nearly
blind to sins of communal neglect or injustice the text emphasizes just
as strongly. Finally, the assumption that Scripture exists primarily to
serve an individual's personal life plan or self-fulfillment ("God has
a wonderful plan for my life") is examined as a particularly Western
lens that can distort passages more concerned with God's larger
purposes for a people or the world than with any one individual's
personal trajectory.

## A Posture for Reading Well

Rather than claiming any reader can achieve a perfectly neutral,
lens-free reading of Scripture, the constructive proposal is a set of
practical postures: embracing the genuine complexity of cross-cultural
interpretation rather than looking for artificially simple answers,
guarding against overcorrecting into assuming every non-Western reading
is automatically more accurate, remaining teachable rather than
defensive when a cultural blind spot is pointed out, treating the
inevitability of some interpretive error as an occasion for humility
rather than paralysis, and reading Scripture in the company of
believers from different cultural backgrounds specifically because they
will see what any single reader, on their own, cannot.

## Application to cross-cultural and diaspora ministry

This framework is directly reversible and mutually corrective: just as
Western missionaries must learn to recognize their own cultural
blinders when reading Scripture, they should expect that believers from
honor-shame, collectivist, or oral cultures may already grasp certain
dimensions of the text (honor-shame dynamics, communal identity,
event-oriented time) more readily than they do — meaning cross-cultural
Bible study is not simply the missionary correcting a "foreign"
reading, but a genuine two-way exchange in which the missionary has as
much to learn as to teach.

## Discussion prompts for training

- Which of the blind spots described here (individualism, rule-based
  ethics, the "personal life plan" reading of Scripture) do you
  recognize most strongly in your own habits of reading the Bible?
- How might a believer from an honor-shame culture read a passage like
  the prodigal son differently — and potentially more accurately — than
  a guilt-innocence-oriented Western reader?
- What would it look like, practically, to read a specific biblical
  text together with believers from a very different cultural
  background, and to treat their reading as a genuine correction rather
  than simply a different opinion?
