# Global Approaches to Discipleship and Spiritual Formation

## Overview

Most discipleship materials produced for global use were originally
written within a Western, literate, individualist framework — private
devotional reading, structured curricula, one-on-one mentoring
relationships modeled on a classroom or coaching relationship. That
framework works well in the culture that produced it, but it is only
one of many legitimate patterns believers around the world have used to
grow into Christlikeness. Understanding how discipleship actually
happens across different cultural settings — rather than assuming the
Western model is simply "discipleship" and everything else is a
regional variant of it — equips missionaries to form disciples who can
in turn form other disciples using patterns that fit their own culture,
rather than depending indefinitely on an imported model.

## Group-Oriented Versus Individual Discipleship

In strongly collectivist cultures, spiritual formation often happens
primarily through the family or community unit rather than through
one-on-one mentoring relationships focused on an individual's private
growth. A father's conversion in some contexts functions as an
invitation for the whole household to consider following Christ
together, and decisions of faith are frequently made, or at least
significantly shaped, by extended family consensus rather than in
isolated individual reflection. Missionaries trained primarily in
individualist discipleship models can misread this as spiritually
shallow or socially coerced faith, when it may simply reflect a
different, equally legitimate pathway into genuine conviction — one
where the community, not just the individual, is the natural unit
being discipled.

## Oral and Narrative Discipleship

In cultures where oral communication is primary and literacy is
limited or simply not the preferred mode of learning, discipleship
often proceeds through story, proverb, song, and communal recitation
rather than through written curriculum and private reading assignments.
A believer in an oral culture may grow enormously in biblical
understanding through repeated group storytelling of Scripture
narratives, memorized songs carrying doctrinal content, and structured
communal discussion after a story is told — without ever completing a
written workbook. Effective discipleship in these settings measures
growth by a believer's ability to retell, apply, and pass on a story
accurately, not by their completion of written lessons.

## Apprenticeship and Modeled Life

Across many cultures, the most trusted form of learning is not
verbal instruction alone but sustained, close observation of a
respected life — an apprenticeship model where a disciple learns by
living alongside a mature believer over an extended period, absorbing
character and practice as much through proximity as through explicit
teaching. This mirrors, in many ways, how Jesus discipled the Twelve:
not primarily through classroom instruction but through shared life,
travel, meals, and ministry over an extended period. Missionaries
working in apprenticeship-oriented cultures often find that inviting
new believers into shared daily life and ministry produces deeper,
more durable formation than a purely instructional curriculum offered
at a distance.

## Contextualized Spiritual Disciplines

Classic spiritual disciplines — prayer, fasting, Scripture engagement,
solitude, accountability — remain constant across cultures in their
underlying purpose, but the specific form they take can and should be
contextualized. Fasting patterns, prayer postures, and the rhythms of
corporate versus individual spiritual practice vary considerably, and
imposing one culture's specific expression of a discipline as though it
were the discipline itself risks confusing cultural packaging with
spiritual substance. In honor-shame cultures, for instance,
accountability structures often work more effectively when framed
around communal honor and mutual obligation rather than an
individualized, privatized confession model, without changing the
underlying biblical call to mutual confession and correction (James
5:16).

## Honor-Shame Frameworks for Growth

In many majority-world cultures, spiritual growth is experienced and
described less in terms of individual guilt resolved through private
confession, and more in terms of restored honor and right standing
within a community — language of adoption into God's family, the honor
of belonging to Christ, and restored face before God and others often
resonates more immediately than a purely guilt-innocence-framed call to
repentance. Discipleship material developed exclusively within a
guilt-innocence framework can therefore feel emotionally distant or
even confusing in these contexts unless deliberately supplemented with
honor-shame vocabulary equally present throughout Scripture.

## Multiplication Through Reproducible Simplicity

A recurring principle across effective global discipleship movements is
that the simpler and more reproducible a discipleship pattern is, the
further and faster it multiplies. Complex curricula requiring extensive
training, literacy, or imported materials tend to stall at the first or
second generation of disciples, since only the original, specially
trained leader can teach it onward. Patterns built around a few
memorable, easily transferable elements — a short set of questions
asked in every gathering, a simple Scripture engagement method any
believer can lead, a repeatable story-and-discussion format — tend to
multiply far more readily into the third, fourth, and later generations
of disciples a movement actually needs for lasting indigenous growth.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

Missionaries preparing to disciple believers in a new cultural context
benefit from first observing how that culture already transmits values,
skills, and identity across generations — through story, apprenticeship,
family structure, or communal ritual — and then asking how biblical
discipleship can be carried through those same existing channels, rather
than importing an unfamiliar structure wholesale. The goal is not to
abandon sound doctrine or biblical content, but to let the culture's own
proven methods of transmission carry that content, since a discipleship
pattern a culture already trusts and knows how to reproduce will
outlast one that depends indefinitely on foreign materials or an
outside teacher's continued presence.

## Discussion prompts for training

- Which discipleship pattern (group-oriented, oral/narrative,
  apprenticeship-based) most closely matches how your host culture
  already transmits important skills or values across generations?
- How would you adapt a classic spiritual discipline (fasting, prayer,
  confession) to fit an honor-shame or collectivist cultural framework
  without losing its underlying biblical substance?
- What would it take to simplify your current discipleship approach
  enough that a believer only a few weeks old in the faith could
  realistically reproduce it with someone else?
