# Worldview Categories for Intercultural Discipleship

## Overview

Effective discipleship across cultures depends on a missionary's
ability to recognize how a host culture actually organizes belonging,
handles everyday spiritual crisis, and transmits values — not just
what doctrines that culture affirms. This piece draws together several
well-established anthropological and missiological concepts — bounded
and centered sets and the "excluded middle," from Paul Hiebert's
anthropological work (the same author already covered more fully in
Module 11), and the classic structure of rites of passage, from
anthropologists Arnold van Gennep and Victor Turner — and applies them
directly to the practice of discipling believers across cultures.

## Bounded Sets and Centered Sets

Hiebert distinguished two ways a group can define its membership. A
bounded set draws a sharp, static boundary and evaluates people by
whether they have crossed it — a person is either clearly inside or
clearly outside, based on meeting a fixed checklist of entry
requirements, regardless of which direction they are currently moving.
A centered set instead defines membership by relationship to a center
and by the direction a person is moving relative to it — someone far
from Christlike maturity is still genuinely part of the set if they are
oriented toward Christ and moving toward him, while someone who looks
outwardly compliant but is drifting away from that center is not
truly centered on it.

Applied to discipleship, a bounded-set approach tends to measure
spiritual progress by whether specific external markers have been
checked off (attendance, doctrinal assent, particular behavioral
rules), which can produce converts who technically qualify as
"inside" while remaining spiritually stagnant, or which can wrongly
exclude a genuine seeker who hasn't yet mastered the expected external
markers. A centered-set approach instead asks a more dynamic question:
is this person's life oriented toward Christ, and are they continuing
to move toward him, however far they currently are from full
maturity? This reframes discipleship conversations away from
policing a fixed boundary and toward encouraging continued movement
toward the center, treating everyday struggles and setbacks as normal
territory for growth rather than as evidence of exclusion.

## The Excluded Middle

Hiebert also identified a common blind spot in Western, post-
Enlightenment worldviews, which he called the "excluded middle."
Western thought typically operates with two levels: a "high" level
addressing ultimate religious questions (God, eternity, salvation) and
a "low" level addressing empirical, everyday problems (illness,
weather, mechanical failure), solved through science and technology.
Many traditional and majority-world cultures, however, operate with an
additional middle level — a zone of everyday, this-worldly problems
(sudden illness, misfortune, protection for a journey, success in a
venture) that these cultures address through spiritual means: ancestral
mediation, divination, protective rituals, or appeals to spirits and
lesser powers. Western missionaries whose own worldview lacks this
middle level often unintentionally leave it unaddressed in their
teaching and discipleship materials, offering answers only to "the big
questions" and "the technical problems" while remaining silent on the
everyday spiritual concerns a new believer's culture is most
preoccupied with.

When this gap is left unaddressed, two common distortions result.
"Split-level" or "double-level" Christianity occurs when a believer
sincerely holds formal Christian answers to eternal questions while
quietly continuing to consult traditional practitioners or protective
practices for everyday middle-level crises, producing two parallel,
never-integrated belief systems in the same person. Syncretism occurs
when traditional middle-level practices are absorbed into Christian
practice without real theological evaluation, so that, for example, a
Bible or a cross comes to be treated as a magical protective object
rather than as Scripture or a symbol pointing to Christ. The corrective
is not to ignore middle-level concerns as spiritually illegitimate, nor
to accept traditional answers to them uncritically, but to address them
directly and biblically — teaching what Scripture actually says about
God's provision, protection, and sovereignty over exactly the everyday
concerns a culture's traditional middle-level practices were designed
to address.

## Rites of Passage in Discipleship Formation

Separately from Hiebert's worldview categories, anthropologists Arnold
van Gennep and later Victor Turner described a recurring three-part
structure found in rites of passage across cultures: separation (a
deliberate removal from ordinary routine and social role), transition
or liminality (an in-between state where ordinary social hierarchy is
suspended, often producing an intense sense of equality and mutual
bonding among participants that Turner termed communitas), and
reincorporation (return to society in a new, socially recognized
status with new responsibilities). This structure explains why
deliberately structured, set-apart experiences — a baptism, a retreat,
a short-term mission trip, a defined discipleship cohort with a clear
beginning and ending — often produce disproportionately deep and
lasting formation compared to ordinary, uninterrupted daily instruction:
the deliberate separation and shared liminal experience create a bond
and openness to change that routine teaching alone rarely produces.
Missionaries can use this structure intentionally when designing
discipleship milestones (baptism, commissioning, ordination) rather
than treating such events as merely ceremonial add-ons to the "real"
content-based discipleship happening elsewhere.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

Together, these concepts equip missionaries to diagnose discipleship
problems more precisely: a convert who seems stuck may be caught in a
bounded-set mentality that treats a fixed checklist as the finish line
rather than continuing to move toward Christ; a believer who quietly
maintains traditional practices alongside Christian profession may be
living with an unaddressed excluded middle rather than being
insincere; and a discipleship program lacking any structured,
set-apart milestone experiences may be missing an opportunity for the
kind of deep bonding and transformation rites of passage reliably
produce elsewhere in that same culture.

## Discussion prompts for training

- Where in your own discipleship practice do you see a bounded-set
  mentality (fixed checklist of requirements) operating instead of a
  centered-set orientation (direction of movement toward Christ)?
- What "middle-level" everyday concerns (illness, misfortune,
  protection, success) does your host culture typically address
  through non-Christian means, and how would you address these
  directly and biblically rather than leaving them unaddressed?
- What structured, set-apart milestone experience (retreat, baptism
  preparation, defined cohort) could you build into your discipleship
  process to intentionally create the kind of bonding and
  transformation rites of passage produce?
