# Christian Theology: A Moderate Evangelical Systematic Framework (based on Millard Erickson's four-volume work)

## Overview

This framework presents a comprehensive systematic theology
distinguished by its careful attention to theological method itself —
examining how theology relates to philosophy, biblical criticism, and
changing cultural context — before working through the full range of
traditional doctrinal loci from a moderate, broadly evangelical
Baptist perspective. Compared to more confessionally specific
systematic theologies, this framework spends unusually extensive
attention on methodological questions: what theology actually is, how
it should be done, and how enduring biblical truth relates to
historically and culturally conditioned theological formulation.

## Theological Method and the Nature of Theology

Significant early attention addresses foundational questions many
systematic theologies simply assume: theology's relationship to
philosophy across different historical approaches, how theology should
engage critical biblical scholarship (including form and redaction
criticism) with appropriate discernment rather than either wholesale
adoption or wholesale rejection, and the perennial challenge of
"contemporizing" Christian doctrine — communicating unchanging biblical
truth in ways that remain intelligible to each new generation without
compromising its actual content. This includes direct engagement with
philosophical challenges to religious language itself (verificational
and functional analysis accusing theological statements of being
literally meaningless), answered by showing how theological language
functions coherently and meaningfully within its own proper context.

## Knowing God: Revelation and Scripture

God's self-disclosure is treated in two movements: general revelation
(available to all humanity through creation and conscience, alongside
careful attention to its real but limited efficacy and how it grounds
human moral responsibility even where special revelation has not yet
reached) and special revelation (Scripture specifically). The doctrine
of inspiration receives careful, nuanced treatment — examining the
extent and intensiveness of inspiration, proposing a specific model for
how divine and human authorship cooperate in producing Scripture, and
addressing inerrancy with careful attention to what inerrancy does and
does not claim, how it relates to specific difficult textual
phenomena, and its practical significance for the Bible's ongoing
authority. Scripture's authority is further examined alongside reason
and the Holy Spirit's internal witness, addressing how these three
sources of religious authority properly relate to one another.

## What God Is Like: Attributes and Trinity

God's attributes are organized around his greatness (attributes
describing his transcendent nature and power) and his goodness (moral
attributes describing his character), with careful attention to
apparent tension between God's love and justice and how these are
properly held together rather than allowed to undercut one another.
God's relationship to creation is examined through the classic
categories of immanence (God's nearness and active presence within
creation) and transcendence (God's otherness and independence from
creation), followed by careful historical and biblical treatment of
the Trinity — surveying historical trinitarian formulations before
identifying the essential elements any adequate doctrine of the
Trinity must include.

## Distinctive Methodological Contribution

What most distinguishes this framework from other systematic
theologies is its sustained insistence that doing theology well
requires ongoing methodological self-awareness — recognizing that even
careful, evangelical theology is done by finite, historically situated
interpreters, and that acknowledging this does not require abandoning
confidence in Scripture's own unchanging authority and truth. This
balance — full confidence in biblical authority alongside honest
methodological humility about the interpreter's own limitations —
runs throughout the work's treatment of every subsequent doctrine.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

This framework's extensive attention to theological method offers
missionaries a valuable model for their own cross-cultural theological
reflection: recognizing that their own theological formulations,
however carefully reasoned, remain historically and culturally
situated interpretations of unchanging biblical truth, which should
produce humility and openness to learning from believers in other
cultural contexts without abandoning confidence in Scripture's own
authority. Its careful treatment of general revelation's real but
limited efficacy also directly informs how missionaries think about
the spiritual and moral condition of people who have not yet
encountered the gospel.

## Discussion prompts for training

- How does distinguishing "general revelation" from "special
  revelation" help you think about the moral and spiritual condition of
  people in your ministry context who have not yet heard the gospel?
- Where have you noticed your own theological formulations reflecting
  your particular cultural or historical situation, and how does
  recognizing this change how you engage believers from different
  theological traditions?
- How would you explain the doctrine of inerrancy in a way that
  honestly addresses specific difficult textual questions rather than
  avoiding them?
