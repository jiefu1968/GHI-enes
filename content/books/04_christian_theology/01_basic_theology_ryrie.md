# Basic Theology: A Systematic Overview (based on Charles Ryrie's framework)

## Overview

This framework presents a comprehensive, popularly accessible
systematic theology moving through all the traditional loci
(theological topics) in sequence — the doctrine of Scripture, God,
angels and Satan, humanity and sin, Christ, salvation, the Holy Spirit,
the church, and last things — from a dispensational, premillennial
evangelical perspective. Its stated aim is to make careful, doctrinally
precise theology accessible to ordinary readers rather than only
academic specialists, while still engaging seriously with the
historical development and internal debates within each doctrine.

## Prolegomena and the Doctrine of God

Theological method begins by examining foundational presuppositions and
the question of theological authority before turning to the doctrine
of God himself — his knowability, the ways he has revealed himself, his
perfections (attributes), his names (each disclosing something of his
character), and his triune nature. Scripture's own God-breathed
character receives extended treatment: the process and meaning of
inspiration, historic departures from a robust doctrine of inspiration,
the case for inerrancy (including how inerrancy relates to Christ's own
use of the Old Testament and how to handle specific difficult or
"problem" passages), the formation of the biblical canon, and sound
principles of biblical interpretation.

## Angels, Satan, and Demons

A substantial section addresses the spiritual realm often
underemphasized in more academically oriented systematic theologies:
the existence, creation, nature, and organized ministry of angels; the
reality, fall, and ongoing activity of Satan; and the nature and
activity of demons. Treating this material with the same systematic
seriousness given to other doctrines reflects a conviction that the
biblical text's own extensive attention to the spiritual realm warrants
equally serious doctrinal treatment, not merely devotional or
anecdotal mention.

## Humanity, Sin, and the Person of Christ

Human origins are addressed directly alongside evolutionary theory,
followed by a careful anthropology examining the specific facets of
human nature and the biblical account of the Fall. Sin is treated
comprehensively — its essential biblical concept, Christ's own
teaching about it, how it is inherited and imputed across humanity, the
distinct category of personal sins, and how believers relate to
ongoing sin after conversion. Christology proceeds from Christ's
pre-incarnate existence through his incarnation, his integrated divine-
human person, his threefold office (prophet, priest, king), the
theological puzzle of his self-emptying (kenosis), his sinlessness, and
his resurrection, ascension, and ongoing post-ascension ministry.

## Salvation and the Holy Spirit

Salvation is treated with attention to precise biblical terminology,
the meaning and significance of Christ's death, competing historical
theories of the atonement, the doctrine of election, debates over the
atonement's extent, and the security believers have once savingly
united to Christ — concluding with careful attention to how the gospel
itself should be defined and communicated. The Holy Spirit's person and
work receive extended treatment across both testaments and church
history, including his distinct ministries of indwelling, sealing,
baptizing, gifting, and filling believers.

## The Church and Eschatology

Ecclesiology addresses the church's nature and distinctiveness, church
government models, biblically qualified leadership, the ordinances,
and corporate worship. The framework's eschatology section is
particularly extensive and reflects a clearly premillennial,
dispensational perspective — surveying postmillennial and
amillennial views before presenting premillennialism in detail,
alongside God's covenants with Abraham and David, a detailed proposed
outline of future events, the tribulation period, and a thorough
comparison of pretribulational, midtribulational, and posttribulational
views of the rapture, concluding with the millennium, future
judgments, and final resurrection and eternal destiny.

## Application to cross-cultural and missionary training

This framework's willingness to state a clear, specific theological
position (particularly its dispensational, premillennial eschatology)
rather than remaining deliberately neutral offers missionaries a
useful contrast case for recognizing how much historic evangelical
theology genuinely varies on secondary doctrines, equipping them to
teach with clarity on primary gospel doctrines while handling secondary
doctrinal differences (like eschatological timing views) with
appropriate charity toward believers from other theological traditions.

## Discussion prompts for training

- How would you explain the difference between primary doctrines
  (Christ's deity, the necessity of the atonement) and secondary
  doctrines (specific millennial or rapture views) to a new believer
  encountering doctrinal disagreement for the first time?
- Where do you see this framework's serious systematic treatment of
  angels, Satan, and demons as either a helpful corrective or a
  potential overemphasis compared to your own theological training?
- How would you present the doctrine of the church's ordinances and
  government to believers from a culture with very different
  assumptions about religious authority and hierarchy?
